    AI-First Stories¶

    This book uses stories to show that moving to an AI-first school has real benefits and real risks. Our goal is to strengthen the positives and reduce the negatives. This appendix collects the real cases the chapters draw on. Each story is labeled as positive, cautionary, or both-sided, and each ends with the lesson it teaches.

    Check the facts before citing

    Many of these stories are still unfolding, and the figures come from news coverage, preprints, and the schools' own claims. Before a chapter quotes a number, check it against the original source and note the date.

    Stories That Worked¶

    Nigeria AI Tutoring Pilot¶

    Positive · 2024 · World Bank

    In a six-week after-school program in Edo State, Nigeria, students used a GPT-4-based tutor to practice English while teachers were in the room. The World Bank team reported large learning gains for a very small cost per student.

    Lesson: Low-cost AI tutoring, with a teacher present, can help students in places with few resources. This supports the book's claim that AI-first learning does not have to be a luxury.

    Harvard Physics AI Tutor¶

    Positive · 2024–2025 · Kestin et al.

    In a randomized study in an introductory Harvard physics course, students who used a carefully designed AI tutor learned more, in less time, than students in an active-learning class led by an instructor. The tutor was built on proven teaching practices instead of being an open chatbot.

    Lesson: A well-designed AI tutor can make the shorter academic morning work. Design matters more than raw AI power.

    Tutor CoPilot¶

    Positive · 2024 · Stanford University

    Tutor CoPilot gave human tutors live AI suggestions during their sessions instead of replacing them. Students' mastery improved, the weakest tutors improved the most, and the cost per tutor for a year was small.

    Lesson: AI works well when it makes people better at helping students. This supports the book's picture of the teacher as a coach.

    Estonia's AI Leap¶

    Positive · 2025 · Estonia (TI-Hüpe)

    Estonia launched a national program giving secondary students and teachers access to AI learning tools, along with teacher training. It builds on the country's earlier success bringing computers into schools.

    Lesson: A school system can adopt AI on purpose, with a plan, training, and public support. This is a useful example for the change-management chapters.

    Stories That Went Wrong¶

    Unbound Academy¶

    Cautionary · 2025– · Arizona

    Unbound Academy is a free, online charter school for grades 4–8 that uses Alpha School's 2 Hour Learning software. The Arizona State Board for Charter Schools approved it by a 4–3 vote. A board slideshow reportedly showed 10% proficiency in math and 28% in English Language Arts, below state averages. The school had projected 90%+ within two to three years, and separately claims students learn 2.8 times faster on its own growth tests. The reports do not say where students started, so it is unclear how much they grew.

    Lesson: This is the key story to pair with Alpha. The morning software alone is not the whole model. When the program moves online and opens to everyone without in-person afternoons and adult guides, the results do not carry over automatically.

    The LA Unified Chatbot "Ed"¶

    Cautionary · 2024 · Los Angeles Unified School District

    LA Unified committed about $6 million to the startup AllHere for an AI chatbot called "Ed" and spent about $3 million of it. The company collapsed within months of launch. In November 2024 its founder was charged with defrauding investors of nearly $10 million. In 2026 the FBI searched the superintendent's home and office in an investigation reported to be connected to AllHere; he has not been charged with a crime.

    Lesson: Rushed purchasing, depending on one vendor, and sending student data to an unproven company all put students at risk. Schools need procurement rules, such as those based on the five-level model, before they buy AI products.

    AltSchool¶

    Cautionary · 2013–2019 · Silicon Valley

    AltSchool raised more than $170 million to build a network of "personalized learning" microschools run on its own software. It closed its schools within a few years and shifted to selling software.

    Lesson: Personalization led by technology, without a lasting school culture and community, does not last.

    Summit Learning¶

    Cautionary · 2018–2019 · Brooklyn and Kansas

    Summit Learning, a personalized-learning platform backed by Facebook engineers, was rolled out to many schools. Students walked out in Brooklyn and families protested in Kansas, complaining about long hours on screens and too little contact with teachers.

    Lesson: This is the clearest real example of the screen-time risk. An AI morning must stay short, and the afternoon must be truly with other people.

    inBloom¶

    Cautionary · 2014

    inBloom was a $100 million, Gates Foundation-funded project to store and share student data across states. It closed after parents pushed back over privacy.

    Lesson: Any xAPI and Learning Record Store system needs the trust of families from the start, with clear rules about who owns and controls student data.

    PowerSchool Data Breach¶

    Cautionary · December 2024

    A breach at PowerSchool, one of the largest student information system vendors, exposed records for tens of millions of students and teachers.

    Lesson: Level 3 textbooks and higher need student records. The more centralized those records are, the bigger the target. This makes the level 3 privacy threshold concrete.

    LA Unified iPad Rollout¶

    Cautionary · 2013 · Los Angeles Unified School District

    LA Unified began a $1.3 billion plan to give every student an iPad loaded with Pearson curriculum. The plan was cancelled amid technical problems, weak curriculum, and a scandal over how the contract was awarded.

    Lesson: Buying devices before having a plan for teaching wastes money and public trust.

    Stories With Both Sides¶

    Alpha School¶

    Both-sided · 2014– · Austin and other cities

    Alpha School students spend about two hours a day on adaptive academic software and the rest of the day on life-skills workshops and projects led by adult guides. Tuition ranges from about $40,000 a year at the original Austin campus to about $65,000 a year at newer campuses in Boston, Miami, and New York.

    Lesson: The morning-and-afternoon structure can work, but at private-school prices. The book asks how to offer the same structure to every student using open intelligent textbooks, MicroSims, learning graphs, xAPI, and a Learning Record Store instead of costly live AI.

    David Game College¶

    Both-sided · 2024 · London

    David Game College opened what was described as the UK's first GCSE class taught mainly by AI. Students work with AI learning platforms and are supported by learning coaches instead of traditional teachers.

    Lesson: It shows the AI-first idea spreading beyond the United States. It also shows the same open question as Alpha: how much of the result comes from the software and how much from the adults around it.

    The Turkish High-School Study¶

    Both-sided · 2024 · Bastani et al., Wharton

    In a study at a high school in Turkey, students given open GPT-4 access did much better on practice problems. On later exams taken without AI, however, they did about 17% worse than students who never had access. A second version with guardrails, which gave hints instead of answers, removed most of the harm.

    Lesson: This is the book's thesis in one experiment. The same AI can help or hurt depending on how it is designed. It also supports the book's low-cost design: guided, structured adaptation is safer than an open chatbot.

    "Your Brain on ChatGPT"¶

    Both-sided · 2025 · MIT Media Lab

    In this study, students who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed lower brain engagement in EEG readings and remembered less of what they had written than students who wrote on their own. The study was small and is still a preprint.

    Lesson: It supports the risk of students handing their thinking over to AI, and shows why the book puts writing, debate, and making things into the afternoon.

    Khanmigo¶

    Both-sided · 2023– · Khan Academy

    Khanmigo is an AI tutor built specifically for learning. It guides students with questions instead of giving answers, and it has been rolled out in many school districts. Early versions made math mistakes, and students in some districts used it less than expected.

    Lesson: Even careful design meets the reality of how classrooms actually use a tool. A level 4 chatbot is not automatically better than a well-built level 2 or level 3 textbook.

    AI Cheating Detectors¶

    Cautionary · 2023 · Liang et al., Stanford

    A Stanford study found that popular AI-writing detectors often wrongly flagged essays by non-native English writers as written by AI.

    Lesson: Fighting AI can create its own equity problems. Schools should redesign assignments instead of relying on detection.

    The Non-AI Afternoon¶

    Positive · ongoing

    The pro-social afternoon does not need to be invented from scratch. These programs already show it working:

    • High Tech High (San Diego): project-based learning, where students work in teams on real projects and present them in public exhibitions
    • Finland's phenomenon-based learning: students study real-world topics across subjects, often in groups
    • El Sistema (Venezuela and worldwide): youth orchestras that use group music-making to build discipline, belonging, and community

    Lesson: The afternoon half of the AI-native school day is already proven. AI frees the time; these models show how to use it.
