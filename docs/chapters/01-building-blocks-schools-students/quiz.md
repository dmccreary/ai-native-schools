Test your understanding of the core vocabulary of schools, students, teachers, and learning science with these review questions.

#### 1. How does this chapter distinguish education from learning?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Education happens in school, while learning happens only at home
2. Education is an organized, intentional effort; learning is a lasting change inside the learner
3. Education measures what students know; learning is the process of teaching them
4. Education and learning are synonyms that differ only in how formal the setting is
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter defines education as an organized, intentional effort to help people acquire knowledge and skills, and learning as a relatively lasting change in knowledge or skill that happens inside the learner. Each can occur without the other: a forgotten lecture is education with little learning, and a child touching a hot stove learns with no education. Education is not limited to school, so the first option is wrong, and the chapter says outright the two words are not synonyms.

    **Concept Tested:** Education and Learning

#### 2. In the three versions of a curriculum, which one describes what students actually learn and can show?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The intended curriculum
2. The enacted curriculum
3. The adopted curriculum
4. The attained curriculum
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The attained curriculum is what students actually learn and can demonstrate. The intended curriculum is what the documents say students should learn, and the enacted curriculum is what teachers actually teach. Every gap between the three is a place where learning leaks out, as in the unit-rates example where a standard looked fully covered on paper but was only partly learned. "Adopted curriculum" is not one of the three versions.

    **Concept Tested:** Curriculum

    **See:** [Curriculum](index.md#curriculum)

#### 3. Which of the following is the strongest learning outcome by the chapter's standards?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Students will compare two primary sources and identify three points of disagreement
2. Students will understand the main causes and consequences of the Civil War
3. Students will appreciate how poets use figurative language in their work
4. Students will learn about the major ecosystems of North America
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A strong learning outcome names an observable action, specific content, and a way to tell whether the learner succeeded. "Compare two primary sources and identify three points of disagreement" passes the watch-them-do-it test. The other three use *understand*, *appreciate*, and *learn about*, verbs that cannot be observed directly, so a teacher or a piece of software could not check them.

    **Concept Tested:** Learning Outcome

#### 4. Which statement about higher-order thinking matches the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. It covers the Remember, Understand, and Apply levels of Bloom's Taxonomy
2. It is reserved for advanced students who have finished the lower levels for good
3. It depends on a large store of remembered and understood knowledge
4. It is the kind of thinking most classroom assignments already exercise
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Higher-order thinking refers to analyzing, evaluating, and creating, and the chapter stresses that it depends on a strong base of lower-level knowledge: you cannot critique a historical argument without knowing the history. The first option names the lower-order levels. The chapter warns against reading the taxonomy as a ladder climbed once, and it notes that most assignments sit at the Remember and Apply levels, not the higher ones.

#### 5. What is a learning gap, as the chapter defines it?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The difference between what a student is expected to know and what the student actually knows
2. The time that passes between teaching a lesson and testing it on a summative exam
3. The difference between a student's reading level and the student's mathematics level
4. The number of school days a student misses during the year because of illness or absence
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A learning gap is the difference between what a student is expected to know or do, as defined by learning outcomes, and what the student actually knows or can do. The chapter shows that gaps compound, because each year's lessons assume last year's outcomes were attained. A mismatch between reading and math levels, like Maya's, can reveal a gap, but the gap is measured against expected outcomes, not between two subjects.

    **Concept Tested:** Learning Gap

    **See:** [The Learning Gap](index.md#the-learning-gap)

#### 6. A teacher gives a five-question quiz mid-unit, records the scores in the gradebook, and starts the next topic the following day regardless of the results. Is this formative assessment by the chapter's definition?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Yes, because the quiz was low stakes and happened during the unit
2. No, because the results did not change what happened next in teaching or learning
3. Yes, because any quiz given before the final exam counts as formative
4. No, because formative assessment must use mini whiteboards or hinge questions
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The key phrase in the definition is that evidence is used to adjust teaching and learning while there is still time to act. A quiz is not formative because of its format or timing; it is formative only if the results change what happens next. Here the scores were recorded and ignored. Timing and low stakes alone are not enough, and whiteboards and hinge questions are examples of formative techniques, not requirements.

    **Concept Tested:** Formative Assessment

    **See:** [Formative Assessment: Checking Learning While It Happens](index.md#formative-assessment-checking-learning-while-it-happens)

#### 7. What is the difference between self-paced learning and student agency?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Self-paced learning lets students choose topics; student agency lets them choose speed
2. Self-paced learning requires computers; student agency requires a project-based school
3. Self-paced learning and student agency are two names for the same arrangement
4. Self-pacing concerns speed through a sequence; agency concerns choosing what and how
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter separates the two: self-pacing gives a student control over how fast they move through a fixed sequence, while agency gives a student meaningful choices about what to study, how to study it, and how to show learning. A self-paced program can offer almost no agency, and a project can offer great agency with a fixed deadline. The first option reverses the two ideas, and Keller's 1968 system shows self-pacing does not require computers.

    **Concept Tested:** Self-Paced Learning and Student Agency

    **See:** [Student Agency](index.md#student-agency)

#### 8. A student has a test in 30 days and time for three short review sessions. Which plan does the research in this chapter predict will produce the strongest retention on test day?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Rereading her notes three times back-to-back on the first day
2. Rereading her notes on days 1, 3, and 7
3. Closing her notes and writing down what she remembers on days 1, 4, and 12
4. Highlighting the key terms in her notes on days 28, 29, and 30
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. This plan combines spaced practice (reviews spread over time) with retrieval practice (recalling from memory rather than rereading), and the chapter's worked example predicts it gives the strongest retention of the plans compared. Spaced rereading beats cramming but is weaker than spaced retrieval. Highlighting was rated a low-utility strategy in the Dunlosky review, and the last option clusters reviews into a cram just before the test.

    **Concept Tested:** Spaced Practice and Retrieval Practice

    **See:** [Worked Example: Planning Three Reviews](index.md#worked-example-planning-three-reviews)

#### 9. A district's annual report includes salaries, supplies, transportation, and the cost of building a new middle school. To calculate per-pupil spending the standard way, what should be left out?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The construction cost of the new building
2. Teacher salaries and benefits
3. Transportation costs for school buses
4. The cost of instructional supplies
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Most published per-pupil figures use current spending, which covers day-to-day operations such as salaries, benefits, supplies, and transportation, and excludes construction and debt payments. In the chapter's example, including a new school's construction raised the figure from \$15,000 to about \$18,333 per student, a misleading number for comparing everyday costs. Salaries, transportation, and supplies are all part of current spending and stay in the calculation.

    **Concept Tested:** Per-Pupil Spending

    **See:** [Worked Example: Computing Per-Pupil Spending](index.md#worked-example-computing-per-pupil-spending)

#### 10. What lesson does the chapter draw from a century of educational technology waves, from Pressey's testing machine to one-to-one laptops?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Technology has consistently failed to improve learning and should be avoided
2. Technology bolted onto an unchanged school day tends to be absorbed into old habits
3. Each wave succeeded, but schools could not afford to keep the equipment running
4. Technology works best when it replaces the teacher's role in the classroom
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter says the lesson is not that technology fails, but that technology alone rarely changes the grammar of schooling, such as age-graded classes, the bell schedule, and the single pace. Larry Cuban documented a pattern of tools being oversold and underused. The tablet example shows the same device can either digitize the old model or change how learning works. The first option overstates the point, and the chapter argues AI should change what teachers do, not replace them.

    **Concept Tested:** Educational Technology

    **See:** [Educational Technology](index.md#educational-technology)
