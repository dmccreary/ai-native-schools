## Before We Change Anything, Let's Name It

This book argues that the school day is about to change more in the next few years than it has in the last hundred. Before we can talk sensibly about that change, we need a shared vocabulary for the thing being changed. Teachers, principals, board members, and parents all use words like *curriculum*, *learning*, and *assessment*, but they often mean slightly different things by them. Those small differences turn into large arguments once money, schedules, and children's futures are on the table.

So this chapter does something deliberately modest. It describes how schools work *today*, using terms that education researchers and practitioners would recognize, and it does so without any artificial intelligence in the picture. ## Education and Learning: Two Words That Are Not Synonyms

The broadest concept in this book is **education**: the organized, intentional effort by a society, community, or family to help people acquire knowledge, skills, values, and ways of thinking. The key words are *organized* and *intentional*. A grandmother deliberately teaching a grandchild to bake bread is education. So is a university lecture, an apprenticeship, a driver's education course, and a public school. Education is something people *do for* or *with* a learner, and it always has a purpose behind it.

**Learning** is different. Learning is a relatively lasting change in a person's knowledge, skills, or understanding that results from experience. Learning happens inside the learner. It can happen with no education involved at all: a child who touches a hot stove learns something durable without anyone intending to teach. And education can happen with little learning: a lecture delivered to a room of students who forget it by Friday was education in intent and not much learning in result.

The phrase "relatively lasting" matters more than it first appears. Researchers distinguish **performance**, what a learner can do right now, from learning, what the learner can still do days or weeks later. A student who crams the night before a test may perform well in the morning and retain little by the next month. That student showed strong performance and weak learning. This gap between how learning *feels* in the moment and what actually lasts will come back later in this chapter, when we reach spaced and retrieval practice, and it shapes nearly every argument in the book about what an AI tutor should and should not do.

!!! mascot-thinking "Learning happens in the learner"
    ![Indigo thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Notice where each word lives. Education lives in the plans, people, and places a society builds; learning lives only inside the student's head. Every reform in this book is a bet that changing the first will change the second — so always ask what evidence shows the second actually moved.

A third word, *schooling*, is often blurred with both. Schooling is education delivered through the specific institution of a school. Most of this book is about schooling, because that is the institution AI is about to reshape. The table below summarizes the three terms you have just met; it adds no new ideas, but it is a useful reference when later chapters use the words carefully.

| Term | What it is | Where it lives | Can it happen without the others? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Education | Organized, intentional effort to help people learn | Families, communities, institutions | Yes — a lecture no one remembers is education with little learning |
| Learning | A relatively lasting change in knowledge or skill from experience | Inside the learner | Yes — a child learns from touching a hot stove with no teacher present |
| Schooling | Education delivered through the institution of a school | Schools | No — schooling is one form of education |

### Worked Example: Is This Education, Learning, or Both?

Consider four short scenes from Maya's week and decide which concept each one illustrates.

1. **Monday, 9:10 a.m.** Ms. Rivera explains how to add fractions with unlike denominators. Maya is texting under her desk and absorbs none of it. *This is education (intentional, organized teaching) with almost no learning.*
2. **Monday, 6:00 p.m.** Maya watches an older cousin fix a bicycle chain, and the next weekend fixes her own. *This is learning with no formal education; the cousin was not trying to teach.*
3. **Wednesday.** Maya's grandfather spends an evening deliberately teaching her to read a bus map, and she navigates to the library alone the following Saturday. *This is both: intentional education outside school, and lasting learning.*
4. **Friday.** Maya scores 95 percent on a vocabulary quiz she crammed for that morning; three weeks later she recalls four of the twenty words. *This is strong performance with weak learning, which is exactly the distinction researchers warn about.*

The fourth scene is the one that matters most for the rest of this book. A school that measures only Friday's quiz will believe it is succeeding even when little lasting learning occurred.

## Schools, Students, Teachers, and Classrooms

With education and learning defined, we can name the institution and the people where most formal education for children happens.

### School

A **school** is an institution, usually with dedicated buildings and staff, that a society sets up to provide education to a defined group of learners on a regular schedule. In the United States, public schools serve roughly 49 million students in about 99,000 schools, according to the National Center for Education Statistics, with private schools and homeschooling serving millions more. A school is more than a building. It is a bundle of decisions about who attends, what they study, who teaches them, when they show up, how they are grouped, and how their progress is judged.

That bundle has been remarkably stable. Historians David Tyack and Larry Cuban called the durable structure underneath it the "grammar of schooling": age-graded classes, one teacher per room, subjects divided into periods, and a bell schedule. Chapter 3 traces where that grammar came from. For now, notice that most of the features we think of as "what a school is" were design choices made more than a century ago, and design choices can be revisited.

A school also performs several jobs at once, and confusing them causes trouble in any reform conversation. Schools teach academic content. They also provide safe supervision for children while parents work, meals for students who need them, a place to make friends, access to counseling and special-education services, sports, the arts, and a sense of community. A reform that improves academic learning but breaks the supervision or community job will meet fierce resistance, and it should.

### Worked Example: Listing a School's Jobs

Suppose a parent asks the principal of Maya's school, "What is this school actually *for*?" A complete answer lists at least six distinct jobs:

1. **Academic instruction** in reading, writing, mathematics, science, history, and other subjects.
2. **Supervision**: a safe, adult-supervised place for children roughly from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m.
3. **Nutrition**: breakfast and lunch, often free or reduced-price for students from low-income families.
4. **Support services**: special education, counseling, health screening, English-language support.
5. **Social development**: friendships, teamwork, conflict resolution, belonging.
6. **Enrichment**: music, art, athletics, clubs.

When a later chapter proposes moving academic instruction into a shorter morning, this list is a checklist. Every job still has to be done by someone, somewhere, during the day.

### Student

A **student** is a person enrolled in a course of study at an educational institution. That definition is administrative; the more useful view for this book is that each student arrives as a different learner. Students differ in prior knowledge, reading level, interests, motivation, language background, disabilities, home circumstances, sleep, and confidence. A seventh grader like Maya might read at a ninth-grade level, do math at a fifth-grade level, love drawing, and be quietly anxious about speaking in front of the class.

Traditional schools group students mainly by *age*, not by readiness. Two students born a week apart sit in the same grade regardless of what each already knows. This is efficient for scheduling and fair in a rough sense, but it means every classroom contains students at very different starting points. That fact drives the concept of the learning gap later in this chapter.

### Worked Example: One Student, Several Starting Points

Here is a profile of Maya, drawn as a composite, to make "each student is different" concrete:

- **Reading:** finishes young-adult novels quickly; reads about two grade levels above her grade.
- **Mathematics:** never fully mastered fractions in fifth grade, so ratio problems in seventh grade stall her.
- **Interests:** drawing, graphic novels, animals.
- **Constraints:** cares for a younger brother after school, so homework time is short.
- **Confidence:** high in English class, low in math class, and she hides confusion rather than ask.

Notice that "seventh grader" describes almost nothing useful about what Maya needs next. Her next step in reading and her next step in math are years apart.

### Teacher

A **teacher** is a professional whose job is to help students learn: planning instruction, explaining ideas, designing practice, assessing progress, giving feedback, managing a group of young people, and building relationships with them and their families. In most countries, public school teachers hold a license that requires a degree, supervised practice, and examinations.

The job is far larger than the time spent talking at the front of a room. Teachers make hundreds of small decisions every hour: who to call on, when to slow down, whether a student's silence means confusion or boredom, how to calm a conflict. Researchers who study classroom teaching consistently find that the teacher is the most important school-based factor in how much students learn. That is why this book will argue that AI should change *what teachers spend their time on*, not whether schools need them.

### Worked Example: The Arithmetic of a Teacher's Week

Ms. Rivera teaches five sections of seventh-grade math, each with 28 students. That is \( 5 \times 28 = 140 \) students. Suppose she wants to give each student five minutes of written feedback on one homework assignment per week:

\[
140 \text{ students} \times 5 \text{ minutes} = 700 \text{ minutes} \approx 11.7 \text{ hours}
\]

That is nearly twelve hours a week for one round of feedback on one assignment, on top of roughly 25 hours of teaching and the time needed to plan lessons. The arithmetic explains why so much student work gets a checkmark rather than a comment. It also explains why any tool that takes routine feedback off a teacher's desk is attractive, and why a tool that gives *bad* feedback is dangerous: it scales errors across 140 students at once.

### Classroom

A **classroom** is the room, and the group of students and teacher within it, where scheduled instruction happens. It is both a physical space and a social one. The physical classroom has seats, a board or screen, and supplies. The social classroom has norms (raise your hand, do your own work), routines (warm-up, lesson, practice, exit ticket), and a culture that can make students feel safe to try or afraid to be wrong.

The classroom's central constraint is simple: one adult, many students, fixed time. That constraint shapes almost every teaching method.

### Worked Example: How Much One-on-One Time Is There?

Maya's math period lasts 50 minutes and has 28 students. If Ms. Rivera spent the *entire* period moving from student to student, with no whole-class teaching, no transitions, and no interruptions, each student would get:

\[
\frac{50 \text{ minutes}}{28 \text{ students}} \approx 1.8 \text{ minutes per student}
\]

In practice, after taking attendance, opening the lesson, and managing transitions, a student might get less than a minute of individual attention per period. This single number is a large part of why one-to-one tutoring is so effective and so expensive, a theme Chapter 11 explores through Bloom's two-sigma problem.

## Curriculum and Learning Outcomes: What Schools Intend to Teach

Schools do not leave the content of education to chance. They describe it.

### Curriculum

A **curriculum** is the planned body of knowledge, skills, and experiences that a school or system intends students to learn, along with the sequence in which they will learn it. In the United States, states usually set **standards** (broad statements of what students should know at each grade), districts adopt or write a curriculum aligned to those standards, and teachers turn the curriculum into daily lessons. Textbooks, unit plans, pacing guides, and assessments are all parts of a curriculum's machinery.

Researchers often distinguish three versions of any curriculum. This distinction, used in international studies such as TIMSS, is one of the most useful ideas a school leader can carry:

- **The intended curriculum** is what the documents say students should learn.
- **The enacted curriculum** is what teachers actually teach in their classrooms.
- **The attained curriculum** is what students actually learn and can show.

Every gap between the three is a place where learning leaks out. A standard can be in the documents and never taught because the class ran out of time. A topic can be taught and never learned because it moved too fast.

### Worked Example: One Standard, Three Curricula

Take a seventh-grade math standard: *students compute unit rates involving ratios of fractions.* Trace it through Maya's school year:

| Curriculum layer | What happened | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Intended | The state standard and district pacing guide schedule three weeks of unit rates in October | Pacing guide |
| Enacted | A fire drill, a school assembly, and a review day cut instruction to eight class periods | Ms. Rivera's lesson plans |
| Attained | Students fluent with fractions mastered unit rates; Maya and several others did not, because the lessons assumed fraction skills they lacked | Unit test results |

The table shows the same standard looking fully covered on paper and only partly learned in practice. Most of this book's proposals are about narrowing the distance between the intended and attained curriculum for each individual student.

### Learning Outcome

A **learning outcome** is a specific, observable statement of what a learner will be able to do after instruction. Where a curriculum describes a whole course, a learning outcome describes one target. Good outcomes have three properties: they name an observable action, they name specific content, and they make it possible to tell whether the learner succeeded.

Compare two statements:

- *Weak:* "Students will understand fractions."
- *Strong:* "Students will add two fractions with unlike denominators and simplify the result, correctly solving at least four of five problems."

The weak version uses *understand*, which cannot be observed directly. You cannot watch someone understand. The strong version names an action (add and simplify), specific content (unlike denominators), and a criterion (four of five). A teacher, a parent, or a piece of software could check it.

!!! mascot-tip "The watch-them-do-it test"
    ![Indigo giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Here's a shortcut for checking any learning outcome: ask, "Could I watch a student do this and agree with a colleague on whether they succeeded?" If the verb is *understand*, *know*, *appreciate*, or *learn about*, the answer is no — swap in a verb you can see, like *explain*, *calculate*, *compare*, or *design*.

### Worked Example: Rewriting Outcomes

Here are three outcomes taken from a typical unit plan, rewritten to pass the test above.

| Original outcome | Problem | Rewritten outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Students will know the causes of the Civil War | *Know* is not observable | Students will explain three causes of the Civil War, citing one primary source for each |
| Students will appreciate poetry | *Appreciate* is not observable and not assessable | Students will identify two uses of figurative language in an unfamiliar poem and describe the effect of each |
| Students will learn about ecosystems | *Learn about* names a topic, not an action | Students will predict how removing one species changes a given food web and justify the prediction |

Outcomes matter to this book for a practical reason. An adaptive textbook can only decide what a student should do next if it knows what the student is trying to be able to do. Precise learning outcomes are the raw material for the learning graphs and mastery tracking introduced in Chapters 8 and 9.

## Bloom's Taxonomy and Higher-Order Thinking

Learning outcomes differ not only in content but in the *kind* of thinking they demand. Reciting a definition and designing an experiment are both outcomes, but they ask for very different mental work.

**Bloom's Taxonomy** is a framework for classifying learning outcomes by the kind of cognitive work they require. Benjamin Bloom and colleagues published the original version in 1956. In 2001, Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl published a revision that renamed the levels as verbs and moved *Create* to the top. The revised taxonomy has six levels, listed here from least to most complex:

1. **Remember** — retrieve facts and terms from memory (list, define, recall).
2. **Understand** — explain ideas in one's own words (summarize, explain, classify, compare).
3. **Apply** — use a procedure or idea in a given situation (calculate, solve, use).
4. **Analyze** — break material into parts and see how they relate (compare, organize, differentiate).
5. **Evaluate** — make judgments against criteria (judge, critique, justify).
6. **Create** — put elements together into something new (design, compose, construct).

The levels are cumulative in a loose sense. You cannot evaluate an argument about ecosystems without remembering what a food web is, understanding how energy moves through it, and analyzing which species depend on which. This book uses the 2001 revision throughout, including in its own learning outcomes in the course description.

### Higher-Order Thinking

**Higher-order thinking** refers to the cognitive work in the top three levels of Bloom's Taxonomy: analyzing, evaluating, and creating. The lower three levels (remember, understand, apply) are sometimes called lower-order thinking. The labels are not a ranking of importance. Higher-order thinking depends on a large store of remembered and understood knowledge; a student cannot critique a historical argument without knowing the history.

Higher-order thinking matters to this book for two reasons. First, it is the kind of thinking employers, universities, and citizens most need, and the kind that traditional lecture-and-test schooling exercises least. Studies of classroom tasks repeatedly find that most assignments sit at the Remember and Apply levels. Second, it is exactly the kind of thinking a student can skip by asking a chatbot for the answer. Chapter 12 returns to this risk under the name *cognitive offloading*.

!!! mascot-warning "The pyramid is not a ladder you climb once"
    ![Indigo warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A common trap is reading Bloom's Taxonomy as "teach facts first, then never return to them," or as "lower levels are for weak students." Both are wrong: experts move up and down the levels constantly, and a hard Remember task (recall 50 capitals) can be harder than an easy Analyze task. Classify an outcome by the *kind* of thinking it asks for, not by how difficult or impressive it sounds.

### Worked Example: One Topic at Six Levels

The water cycle can be taught at every level of the taxonomy. Here is a set of tasks on the same topic, one per level:

| Level | Task |
|---|---|
| Remember | List the four main stages of the water cycle |
| Understand | Explain in your own words why puddles disappear on a sunny day |
| Apply | Use the water cycle to predict where dew will form on a cool morning |
| Analyze | Compare rainfall data from two cities and identify which stage of the cycle explains the difference |
| Evaluate | Judge which of two proposed school rain-garden designs will capture more runoff, and defend your judgment |
| Create | Design an experiment to measure evaporation rates in different locations around the school |

A unit that contained only the first two rows would be easy to teach and test. It would also leave students unable to *use* what they know. In the sim specified below, you practice classifying tasks from many subjects.

## The School Day and Direct Instruction

We now know what schools intend to teach. The next question is how they spend their time teaching it.

### School Day

The **school day** is the daily schedule during which students attend school: start and end times, the division of time into periods or blocks, and the transitions, meals, and breaks between them. A typical U.S. public school day runs about six and a half to seven hours, for about 180 days a year, according to NCES surveys. Middle and high schools usually divide the day into six to eight subject periods of 45 to 55 minutes each, separated by passing time. Elementary schools more often keep one class with one teacher for most of the day, with blocks for reading, math, and specials such as art and physical education.

The school day is one of the most stubborn parts of the grammar of schooling. Bus routes, teacher contracts, parents' work hours, athletics, and state instructional-time requirements are all built around it. That is why proposals to change *what happens* during the day, as this book makes, are far more realistic than proposals to change its total length.

Before you look at how the minutes are actually spent, one more term is needed.

### Direct Instruction

**Direct instruction** is a teacher-led method in which the teacher explicitly explains and models new content, guides students through practice, checks understanding, and then has students practice independently. The pattern is often summarized as *I do, we do, you do*. In lowercase, "direct instruction" names this general approach. In capitals, "Direct Instruction" refers to a specific, tightly scripted program developed by Siegfried Engelmann and colleagues in the 1960s, which performed strongly in the large federal Project Follow Through evaluation.

Direct instruction has a good research record for teaching well-defined skills to novices, especially when lessons are broken into small steps, students practice frequently, and the teacher checks understanding before moving on. Barak Rosenshine's widely used *Principles of Instruction* summarize this evidence. The method's weakness is not that explanation is bad. It is that a single explanation, at a single pace, is delivered to a whole classroom of students who are ready for different things.

### Worked Example: An I-Do, We-Do, You-Do Lesson

Here is how Ms. Rivera's 50-minute lesson on unit rates might run:

1. **Warm-up (5 minutes).** Students solve two review problems on fractions while she takes attendance.
2. **I do (10 minutes).** She models, aloud, how to find the price per ounce for a 12-ounce bottle costing \$3.00: \( \$3.00 \div 12 = \$0.25 \) per ounce, explaining each step.

## The Learning Gap

Direct instruction to a whole class works best when students arrive at roughly the same starting point. They rarely do.

A **learning gap** is the difference between what a student is expected to know or be able to do at a given point, as defined by learning outcomes, and what the student actually knows or can do. The term is used at two scales. For an individual, it names specific missing pieces: Maya's gap is fraction operations from fifth grade. For a group, it describes average differences between groups of students, such as the gaps that widened during pandemic school closures.

Learning gaps compound. Mathematics is the clearest case, because so much of it is built on earlier skills. A student who misses fractions struggles with ratios, then with proportional reasoning, then with algebra. Each year's lessons assume last year's outcomes were attained. When they were not, the student falls further behind even while sitting through every lesson. Chapter 9 will show how a learning graph makes these prerequisite chains explicit, which is the first step toward closing gaps one concept at a time.

!!! mascot-thinking "A gap is a missing prerequisite, not a missing effort"
    ![Indigo thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Think of a learning gap like a missing stair in the middle of a staircase. Running harder up the stairs doesn't help; someone has to go back and build the stair. Many students who look unmotivated are standing at a missing stair, and the lesson in front of them assumes it is there.

## Self-Paced Learning and Student Agency

If one pace cannot fit a whole class, an obvious alternative is to let each student move at their own pace. That idea is old, and it comes with an important companion idea about who makes decisions.

### Self-Paced Learning

**Self-paced learning** is an arrangement in which each student moves through learning material at their own speed, advancing when ready rather than when the class calendar says to. The material is usually broken into small units, each with a check, so a student who needs more time on fractions takes it, and a student who already knows unit rates moves on.

Self-pacing has a long history. In 1968 the psychologist Fred Keller described the Personalized System of Instruction, in which college students worked through written units at their own pace and took short unit tests, advancing only after passing. Studies of the system found good results, but it spread slowly. Before cheap computers, self-pacing required a teacher to manage dozens of students on dozens of different pages, with a separate test ready for each. That logistical burden, not a lack of evidence, kept it on the margins. Much of this book's argument is that interactive textbooks remove that burden.

Self-pacing also has real risks. Without structure, some students drift, procrastinate, or move quickly through material without learning it. Students who most need support are often the least able to regulate their own pace. A self-paced system needs frequent checks, visible progress, deadlines that still exist, and adults who notice when a student stalls.

### Worked Example: Two Students, One Unit

Imagine Ms. Rivera reorganizes her ratios unit into eight short lessons, each ending in a five-question check that a student must pass at four of five to advance.

| Student | Starting point | Path through the unit | Days to finish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maya | Missing fraction skills | Two review lessons on fractions first, then all eight lessons, retaking the check for lesson 3 once | 14 |
| Jordan | Mastered ratios last year | Passes the checks for lessons 1–5 on the first day, completes lessons 6–8 | 4 |

Under whole-class pacing both students would spend the same ten days on the unit: Maya would leave without the foundation, and Jordan would spend six days waiting. Under self-pacing, Maya takes longer and actually learns it; Jordan finishes early and has ten days for something else. Notice the new problem this creates: *what does Jordan do with those ten days?* That question, scaled up to a whole school, is the central question of this book.

### Student Agency

**Student agency** is the capacity and opportunity for students to make meaningful choices about their own learning: what to study, how to study it, how to show what they learned, and when to move on. The OECD's *Learning Compass 2030* describes agency as a student's ability to set goals, reflect, and act responsibly to bring about change. Agency has two parts that are easy to blur. *Capacity* is the student's skill and confidence in making decisions. *Opportunity* is whether the school actually gives them decisions to make.

Agency is a matter of degree. A student who chooses which of two essay prompts to answer has a little agency. A student who chooses a semester-long research question, plans the steps, and decides how to present the result has a great deal. Most traditional schools give students little agency over academic work, because a whole-class schedule leaves little room for choice.

Self-paced learning and student agency are related but distinct. Self-pacing is about *speed*: a student controls how fast they move through a fixed sequence. Agency is about *choice*: a student controls what and how. A self-paced program can offer almost no agency (every student does the same lessons in the same order, only at different speeds), and a project can offer great agency with a fixed deadline.

### Worked Example: Four Degrees of Agency

The same assignment, a report on a local ecosystem, can be offered at different degrees of agency:

| Degree | What the student decides | Example |
|---|---|---|
| None | Nothing | Everyone writes a five-paragraph report on the pond behind the school, due Friday |
| Low | One choice within a set | Choose the pond, the creek, or the park woodland |
| Moderate | Topic and format | Choose any local ecosystem and present it as a report, a video, or a field guide |
| High | Question, method, and product | Pose your own question about a local ecosystem, plan how to investigate it, and decide how to share what you found |

Higher agency is not automatically better. A student new to research may flounder at the "High" row without support, while a confident student may be bored at "None." Good teaching gradually hands over more decisions as students build the capacity to make them well.

## Formative Assessment: Checking Learning While It Happens

Self-paced learning depends on knowing when a student is ready to move on. Whole-class teaching depends on knowing whether to slow down. Both depend on assessment.

**Formative assessment** is the process of gathering evidence of student learning *during* instruction and using it to adjust teaching and learning while there is still time to act. It is contrasted with **summative assessment**, which measures learning at the end of a unit or course to assign a grade or report results. The terms come from Michael Scriven's 1967 work on evaluation, and Benjamin Bloom applied them to classroom learning shortly after. A useful shorthand: formative assessment is a check-up, summative assessment is an autopsy.

The definition's key phrase is *uses it to adjust*. A quiz is not formative because of its format. It is formative only if the results change what happens next: the teacher reteaches, the student practices a specific skill, or the class moves on with evidence that it is ready. In their influential 1998 review *Inside the Black Box*, Paul Black and Dylan Wiliam concluded that strengthening formative assessment produced some of the largest learning gains of any classroom intervention they reviewed.

Common formative techniques include exit tickets (one or two questions at the end of a lesson), hinge questions (a single multiple-choice question mid-lesson whose wrong answers each reveal a specific misconception), mini whiteboards that every student holds up at once, and short low-stakes quizzes.

### Worked Example: A Hinge Question in Action

Midway through her unit-rate lesson, Ms. Rivera asks one question and has every student hold up the letter of their answer:

*A 6-pack of juice costs \$4.50. What is the price per bottle?*

- A. \$0.75 (correct: \( 4.50 \div 6 = 0.75 \))
- B. \$1.33 (divided 6 by 4.50 — reversed the division)
- C. \$27.00 (multiplied instead of divided)
- D. \$1.50 (guessed from rounding)

She sees 17 A's, 8 B's, and 3 C's. Instead of moving on, she pulls the 11 students with B or C to the side board for five minutes on *which quantity goes on top*, while the other 17 begin practice. That is formative assessment: the evidence changed what happened next for 11 students, in the same period, before the misconception was practiced ten more times.

The following table contrasts the two kinds of assessment you have now seen.

| | Formative assessment | Summative assessment |
|---|---|---|
| When | During learning | At the end of a unit or course |
| Purpose | Adjust teaching and learning | Judge and report what was learned |
| Typical stakes | Low or none | Often high (grades, promotion, accountability) |
| Example | Hinge question, exit ticket | Unit exam, state test, final project |
| Who acts on the result | Teacher and student, right away | Teachers, families, administrators, later |

The constraint, again, is the classroom. A teacher can run one hinge question per lesson for 28 students and act on the result. Running a different check for each student after every small step, and responding to each, is beyond what one adult can do. That is exactly the work an interactive textbook can take on, as Chapter 8 shows.

## Spaced Practice and Retrieval Practice: How Learning Lasts

Earlier we separated performance from learning. Two of the most reliable findings in all of learning science explain how to turn short-term performance into lasting learning, and both are underused in most schools.

### Spaced Practice

**Spaced practice** (also called distributed practice) is the strategy of spreading study or practice sessions over time, with gaps between them, instead of concentrating them in one long session. The opposite, **massed practice**, is cramming: doing all the practice at once.

The finding goes back to Hermann Ebbinghaus, who in 1885 measured his own forgetting of nonsense syllables and charted what is now called the *forgetting curve*: memory fades quickly at first, then more slowly. He also found that reviews spread over days held up far better than the same number of reviews done in one sitting. More than a century of later research, summarized in a large 2006 meta-analysis by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues, confirms that spacing improves long-term retention across ages, subjects, and kinds of material.

Why does spacing work? A useful explanation is that a review is most valuable when the memory has started to fade. Pulling back a half-forgotten idea takes effort, and that effort strengthens the memory more than reviewing something you already have in mind. Psychologist Robert Bjork calls this a **desirable difficulty**: a condition that makes practice feel harder and slower but improves long-term learning.

### Retrieval Practice

**Retrieval practice** is the strategy of learning by actively recalling information from memory, through practice questions, flashcards, or trying to write down what you remember, rather than by rereading or reviewing the material. It is sometimes called the *testing effect*, because low-stakes testing turns out to be one of the most powerful ways to learn, not just a way to measure learning.

In a well-known 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students read a short passage and then either reread it or take a recall test on it. Five minutes later, the rereading group remembered slightly more. One week later, the retrieval group remembered substantially more. Rereading produced better short-term performance; retrieval produced better learning. A 2013 review by John Dunlosky and colleagues rated retrieval practice and spaced practice the two highest-utility study strategies of the ten they examined, while rereading and highlighting, the strategies students use most, rated low.

### Worked Example: Planning Three Reviews

Maya has a science test on cell structure on day 30. She has time for three short review sessions after the first lesson on day 0. Compare three plans:

| Plan | When she reviews | How she reviews | What the research predicts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cram | Three sessions back-to-back on day 0 | Rereads her notes | Feels confident on day 0; most is forgotten by day 30 |
| Spaced reread | Days 1, 3, and 7 | Rereads her notes | Much better retention on day 30 than cramming |
| Spaced retrieval | Days 1, 4, and 12 | Closes her notes and writes down everything she remembers, then checks | The strongest retention of the three on day 30 |

The total study time is the same in all three plans. Only the timing and the method change. This is why spacing and retrieval matter so much for the AI-native model: an adaptive textbook can schedule reviews for each student at the right moment, something no teacher with 140 students can do by hand.

## Educational Technology

Everything in this chapter so far could happen with paper, chalk, and a teacher. The last group of concepts concerns the tools schools add to that picture and the money that pays for all of it.

**Educational technology**, often shortened to *edtech*, is the use of tools, devices, software, and media to support teaching and learning, together with the practices for using them well. The definition includes the practice, not just the device: a projector is hardware, but how a teacher uses it to show student work for discussion is educational technology.

Edtech has a long and humbling history. In the 1920s the psychologist Sidney Pressey built a machine that gave students multiple-choice questions and advanced only when they answered correctly. In the 1950s B. F. Skinner promoted "teaching machines" that broke material into small steps with immediate feedback. In 1960 the University of Illinois launched PLATO, a computer-based instruction system that pioneered features later familiar online. Each wave came with predictions that it would transform schools. The historian Larry Cuban documented a recurring pattern across decades: new technology is purchased with great enthusiasm, then used far less, and in more traditional ways, than its promoters expected. His 2001 book on school computers was titled *Oversold and Underused*.

The following table gathers the edtech waves just described, along with later ones, so you can see the pattern at a glance.

| Era | Technology | Promise | What mostly happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1920s | Pressey's testing machine | Automate drill and free teachers | Little adoption; seen as a curiosity |
| 1950s–1960s | Skinner's teaching machines, programmed instruction | Each student learns at their own pace with instant feedback | Brief enthusiasm, then decline |
| 1960s–1970s | PLATO and mainframe instruction | Computer-based lessons at scale | Influential, but costly and limited to a few institutions |
| 1980s | Classroom microcomputers | Computers in every school | Machines often sat in labs, used for typing and games |
| 1990s–2000s | Internet, interactive whiteboards | Access to the world's information; engaging lessons | Whiteboards often used as expensive projector screens |
| 2000s–2010s | One-to-one laptops and tablets, learning management systems, online video lessons | Personalized learning for every student | Mixed results; strongest where teaching practice changed too |

The lesson of this history is not that technology fails. It is that technology alone rarely changes the *grammar of schooling*: the age-graded classroom, the bell schedule, the single pace. When the tool is bolted onto an unchanged school day, it tends to be absorbed into old habits.

### Worked Example: Two Uses of the Same Tool

A district buys a tablet for every seventh grader. Consider how two classrooms use them for the same unit:

| | Classroom A | Classroom B |
|---|---|---|
| How tablets are used | Students read the textbook as a PDF and type answers to the same worksheet | Students work through self-paced lessons with immediate checks; the teacher's dashboard shows who is stuck |
| Learning job the tool does | None that paper didn't already do | Practice with feedback, and formative assessment data for the teacher |
| What the teacher does | Lectures, then grades typed worksheets | Pulls small groups based on the dashboard |
| Change to the grammar of schooling | None | Pace becomes individual; teacher time shifts from lecturing to targeted help |

Both classrooms "use technology." Only one used it to change how learning works. This distinction, between digitizing the old model and changing the model, is the thread that connects this chapter to the five-level model of intelligent textbooks in Chapter 6.

## Paying for It: Public School Funding and Per-Pupil Spending

Every design choice in a school, from class size to the tablet cart, is constrained by money. Two terms describe where it comes from and how it is usually measured.

### Public School Funding

**Public school funding** is the system of revenues, mainly taxes, that pays for public schools. In the United States, funding comes from three levels of government. Local revenue comes mostly from property taxes. State revenue comes from income and sales taxes and is often distributed through formulas meant to help districts with less local wealth. Federal revenue, historically under 10 percent of the total, is targeted at specific purposes such as supporting students from low-income families (Title I) and students with disabilities (IDEA). The federal share rose temporarily during the pandemic-relief years. State and local sources have each historically provided a little under half of the total.

Because local revenue depends on property values, districts in wealthier areas can raise more money with the same tax rate than districts in poorer areas. State formulas reduce this gap but rarely close it. School funding lawsuits in many states have turned on exactly this question. Equity in funding comes back in Chapter 3 and again in Chapter 21, where the book asks whether an AI-native school would narrow or widen such gaps.

### Per-Pupil Spending

**Per-pupil spending** is the total amount spent on a group of public schools in a year divided by the number of students enrolled, giving the average cost to educate one student for one year. It is the standard unit for comparing school costs across districts, states, and countries. Most published figures use *current* spending, which covers day-to-day operations such as salaries, benefits, supplies, and transportation, and excludes construction and debt payments.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau's annual survey of school finances, U.S. public schools spent about \$15,600 per pupil in current spending in fiscal year 2022, with wide variation between states. Roughly 60 percent of current spending goes to instruction, mostly teacher salaries and benefits. Check the latest Census release before citing these figures, since they rise most years.

### Worked Example: Computing Per-Pupil Spending

An illustrative district reports the following for one year:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Current spending (salaries, benefits, supplies, transportation, services) | \$180,000,000 |
| Construction of a new middle school (capital outlay) | \$40,000,000 |
| Enrollment | 12,000 students |

Per-pupil current spending excludes the construction cost:

\[
\text{per-pupil spending} = \frac{\$180{,}000{,}000}{12{,}000 \text{ students}} = \$15{,}000 \text{ per student}
\]

Including the construction would give \( \$220{,}000{,}000 \div 12{,}000 \approx \$18{,}333 \), a misleading figure for comparing everyday costs, because a new building is a once-in-decades expense.

Now divide by the school year to get a daily figure. With 180 school days:

\[
\frac{\$15{,}000}{180 \text{ days}} \approx \$83 \text{ per student per day}
\]

Keep that daily figure in mind. Later chapters compare it with the cost of interactive textbooks, which the course description describes as heading toward pennies per student per day, and with the tuition of private AI-first schools.

!!! mascot-warning "An average hides the spread"
    ![Indigo warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A common trap is treating one per-pupil number as "what a student costs." Averages blend students who need far more (special education, English learners) with students who need less, and they mix current spending with capital spending. When comparing two figures, first check that both use current spending and the same year.

## Key Takeaways

The vocabulary in this chapter describes a school system that works, often well, within a hard constraint: one teacher, one pace, many students, fixed time.

- **Education** is the organized effort; **learning** is the lasting change inside the learner. Strong performance on today's test is not the same as learning.
- A **school** does many jobs besides academic instruction, and any redesign of the **school day** must still do all of them.
- Every **student** arrives at a different starting point, while a **classroom** gives one **teacher** less than two minutes per student per period for individual attention.
- A **curriculum** exists in three versions (intended, enacted, attained), and precise **learning outcomes** with observable verbs are how you tell the three apart.
- **Bloom's Taxonomy** classifies outcomes by the kind of thinking they ask for, and **higher-order thinking** (analyze, evaluate, create) depends on a strong base of the lower levels.
- **Direct instruction** is effective for novices but paces the whole room together, which turns ordinary differences in readiness into **learning gaps**.
- **Self-paced learning** and **student agency** answer the one-pace problem in different ways (speed versus choice) and both need structure to work.
- **Formative assessment** uses evidence during learning to change what happens next; **spaced practice** and **retrieval practice** turn performance into lasting learning.
- **Educational technology** has repeatedly been oversold when bolted onto an unchanged school day; it matters when it changes which learning job is done and by whom.
- **Public school funding** comes from local, state, and federal sources, and **per-pupil spending** of roughly \$15,000 a year, about \$83 a school day in our example, is the baseline any new model will be compared against.
