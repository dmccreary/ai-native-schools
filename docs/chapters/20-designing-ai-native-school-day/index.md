## AI-First and AI-Native

Two terms need separating, since the field uses them loosely. An **AI-first school** is a school that puts AI technology at the center of how it delivers instruction, often described by the amount of AI software the students use. Alpha School, whose students spend about two hours a day on adaptive academic software, with the rest of the day in life-skills workshops and projects led by adult guides, is the best-known example, and the AI-First Stories appendix tells both its story and the cautionary tale of Unbound Academy, which used the same software without the in-person afternoons.

An **AI-native school** is the design this book proposes: a school built around a pedagogy and a purpose first, and then equipped with the AI that serves it. In an AI-native school the starting question is what students need, which is mastery of the academic core and practice with people, and AI is used where it does that job best, with guardrails, at the lowest level that works (Chapter 6), and usually without a live model per student (Chapter 12). The difference matters because "AI-first" describes a tool, and the tool alone is not the model, as the contrast between Alpha and Unbound shows.

| | AI-first school | AI-native school (this book) |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | The technology | The learning goals and the people |
| Typical AI use | A central part of the day, often at a high level | The minimum level that does the job; live AI used selectively |
| The afternoon | Varies; sometimes absent | Required, structured, pro-social |
| Evidence standard | Often the school's own reports | Independent evidence, with a pilot |
| Main risk | Technology without culture | Under-resourcing the afternoon |

The **morning-afternoon model** is the structure at the core of the AI-native school: an academic morning of adaptive, mastery-based learning, followed by an afternoon of pro-social activity with other people. The **AI-native school day** is the day that results, with the particular schedule, the screen limits (Chapter 12), the curiosity block (Chapter 2), and the afternoon program (Chapter 14) fitted together.

## The AI-Native School Day by Grade

The design is not the same for a six-year-old and a sixteen-year-old. Young children need more play, movement, and adult attention and much less screen time. Older students need more academic depth, credits, and preparation for what follows. The table gives an illustrative design for four grade bands. Every number is a starting point for a school to adjust, not a finding.

| Grade band | Academic morning | Cap on screen time in the morning | Afternoon emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| K–2 | About 90 minutes | About 30 minutes | Play, movement, music, hands-on projects, stories |
| 3–5 | About 120 minutes | About 75 minutes | Projects, arts, sports, simple clubs, service with older buddies |
| 6–8 | About 150 minutes | About 120 minutes | Projects with public exhibitions, clubs, arts, life-skills workshops |
| 9–12 | About 180 minutes, with seminars and labs counted | About 120 minutes | Capstone projects, internships and community partnerships, ensembles, service |

For the youngest children the main message is restraint. National pediatric guidance has for years recommended limits on screen time for young children and emphasized play and interaction, so the K–2 morning is mostly adult-led and hands-on, with a short adaptive block for reading and number practice. High school adds the constraint of credits and external exams, and the Carnegie unit of Chapter 3, so the 180-minute morning there includes teacher-led seminars and laboratory work that do not run on a screen.

### Schedule Redesign

**Schedule redesign** is the rebuilding of a school's daily and weekly timetable to carry the new model. It begins from the fixed constraints that Ms. Okafor listed: bus routes, lunch service, teacher contracts, state rules on instructional time (which in many states require a minimum number of hours or days, and which a school should check, since some states allow waivers for innovation), and the building's rooms. Good schedule redesign negotiates these constraints one at a time instead of ignoring them.

### Worked Example: A Middle School Day

This is an illustrative schedule for a middle school of 280 students, built to the 6–8 row above: a 150-minute morning with a 120-minute screen cap, then a project-and-people afternoon.

| Time | Block | Screen? |
|---|---|---|
| 7:50–8:00 | Arrival and advisory check-in with a coach | No |
| 8:00–8:40 | Adaptive math | Yes (40 min) |
| 8:40–8:45 | Break: stretch, look away | No |
| 8:45–9:25 | Adaptive reading and writing | Yes (40 min) |
| 9:25–9:30 | Break | No |
| 9:30–10:10 | Science and social studies: interactive pages and simulations | Yes (40 min) |
| 10:10–10:15 | Break | No |
| 10:15–10:30 | Small-group coaching with the teacher | No |
| 10:30–11:00 | Movement and snack | No |
| 11:00–12:00 | Project block (driving question, teams) | Some |
| 12:00–12:40 | Lunch | No |
| 12:40–1:40 | Arts, ensembles, or sports | No |
| 1:40–2:40 | Clubs, life-skills workshops, or service learning | Some |
| 2:40–3:00 | Reflection, exhibition preparation, dismissal | No |

The academic morning is 150 minutes, of which 120 is on a screen, matching the cap. The afternoon contains 200 minutes of programming (project, arts or sports, clubs and workshops, and reflection), arranged in four blocks. The bus still leaves at 3:05.

### Staffing Model

A **staffing model** is the plan for which adults are present, in which roles, in what numbers, at each part of the day. The morning and afternoon need different staffing. In the morning, an adult coach with a section of 28 students moves among them, checks the dashboard, and pulls small groups. In the afternoon, programs need smaller groups, since the SAFE practices of Chapter 14 depend on an adult who can structure, observe, and debrief. A common target for project and arts blocks is one adult for 15 to 20 students.

### Worked Example: Adult Hours for the 280-Student School

Compare the adult hours needed for the traditional day and the AI-native day for 280 students, using sections of 28 for the illustration (real pupil-to-teacher ratios in the United States are lower, about 15 to 1, because teachers also have planning periods and non-classroom duties, so these figures show the *shape* of the change and not a budget).

| | Traditional | AI-native |
|---|---|---|
| Morning | 10 sections × 150 min of teaching = 1,500 adult-minutes | 10 sections × 150 min of coaching = 1,500 adult-minutes |
| Afternoon | 10 sections × 240 min of teaching = 2,400 adult-minutes | 14 groups of 20 × 200 min of programming = 2,800 adult-minutes |
| Total per day | 3,900 adult-minutes = 65 adult-hours | 4,300 adult-minutes = about 71.7 adult-hours |

The AI-native day needs about 10 percent more adult-hours in this illustration, not fewer. The change is in *what the adults do*: coaching and facilitating instead of lecturing. The model does not save on staff. Its savings come from elsewhere, such as content and curriculum (Chapter 7), and what it buys is a day in which every adult hour is spent on a person. The cost depends on who fills the afternoon slots, since teachers, specialists, trained paraprofessionals, and community partners cost different amounts and bring different strengths.

## The Changing Teacher Role

The **changing teacher role** is the shift in what teachers do in an AI-native school, from presenting content to the whole class toward guiding, coaching, and supporting individual students and groups. The educator Alison King put the idea in a 1993 phrase, "from sage on the stage to guide on the side." It is easy to say and hard to do, and it must not be mistaken for "teachers matter less." A teacher's expertise, in what students find difficult, how to explain it differently, and how to notice a student in trouble, matters more when software has taken over the routine delivery.

Four overlapping roles describe the new work.

**Teacher as coach** is the role of helping students set goals, practice, and improve, with specific feedback and encouragement, as a sports coach does. **Teacher as mentor** is the role of a longer-term relationship in which an adult offers guidance, advice, and support for a student's growth as a person as well as a learner: the advisory teacher who knows a student for years. **Teacher as facilitator** is the role of structuring group learning, setting up the task, asking the questions that deepen thinking, and stepping back so students do the work. A **learning guide** is the term some AI-first schools, including Alpha School, use for the adult who supports students' motivation, effort, and progress in place of delivering content. A guide may not be a subject expert, and that is a design choice with risks: a student who needs a human explanation of a hard concept needs a person who can give one.

### Worked Example: How a Teacher's Week Changes

An illustrative comparison of how Mr. Hale spends his instructional time, in percent.

| Activity | Traditional week | AI-native week |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-class lecture and explanation | 50 | 5 |
| Supervising practice | 20 | 0 |
| Small-group and one-to-one coaching | 5 | 30 |
| Project and afternoon facilitation | 0 | 30 |
| Mentoring and advisory | 0 | 10 |
| Planning and preparation | 10 | 15 |
| Grading and feedback | 15 | 5 |
| Other | 0 | 5 |
| Total | 100 | 100 |

Lecturing and supervised practice, 70 percent of the week, fall to 5 percent, and coaching, facilitation, and mentoring, none of which is lecture, rise to 70 percent. The shift is large, and it is why Chapters 17 and 19 spent so long on losses, concerns, coaching, and champions. The plan asks the teacher to give up a great deal of what they know how to do.

## Assessment Redesign

**Assessment redesign** is the rebuilding of how a school measures and reports learning so that it fits the new model. In the traditional design most assessment is by tests and grades at the end of units. The AI-native design has two sources of evidence: mastery data from the morning (Chapter 8), which is continuous and automatic, and evidence from the afternoon, which is performances, products, and portfolios. Redesign also has to answer the integrity question that AI raises (below).

**Mastery-based assessment** is assessment in which a student advances after demonstrating mastery of a concept, usually against a stated threshold, with opportunities to try again, in place of earning a grade at a fixed time. It uses the mastery rule and the thresholds of Chapter 12. Reports describe *what a student has mastered*, and not a letter grade for a period. The Mastery Transcript Consortium, founded in 2017, is one effort to build transcripts on this basis.

**Performance assessment** is assessment in which students show what they can do by carrying out a meaningful task, such as presenting, building, performing, or defending a position, and a rubric is used to score it. The public exhibition of Chapter 14 is a performance assessment. Its strength is that it measures the skills that tests do not, such as communication and collaboration. Its challenge is reliability: different scorers can disagree, so scorers need training, shared rubrics, and some double scoring.

**Portfolio assessment** is assessment based on a collection of a student's work over time, selected and reflected on by the student, which shows growth and the quality of what the student can do. In New York State, a consortium of schools has for years used performance tasks and portfolios in place of most state exams under a waiver, which makes it one of the best-known examples.

### Worked Example: What Goes in a Student's Record

| Evidence | Source | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Concept mastery summary (dashboard) | Morning, automatic | Which of 120 concepts are mastered, and when |
| Project portfolio with reflections | Afternoon, student-curated | The quality of work and the student's growth |
| Public exhibition score (rubric, two scorers) | Afternoon, performance | Communication and teamwork, with reliability checks |
| Teacher mentor narrative | Advisory | Character, effort, and growth |

No single piece is the whole picture. The set is a fuller portrait than a grade, and each piece comes with the checks, such as thresholds, double scoring, and reflection, that keep it trustworthy.

## Academic Integrity and the Detector Problem

**Academic integrity** is the commitment of a school community to honesty in learning and assessment: doing one's own work and giving credit for the work of others. AI has made the old rules unclear, since a chatbot can write an essay in seconds. The response has two parts, which together are better than either alone: redesign the work so that cheating is less useful, and set clear rules for when AI is allowed.

Redesign means that important assessments are done in person, in class; that process evidence, such as drafts, notes, and revision history, is part of the work; that students explain or defend their work orally; and that tasks are personal and local, so a generic answer does not fit. Clear rules mean saying, for each task, whether AI is not allowed, allowed for certain steps, or allowed with disclosure. A simple "traffic light" works: red for no AI (an in-class writing sample), yellow for AI in specific ways (brainstorming, feedback on a draft, with notes on what was used), and green for AI as a full tool with disclosure (a research project).

An **AI cheating detector** is software that claims to tell whether a piece of text was written by an AI system. They are tempting, since they promise to restore the old system. The evidence is against relying on them. In July 2023, OpenAI withdrew its own AI text classifier because of its low accuracy. **Detector false positives** are the cases in which the detector wrongly labels human-written work as AI-written. Even a small false-positive rate produces a lot of wrongly accused students when applied to thousands of essays, and the harm falls on the accused student, who must prove they did their own work.

**Bias against ELL writers** is the finding that AI detectors are more likely to flag the writing of English-language learners as AI-generated. A 2023 study by Weixin Liang and colleagues at Stanford tested seven popular detectors on essays written by non-native English speakers for the TOEFL exam, and found that, on average, more than half were wrongly labeled as AI-generated, while essays by U.S. eighth graders, who are native speakers, were classified almost perfectly. One explanation is that detectors often look for text that is highly predictable, and writers working in a second language tend to use simpler, more predictable wording. An approach that wrongly accuses the students who already face the greatest barriers has a clear equity problem (Chapter 3), and it is one reason the AI-First Stories appendix advises redesigning assignments instead of relying on detection.

### Worked Example: How Many Accusations Are False?

Suppose a school runs a detector on 10,000 essays. Five percent, 500, were actually written with AI, and the detector catches 80 percent of them. It wrongly flags 1 percent of the human-written essays, a rate that sounds small.

| | Number |
|---|---|
| Essays written by AI | 500 |
| Human-written essays | 9,500 |
| AI essays correctly flagged (80%) | 400 |
| Human essays wrongly flagged (1%) | 95 |
| Total flagged | 495 |
| Share of flagged essays that are false accusations | 95 ÷ 495 ≈ 19% |

About one in five accusations is wrong even with a favorable detector. Now add the equity problem. Suppose 10 percent of the human-written essays, 950, are by English-language learners, and the detector wrongly flags 20 percent of them, while it still flags 1 percent of the other 8,550.

| | Wrong flags |
|---|---|
| ELL writers: 950 × 20% | 190 |
| Other writers: 8,550 × 1% | 85.5 |
| Total wrong flags | 275.5 |
| ELL writers' share of the wrong flags | 190 ÷ 275.5 ≈ 69% |
| Share of all flagged essays that are false: 275.5 ÷ (400 + 275.5) | about 41% |

Students who are 10 percent of the human writers receive about 69 percent of the false accusations, and about two in five accusations are false. The numbers are illustrative, and they show how a modest-looking error rate, combined with a bias, produces a serious injustice.

## Equity of Access

**Equity of access** is the principle that every student has the devices, connectivity, supports, and opportunities to take full part in the new model, whatever their family's income, location, language, or disability. The design that has been described could narrow gaps by giving every student a patient tutor and a rich afternoon, or widen them if only well-resourced students benefit (Chapter 3). Five commitments help to keep it on the narrowing side.

- **Devices and connectivity:** a school device for every student, loaner or take-home devices where homework needs them, and offline access (Chapter 9) for students without reliable internet.
- **Accessibility:** content that works with screen readers and keyboards, captions, and supports for students with disabilities.
- **Language:** supports for English-language learners, such as bilingual content and tools that do not penalize non-native writing.
- **A free afternoon:** no fees for afternoon programs and transportation so that every student can take part, since a program that costs money recreates the gap.
- **Equitable partnerships:** deliberately seeking community partners for schools that have few nearby, so that enrichment does not cluster where families are already advantaged.

## Procurement and Vendor Evaluation

**Procurement** is the formal process by which a school or district buys goods and services, governed by purchasing laws and board policy: defining the need, soliciting proposals or bids, evaluating them, awarding a contract, and managing it. It is where a good plan can be undone by a bad purchase. The Los Angeles "Ed" chatbot, described in the appendix, is the cautionary case: the district committed about \$6 million to a startup, spent about \$3 million, and the company collapsed within months of launch. The lesson the appendix draws is that schools need procurement rules before they buy AI products.

**Procurement criteria** are the standards a district sets in advance for what a product must meet in order to be bought. This book's earlier chapters supply most of them. The five levels (Chapter 6) give a vocabulary for the requirement: "we need a Level 2 textbook, not a Level 4 chatbot." The criteria also include independent evidence of effect (Chapter 2), privacy and data governance appropriate to the level (Chapter 10), guardrails for any tutor (Chapter 12), the interoperability standards of Chapter 9 (LTI, OneRoster, xAPI), the total cost of ownership (Chapter 7), and, among those, the value beyond content a vendor provides. **Vendor evaluation** is the use of the criteria to assess a particular vendor and its product: reviewing its documents, testing the product, checking references, and judging its financial and security health (vendor risk, Chapter 10).

### Worked Example: A Weighted Scorecard

Lincoln's district uses a scorecard with seven criteria and weights that sum to 100. Each vendor is scored from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) on each criterion. The weighted score is the sum of weight × score ÷ 5, so a perfect vendor would score 100. A *knockout rule* adds that a score of 2 or below on privacy or on vendor viability stops the purchase until the problem is resolved.

| Criterion | Weight | Vendor A score | Vendor B score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independent evidence of effect | 20 | 4 | 3 |
| Privacy and data governance | 20 | 5 | 3 |
| Level and capability fit | 15 | 3 | 5 |
| Guardrails for the AI tutor | 15 | 4 | 3 |
| Standards and interoperability | 10 | 3 | 5 |
| Total cost of ownership | 10 | 4 | 3 |
| Vendor viability (finances, references) | 10 | 2 | 5 |

Vendor A's weighted score is \( 20 \times 4/5 + 20 \times 5/5 + 15 \times 3/5 + 15 \times 4/5 + 10 \times 3/5 + 10 \times 4/5 + 10 \times 2/5 = 16 + 20 + 9 + 12 + 6 + 8 + 4 = 75 \). Vendor B's is \( 12 + 12 + 15 + 9 + 10 + 6 + 10 = 74 \). Vendor A scores higher on total, and the knockout rule stops it, because its viability score is 2. This is the Ed lesson in a rule: a strong product from a company that may not survive is a risk the total does not show, so the district asks Vendor A to resolve the viability concern, with financial statements and an exit plan, before it can proceed. Meanwhile Vendor B may proceed to a pilot.

## Readiness and Pilots

**School readiness assessment** is the review of whether a school is prepared to start the new model, drawing together the readiness checks of earlier chapters: governance readiness (Chapter 10), adoption readiness (Chapter 15), staff AI literacy (Chapter 19), and the capacity to run the schedule and staffing above. It asks: are the privacy basics in place for the level we plan to use? Do the attributes of the innovation fit our teachers? Do teachers know enough about AI to guide students and answer families? Do we have the devices and connectivity? Is there an opinion leader to carry the story and a principal ready to protect the pilot? A school that answers "no" to several should fix them, or stay at a lower level, before it starts.

A **pilot program** is a limited, planned trial of the new model, in one grade, one school, or a few classrooms, designed to find out whether it works and what it needs before it spreads. It extends the pilot classroom of Chapter 19 to the scale of a program. **Pilot goals** are the specific, measurable outcomes the pilot is meant to achieve, written before it begins, together with the rule for deciding what to do next. A pilot without stated goals cannot fail, and so cannot teach anything. Good goals are specific, measured against a baseline, include more than test scores, and are paired with a *decision rule*.

### Worked Example: A Pilot Decision Rule

Lincoln's pilot for one grade of 140 students states five goals and a rule in advance.

| Goal | Baseline | Target | Measured by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learning gain on the state-aligned test | Comparison section | At least 0.15 standard deviations higher | Independent scorer |
| Teacher workload | 53 hours a week (Chapter 3) | No more than 2 hours higher | Weekly time log |
| Family satisfaction | n/a | At least 70 percent satisfied | Survey |
| Privacy | n/a | No serious incident | Incident log |
| No group harmed | Comparison section | No subgroup more than 0.1 standard deviations lower | Subgroup analysis |

The rule: **scale** if all five goals are met; **stop** if there is a serious privacy incident or any subgroup is more than 0.1 standard deviations lower than the comparison; otherwise **extend** the pilot a semester and fix what was missed. The rule means the decision is made on the evidence, not on the mood of the room.

## Key Takeaways

- An **AI-first school** is defined by its technology; an **AI-native school** by its learning goals and people, using the **morning-afternoon model**; the **AI-native school day** varies by grade band, with less screen time for the youngest.
- **Schedule redesign** works within real constraints; the 280-student example needed about 10 percent more adult-hours, so the **staffing model** changes what adults do, not how many are needed.
- The **changing teacher role** moves from lecturing to **teacher as coach**, **teacher as mentor**, and **teacher as facilitator**; a **learning guide** is not necessarily a subject expert, which is a design risk.
- **Assessment redesign** combines **mastery-based assessment**, **performance assessment**, and **portfolio assessment**, each with checks for reliability.
- **Academic integrity** is best served by redesigning assignments and setting clear rules; an **AI cheating detector** produces **detector false positives**, with **bias against ELL writers**, so about two in five flags can be false in our illustration.
- **Equity of access** needs devices, accessibility, language support, a free afternoon, and equitable partnerships.
- **Procurement criteria** and **vendor evaluation**, with a weighted scorecard and a knockout rule, prevent a repeat of the Ed chatbot; a **school readiness assessment** checks the school before a **pilot program** with written **pilot goals** and a decision rule.
