# Quiz: Organizational Change Models

Test your understanding of the major organizational change models, school culture, the implementation dip, and managing expectations with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What are the three stages of Kurt Lewin's change model?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Awareness, desire, and reinforcement
2. Unfreeze, change, and refreeze
3. Ending, neutral zone, and new beginning
4. Urgency, coalition, and vision
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Lewin's 1947 model describes change as unfreezing the current way of doing things, changing to the new way, and refreezing so the new way becomes stable. It is the oldest and simplest model in the chapter and also gave the field the idea of forces for and against change. The first option draws on ADKAR, the third is Bridges' transition model, and the fourth names Kotter's first three steps.

    **Concept Tested:** Organizational Change

    **See:** [The Models at a Glance](index.md#the-models-at-a-glance)

#### 2. What are the five elements of the ADKAR model?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Analysis, Design, Knowledge, Assessment, Review
2. Artifacts, Dialogue, Knowledge, Assumptions, Results
3. Action, Data, Kindness, Adoption, Reflection
4. Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Jeff Hiatt's ADKAR model, published by Prosci in 2006, describes what an individual needs to change: Awareness of the need, Desire to take part, Knowledge of how to change, Ability to do it, and Reinforcement to sustain it. Taken in order, a person without awareness will not want the change, and one with desire but no knowledge cannot do it. Its value is that it points at the specific missing element for one person.

    **Concept Tested:** ADKAR Model

    **See:** [The Models at a Glance](index.md#the-models-at-a-glance)

#### 3. Schein describes organizational culture at three levels. Why does his model explain the failure of change efforts that alter only the visible surface?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Because unseen basic assumptions beneath artifacts and values keep shaping behavior
2. Because visible artifacts are the deepest level and cannot be changed by leaders
3. Because stated values always match what people actually do day to day
4. Because culture changes automatically once a new principal or leader is hired
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Schein's levels are the visible artifacts, the stated values, and the unseen basic assumptions beneath them. Change efforts that alter only artifacts, such as a new schedule or a new tool, leave the assumptions in place, and those assumptions keep driving behavior. That is why an organization can resist what it says it wants. Artifacts are the surface level, not the deepest. The chapter also notes that replacing a leader is not by itself a change in how work is done.

    **Concept Tested:** Organizational Culture

    **See:** [The Models at a Glance](index.md#the-models-at-a-glance)

#### 4. A teacher says, "I believe in the new textbook and want to use it, but I don't know how to set up the mastery checks." Using ADKAR, what is missing, and what kind of help fits?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Awareness; a conversation about the evidence for the change
2. Desire; a visit to a classroom where the textbook is working well
3. Knowledge; training or coaching on how to set up the checks
4. Reinforcement; public recognition for teachers who use the textbook
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The teacher has awareness and desire but lacks knowledge of how to do it. The chapter's tip is to match the help to the missing piece: training fixes missing knowledge and ability, while missing awareness or desire needs a different kind of conversation. A talk about evidence or a classroom visit is what Mr. Hale, who did not see the need, required. Reinforcement comes after a person can already do the new practice.

    **Concept Tested:** ADKAR Model

    **See:** [Worked Example: Two Teachers, Two Diagnoses](index.md#worked-example-two-teachers-two-diagnoses)

#### 5. What distinction is at the center of Bridges' transition model?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Between leaders who plan a change and teachers who carry it out
2. Between short-term wins and long-term anchoring of change in culture
3. Between change as an outside event and transition as inner adjustment
4. Between innovators who adopt early and laggards who adopt last
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. William Bridges separates change, the external event such as a new schedule, from transition, the internal psychological process of adjusting to it. Transition runs through an ending, a neutral zone of confusion, and a new beginning. The chapter calls it the model that takes loss seriously, answering "how are people feeling, and what do they need now?" Short-term wins and anchoring are Kotter's steps, and innovators and laggards come from Rogers.

    **Concept Tested:** Organizational Change

    **See:** [The Models at a Glance](index.md#the-models-at-a-glance)

#### 6. In a new reading program, the share of questions answered correctly falls from 72 percent at baseline to 66 percent by month 3, then climbs back to 73 percent by month 6. At month 3, a board member proposes ending the program. What does the chapter's reasoning support?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. End the program, since any drop below baseline shows the change has failed
2. Replace the teachers, since a dip shows they lack the needed skills
3. Restart the program from the beginning with a different vendor and plan
4. Continue, since a temporary dip while people learn new skills is normal
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Fullan's implementation dip is the temporary drop in performance and confidence when people begin using an innovation that requires new skills. It is the price of learning, not a sign of failure, and a leader who abandons the effort at the first low result stops just before the payoff. The pattern here, falling early and recovering past baseline, is the ordinary shape. The chapter adds that a pilot far outside the normal range deserves investigation, not reflexive abandonment.

    **Concept Tested:** Implementation Dip

    **See:** [Worked Example: A Dip in the Data](index.md#worked-example-a-dip-in-the-data)

#### 7. A principal wants to know where each teacher is in adopting a new practice, from "What is this?" to "How can we do better together?", and what each needs next. Which model was built for that question?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The Concerns-Based Adoption Model
2. Kotter's eight steps
3. Lewin's three stages
4. Senge's learning organization
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. CBAM, developed by Gene Hall, Shirley Hord, and colleagues in the 1970s, was built specifically to track how teachers adopt new practices. It looks at the concerns a teacher holds, which move from "What is this?" toward "How can we do better together?", and at the teacher's level of use, from not using to refined use. Kotter gives leaders an ordered sequence, Lewin describes the phases of any change, and Senge describes an organization that keeps learning.

    **Concept Tested:** Concerns-Based Adoption Model

    **See:** [Fullan, the Implementation Dip, and CBAM](index.md#fullan-the-implementation-dip-and-cbam)

#### 8. Why did Peter Senge call systems thinking "the fifth discipline"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Because it was the last discipline he discovered in his research
2. Because it integrates the other four disciplines into a whole
3. Because it is the easiest discipline for new teams to learn
4. Because it replaces personal mastery once a team is formed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Senge's five disciplines are personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning, and systems thinking. Systems thinking means seeing the whole system and the connections among its parts rather than isolated events, and Senge called it the fifth discipline because it integrates the others. Together they build a learning organization, one that continually expands its capacity to create the results it wants instead of running one initiative after another.

    **Concept Tested:** Senge's Five Disciplines

    **See:** [Learning Organizations](index.md#learning-organizations)

#### 9. Two schools adopt the same Level 2 textbook with identical training. After a semester, most teachers at School A use it, but only about one in five at School B do. Teachers at School A share plans and observe each other; teachers at School B work alone behind closed doors. What best explains the difference?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. School B's teachers received weaker training than School A's teachers
2. School culture, since shared norms decide whether a reform spreads
3. The textbook was better suited to School A's students than to School B's
4. School B's principal failed to follow Kotter's eight steps in order
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The software and the training were identical, so the difference was culture, "the way we do things here." At School A, teachers swapped tips and use spread in weeks; at School B, each teacher decided privately and use stayed with the first few adopters. Researchers such as Deal and Peterson find that school culture strongly shapes whether reforms take hold. The scenario rules out training differences, and nothing indicates the students or Kotter's sequence differed.

    **Concept Tested:** School Culture

    **See:** [Worked Example: Culture in Two Schools](index.md#worked-example-culture-in-two-schools)

#### 10. A principal is drafting a letter to families before a pilot begins. Which approach best reflects the chapter's guidance on managing expectations?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Promise that scores will rise this semester, to build excitement for the pilot
2. Say nothing about results, so that families are not disappointed later
3. Describe only the technology, since families care most about the tools
4. Say scores may dip for two to three months before recovering, and report monthly
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Managing expectations means setting, and resetting, what people believe a change will deliver and how soon. A leader who promises quick gains sets up a collision between the promise and the implementation dip, and trust falls at the first low result. A letter that predicts the dip and commits to regular reports makes the dip part of the plan, so when it arrives families see the plan working. Silence avoids the problem only until results appear.

    **Concept Tested:** Managing Expectations

    **See:** [Worked Example: Two Letters to Families](index.md#worked-example-two-letters-to-families)
