## What Organizational Change Is

**Organizational change** is the process by which an organization moves from its current way of working to a different one, whether in structure, technology, processes, people, or culture. In a school, moving from a six-period day to a morning-and-afternoon design, or adopting a Level 3 textbook, is organizational change, since it alters what many people do each day, and not only what one person does.

The research on change makes three points that apply to every model below. Change is *planned and emergent*: leaders set a direction, and the people doing the work reshape it as they go. Change is *emotional*: it brings loss, uncertainty, and anxiety alongside opportunity. And change is *slow*: culture and habits, not technology, set the pace. You will often see the claim that "70 percent of change efforts fail," repeated in books and talks. The figure is poorly sourced, and a 2011 review by Mark Hughes found no solid evidence for it. What the research does support is that many large change efforts fall short of their goals, and that the usual reasons are about people, not technology.

### Worked Example: Is This Change Organizational?

| Change | Organizational change? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One teacher tries a new simulation in her own class | No (an individual practice change) | Only one person's work changes |
| Six teachers in one school pilot the Level 2 textbook | Small | A group's routines change |
| The district adopts Level 3 textbooks and changes the schedule | Yes | Structures, roles, schedules, and culture all change |
| The district hires a new superintendent | Not by itself | A change of person is not a change of how work is done |

The last row is worth remembering. Replacing a leader is easy to do and rarely changes an organization unless the way of working changes.

## The Models at a Glance

Eight models and ideas do most of the work in education change. Each is introduced here with its author, its central idea, and the question it answers best.

**Kotter's eight steps** is a model of leading large-scale change, from John Kotter of Harvard Business School, first published in 1995 and expanded in his 1996 book *Leading Change*. It gives eight steps in order: establish a sense of urgency, form a guiding coalition, create a vision, communicate the vision, remove obstacles, create short-term wins, build on the change, and anchor the changes in the culture. It answers the question *what should leaders do, and in what order?* Chapters 17 and 18 take the steps in two halves.

**Lewin's change model**, from the psychologist Kurt Lewin in 1947, describes change as three stages: *unfreeze* the current way of doing things, *change* to the new way, and *refreeze* so that the new way becomes stable. It answers *what are the basic phases of any change?* and is the oldest and simplest model on the list. It also gave the field the idea of forces for and against change.

**The ADKAR model**, from Jeff Hiatt of the firm Prosci, published in 2006, is a model of change at the level of the *individual*. It has five elements, each of which a person needs in order to change: Awareness of the need, Desire to take part, Knowledge of how to change, Ability to do it, and Reinforcement to sustain it. It answers *where is this person stuck?* and is useful because it points at a specific missing element.

**Bridges' transition model**, from William Bridges in 1991, separates *change* (the external event, such as a new schedule) from *transition* (the internal, psychological process of adjusting to it). Transition runs through three phases: an ending, a neutral zone of confusion, and a new beginning. It answers *how are people feeling, and what do they need now?* and it is the model that takes loss seriously.

**Schein's culture model**, from Edgar Schein of MIT, describes the culture of an organization at three levels: the visible *artifacts*, the stated *values*, and the unseen *basic assumptions* beneath them. It answers *why does the organization resist what it says it wants?* and explains why change efforts that alter only the visible surface fail.

**Senge's learning organization**, from Peter Senge's 1990 book *The Fifth Discipline*, describes an organization that continually expands its capacity to create the results it wants, built from five disciplines. It answers *how do we become a place that keeps improving, instead of running one change after another?*

**Fullan's educational change**, from Michael Fullan, a Canadian researcher of school reform, applies change research specifically to schools. It stresses that change succeeds through a school's people and culture, that the early period of any change brings a dip in performance, and that coherence across a system matters. It answers *what is different about changing a school?*

**The Concerns-Based Adoption Model** (CBAM), developed by Gene Hall, Shirley Hord, and colleagues at the University of Texas in the 1970s, was built specifically to track how *teachers* adopt new practices. It examines three things: the *concerns* a teacher has about the change, how the teacher actually *uses* the innovation, and what the innovation looks like in practice. It answers *where is each teacher, and what do they need next?*

## Culture: Organizational and School

**Organizational culture** is the set of shared beliefs, values, assumptions, and habits of behavior that characterize an organization, often summarized as "the way we do things here." It is mostly invisible to the people inside it, because it feels like common sense. A new employee notices it quickly, since it is the unwritten rules: how decisions are really made, who may disagree with whom, and what is rewarded.

**School culture** is the organizational culture of a school: its shared beliefs and norms about teaching and learning, its rituals and routines, its expectations of students and staff, and the way adults treat each other. Researchers such as Terrence Deal and Kent Peterson have found that a school's culture strongly shapes whether reforms take hold. It is the deeper form of what Tyack and Cuban called the grammar of schooling in Chapter 1: the age-graded classroom, the bell schedule, and the closed classroom door are cultural as well as structural. A school whose culture holds that teaching is a private act will resist collaborative afternoon teams however well they are designed.

### Worked Example: Culture in Two Schools

Two schools adopt the same Level 2 textbook with the same training.

| | School A | School B |
|---|---|---|
| Staff norm | Teachers share lesson plans and observe each other | Teachers work alone; closed doors are respected |
| What happens to the new textbook | Teachers swap tips; use spreads in weeks | Each teacher decides privately; use stays with the first few adopters |
| After a semester | Most teachers use it | About one in five use it |

The software and the training were identical. The difference was culture, which is why the books on change spend so much time on it, and why Chapter 17 takes up Schein's model.

## Learning Organizations

A **learning organization** is an organization that continually expands its capacity to create the results it truly wants, in which people keep learning how to learn together. Peter Senge popularized the idea. It is the opposite of a school that runs one change initiative after another, none lasting, because nobody learns from the last one.

**Senge's five disciplines** are the practices that build a learning organization:

1. **Personal mastery:** each person's commitment to continual growth and to what they truly care about.
2. **Mental models:** surfacing and questioning the deeply held assumptions that shape how we see things.
3. **Shared vision:** building a picture of the future that people truly want, instead of one imposed on them.
4. **Team learning:** groups that think and learn together, with dialogue and not debate.
5. **Systems thinking:** seeing the whole system and the connections among its parts, rather than isolated events. Senge called it the fifth discipline because it integrates the others.

The learning organization is the destination of the change models: the point is not a single successful change but a school that handles change well. Chapter 18 treats systems thinking in depth.

## Fullan, the Implementation Dip, and CBAM

**Fullan's educational change** is the body of work by Michael Fullan on how change really happens in schools. Three of his ideas recur in this book. Change succeeds through *people*: the knowledge, skill, and commitment of the teachers who carry it out, not through plans on paper. Change takes *time*: several years, and not a school year. And change needs *coherence*: the many initiatives in a school and district have to fit together, since a school asked to do twelve unrelated things will do none of them well.

The **implementation dip** is the temporary drop in performance and confidence that happens when people begin to use an innovation that requires new skills and new understanding. Fullan described it from many school reform efforts: before the new practice becomes fluent, people are worse at it than they were at the old one. The dip is not a sign of failure. It is the price of learning a skill, and a leader who panics at the first low result and abandons the effort stops just before the payoff. The *absence* of a dip can itself be a warning, because it may mean that nothing really changed.

### Worked Example: A Dip in the Data

Lincoln Middle tracks the share of unit-test questions that students answer correctly in the pilot classes. These numbers are illustrative.

| Month | Share correct |
|---|---|
| 0 (baseline) | 70% |
| 1 | 68% |
| 2 | 64% |
| 3 | 63% |
| 4 | 65% |
| 5 | 68% |
| 6 | 71% |
| 9 | 76% |
| 12 | 78% |

Performance falls from 70 to 63 percent by month 3, a drop of 7 points, or 10 percent of the baseline, then recovers past the baseline at month 6 and reaches 78 percent by month 12. A leader who looked only at months 1 to 3 would call the pilot a failure. One who knows about the implementation dip sees an ordinary shape and prepares teachers and families for it in advance.

**The Concerns-Based Adoption Model** is the model that lets a leader see where each teacher is. It was created because early studies showed that schools often assumed teachers had adopted a new practice when many had barely begun. CBAM looks at the *concerns* a teacher holds, which move from "What is this?" and "How will this affect me?" toward "How is it affecting students?" and "How can we do better together?" It also looks at the teacher's *level of use*, from not using the innovation at all, through awkward mechanical use, to routine and refined use. Chapters 17 and 19 apply it in detail.

**The ADKAR model** gives the matching view of the *individual*: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement. Taken in order, they say that a person who does not know why the change is needed (no awareness) will not want it (no desire), and a person who wants it but does not know how (no knowledge) cannot do it (no ability), and without reinforcement will slide back.

### Worked Example: Two Teachers, Two Diagnoses

Ms. Rivera and a colleague, Mr. Hale, both are struggling with the new textbook.

| | Ms. Rivera | Mr. Hale |
|---|---|---|
| What they say | "I want to use it, but I can't get the students' logins to work" | "I don't see why we're changing; the old book worked" |

The same symptom, "not using it," has two different causes. A leader who gives both teachers more training treats one and ignores the other.

## Managing Expectations

**Managing expectations** is the practice of setting, and repeatedly resetting, what people believe the change will deliver and how soon. It draws together three earlier ideas: the hype cycle of Chapter 15, which shows that early expectations run ahead of results; the implementation dip, which shows that results fall before they rise; and the realistic expectations of Chapter 6, which says to describe what each level of textbook can and cannot do.

A leader who promises that the new model will be easy and quick sets the school up for disappointment at the first low result, because the promise and the dip collide. A leader who says in advance, "We expect a dip in the first few months, here is what it will look like, and here is how we will know it is normal," makes the dip part of the plan. This is partly a matter of honesty and partly of trust, since a leader whose predictions come true is believed the next time.

### Worked Example: Two Letters to Families

Ms. Okafor drafts two versions of the letter to parents before the pilot begins.

| | Draft 1 | Draft 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Promise | "Your child's math scores will go up this semester." | "We expect scores to dip slightly in the first two to three months as students and teachers learn the new approach, then recover. We will report every month." |
| What happens at month 3 | Scores are down; families feel misled; trust falls | Scores are down as predicted; families see the plan is working |
| Effect on the pilot | Pressure to stop early | Support to continue |

She sends Draft 2. It is less exciting and it is far more likely to survive month 3.

## Putting the Map to Use

Ms. Okafor now has a toolbox. For the leaders' sequence she uses Kotter's eight steps. For the teacher who does not want the change, she uses ADKAR. For feelings of loss among veterans, Bridges, in Chapter 17. For the culture of closed doors, Schein. For the long-term aim of a school that keeps learning, Senge. For the first-term dip, Fullan. For tracking where each teacher is, CBAM. She does not apply all of them at once, and she knows which question each answers.

The story has both sides. The benefit: decades of research on change, in a form a busy principal can use. The risks: model overload, applying a model's vocabulary without its substance, treating a framework as a guarantee, and forgetting that these models describe people, who surprise. The response is to start with the question and choose the model that fits it, to expect a dip, and to keep checking with the teachers themselves.

## Key Takeaways

- **Organizational change** alters how many people work; replacing a leader is not by itself a change, and the "70 percent of change efforts fail" figure is poorly sourced.
- **Kotter's eight steps** give leaders an ordered plan; **Lewin** gives three phases (unfreeze, change, refreeze); **ADKAR** diagnoses the individual; **Bridges** addresses feelings of loss; **Schein** reveals culture; **Senge** aims at a learning organization; **Fullan** speaks to schools; **CBAM** tracks teachers.
- **Organizational culture** is "the way we do things here," and **school culture** decides whether a reform takes hold: identical training produced very different results in two schools.
- A **learning organization** builds **Senge's five disciplines**: personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning, and systems thinking.
- The **implementation dip** is a temporary fall in performance as people learn a new practice; a normal dip bottoms early and recovers, and the absence of one may mean shallow change.
- **Managing expectations** means saying in advance what the dip will look like, which makes it part of the plan and protects trust.
- Match the model to the question; the models are tools for different jobs, not rivals.
