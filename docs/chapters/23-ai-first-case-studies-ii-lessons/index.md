## When Technology Came First

### The Los Angeles iPad Rollout

The **LAUSD iPad rollout** began in 2013, when the Los Angeles Unified School District started a \$1.3 billion plan to give every student an iPad loaded with curriculum from Pearson. The plan was cancelled amid technical problems, weak curriculum, and a scandal over how the contract was awarded. The lesson is that buying devices before having a plan for teaching wastes money and public trust. It is also an edtech hype wave (Chapter 15) in miniature: an announcement at the peak of expectations, and a cancellation in the trough.

### The Los Angeles "Ed" Chatbot and the AllHere Collapse

A decade later, the same district made a different mistake. The **LAUSD Ed chatbot** was an AI assistant for students and families, built by a startup called AllHere. The district committed about \$6 million to the company and spent about \$3 million before the **AllHere collapse**: the company folded within months of the chatbot's launch. In November 2024, its founder was charged with defrauding investors of nearly \$10 million. In 2026, the FBI searched the home and office of the district's superintendent in an investigation reported to be connected to AllHere; as the appendix notes, he has not been charged with a crime. The case is still developing, and the facts should be checked against current reporting.

What is clear from the facts already established is the pattern of **rushed procurement risk** and **vendor risk** from Chapters 10 and 21: a large commitment to an unproven company, student data sent to a vendor that did not survive, and no check on the vendor's financial health. The lesson the appendix draws is that schools need procurement rules before they buy AI products, such as those based on the five-level model.

### AltSchool

**AltSchool** was a network of "personalized learning" microschools in the San Francisco area and elsewhere, launched in 2013 and run on its own software. It raised more than \$170 million, closed its schools within a few years, and shifted to selling its software to other schools. Its lesson is that personalization led by technology, without a lasting school culture and community, does not last. It is the **Software Is Not the Model** principle of Chapter 22 from another direction: even a well-funded team with its own software could not make the model work without the culture around it.

### Summit Learning and the Student Walkouts

Chapter 22 introduced Summit Learning. The **student walkouts** are the part of that story most relevant to practice: in 2018 and 2019, students in Brooklyn walked out of school and families in Kansas protested, complaining about long hours on screens and too little contact with teachers. The lesson for a school leader is about **student voice** (Chapter 19): the students themselves told the district, loudly, what was not working, and a school that had asked them earlier would have heard it earlier.

### inBloom and the PowerSchool Data Breach

Two cases concern data (Chapter 10). **inBloom** was a \$100 million project, funded by the Gates Foundation, to store and share student data across states. It closed in 2014 after parents objected on privacy grounds. Its lesson is that any xAPI and Learning Record Store system needs the trust of families from the start, with clear rules about who owns and controls the data. The **PowerSchool data breach** came in December 2024, when a breach at one of the largest student information system vendors exposed the records of tens of millions of students and teachers. It makes the Level 3 privacy threshold concrete: the more centralized the records, the bigger the target.

### The Stanford Detector Study

The **Stanford detector study**, by Weixin Liang and colleagues in 2023, found that popular AI-writing detectors often wrongly flagged essays by non-native English writers as written by AI. It is the evidence behind Chapter 20's advice to redesign assignments and not to rely on detection, since fighting AI with detection can create its own equity problem.

### Why School Reforms Fail

**Why school reforms fail** is the question that the failures above answer together. Looking across them, four causes recur. *Technology came before pedagogy*: the iPad rollout and AltSchool began with the tool, and not with the learning. *The grammar of schooling and the culture absorbed the change*: a reform that did not alter the work of teaching left the structure in place (Chapter 18). *Trust was not built*: inBloom and Summit lost the families and students on whom they depended. And *the hype outran the evidence*: the announcements at the peak of expectations were followed by the trough (Chapter 15). The same four causes are the reason that the book's answer to each is a practice, from culture assessment to student voice to pilots, and not a technology.

Mostly the technology worked as designed; what failed was planning, trust, procurement, culture, and listening. Better AI will not fix those, and the book's checklists will.

## Cases That Worked and Cases With Both Sides

The positive and both-sided cases show the other half of the pattern, and each has its own limits.

The **Nigeria AI tutoring pilot** was a six-week after-school program in Edo State, Nigeria, in 2024, in which students used a GPT-4-based tutor to practice English with teachers in the room. The World Bank team reported large learning gains for a very small cost per student. The lesson is that low-cost AI tutoring with a teacher present can help where resources are scarce, and the caution is that it is a short pilot in one setting.

**Tutor CoPilot**, a Stanford study from 2024, gave human tutors live AI suggestions during their sessions instead of replacing them. Students' mastery improved, the weakest tutors improved the most, and the cost per tutor for a year was small. The lesson is that AI works well when it makes people better at helping students, which supports the AI-coaching-teachers idea of Chapter 21.

The **Estonia AI Leap**, a 2025 national program, gave secondary students and teachers access to AI learning tools along with teacher training, building on the country's earlier success bringing computers into schools. The lesson is that a school system can adopt AI on purpose, with a plan, training, and public support, though the results are not yet known (Chapter 18).

**High Tech High**, the San Diego network of project-based charter schools (Chapter 14), shows the pro-social afternoon working as a whole school's design, with driving questions and public exhibitions. **Finland's phenomenon learning** is the national requirement for multidisciplinary, phenomenon-based modules. **El Sistema**, the Venezuelan youth orchestra program, is the model of group music-making. Together the three are the proof, the appendix says, that the afternoon half of the AI-native day is already proven. Each has detractors and uneven evidence, as Chapter 14 noted.

The both-sided cases carry a benefit and a risk in one story. **David Game College**, in London, opened in 2024 what was described as the UK's first GCSE class taught mainly by AI, with learning coaches in place of traditional teachers; it shows the idea spreading, and the same open question as Alpha, which is how much of the result comes from the software and how much from the adults. **Khanmigo**, Khan Academy's AI tutor, built to guide students with questions and not answers, has been rolled out in many districts since 2023; early versions made math mistakes, and students in some districts used it less than expected, a reminder that even careful design meets the reality of classroom use. **Your Brain on ChatGPT**, a 2025 MIT Media Lab study, found that students who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed lower brain engagement in EEG readings and remembered less of what they wrote; it was small and is still a preprint, so it supports the risk of handing thinking over without proving its size.

## Evaluating Result Claims

**Evaluating result claims** is the habit of testing any statement about what a school, program, or product achieved before believing it or repeating it. It draws together Chapters 2 and 3: the evidence levels, effect sizes, self-reported versus independent results, and preprints. Six questions do most of the work.

| Question | What it exposes |
|---|---|
| Where did the students start? | A result with no baseline says nothing about growth |
| Compared with whom? | Without a comparison group, a gain may be ordinary growth |
| Who measured it, and who paid? | Self-reported results are produced by someone with a stake |
| Has anyone outside checked it? | Peer review or independent evaluation raises trust |
| How big, and for how long? | A tiny or very short study may not hold up |
| What was actually measured? | Satisfaction or usage is not learning |

Apply them to the claims in the cases. Unbound Academy's reported proficiency of 10 percent in math came with no starting point, so it says nothing about how much students grew. Its claim that students learn 2.8 times faster is measured on its own growth tests, which are self-reported. The Nigerian pilot and the Harvard study are randomized, which answers "compared with whom," though each has a short duration and a narrow setting. The MIT study answers "has anyone outside checked it" with "not yet," since it is a preprint with a small sample.

## The Affordable Alpha Model

The book's capstone design question is the one Alpha's tuition poses: can the structure of a short adaptive morning and a people-centered afternoon be offered to every student at a public school's budget? The **affordable Alpha model** is this book's answer, the design that has been built up over the chapters: open Level 2 and Level 3 textbooks and MicroSims instead of costly live AI for every student (Chapters 6 to 9), selective use of a guarded chatbot (Chapter 12), the structured afternoon (Chapter 14), and a governed pilot (Chapters 20 and 21). It keeps what Unbound lacked, the in-person afternoon with adults, and avoids what makes Alpha unaffordable, tuition-level pricing for software and staffing.

## Stories That Teach: Two-Sided Stories and Graphic Novel Scenes

Chapter 22 distinguished real cases from composite and illustrated stories. Two forms matter for practice.

A **two-sided story** is a short composite story that shows, in one narrative, a benefit growing stronger and a risk being reduced, with the school's response. It is the course description's capstone: a story of about 1,500 words about one day in an AI-native school, with a one-page commentary that links it to the risk-and-response framework. It is useful because it makes the framework concrete for families and staff who will not read a table. A **graphic novel scene** is an illustrated story told in panels, which suits the visual learner, a short scene such as a student, a tutor, and a teacher working through a moment.

### Worked Example: A Two-Sided Story in Miniature

*Maya and the Hint.* On Tuesday morning, Maya is stuck on a unit-rate problem and asks the tutor for the answer. The tutor, which gives hints first, asks her what the problem is asking. Annoyed, she tries twice, then gets a hint about which number to divide, and solves it. (The benefit: her own pace and a patient tutor, available when no teacher is.) That afternoon, Ms. Rivera reads the weekly log and sees that on Friday the tutor, because of a configuration slip, gave a student the answer on the first request. (The risk: an answer-giving tutor.) She flags it, the vendor fixes it within a day, and the school adds a check of the tutor's first reply to its weekly routine. (The response.) On Friday Maya presents the problem she solved to her team. (The positive strengthened.)

The four parts, benefit, risk, response, and strengthened positive, are the whole structure. A graphic novel scene would show the same moment as six panels: Maya frowning at the screen, the tutor's question, her second try, the hint, her "oh!", and the team presentation.

## The Recommendation to the Board

A **school board recommendation** is the document and presentation in which a leader asks the board to take a decision, here whether to approve the pilot, the contract, or the scale-up. It is the book's last practical task. A good recommendation has seven parts:

1. **The recommendation itself,** stated in one sentence, such as "Approve a one-year pilot of the model in two schools, with a written decision rule for scaling."
2. **The case,** in three or four sentences: the problem (learning gaps, Chapter 1), the opportunity, and the vision.
3. **The evidence,** with its level: what is strong (randomized tutoring studies), what is early or self-reported (whole-school claims), and what is unknown (Chapter 2's questions).
4. **The risks and responses,** in the framework's form, using the pilot risk checklist.
5. **The equity analysis:** who benefits, who might be left behind, and how gaps will be monitored (Chapter 21).
6. **The cost,** with the total cost of ownership and where the money comes from (Chapters 7 and 21).
7. **The decision rule and timeline:** what will be measured, when the board will see results, and what would make the district stop, extend, or scale (Chapter 20).

## What the Whole Book Comes To

The book began with a prediction: that within three to four years almost every student could have access to a free, high-quality, interactive, adaptive textbook, and that schools would then have to decide what to do with the time that frees. Its answer was *mornings with AI, afternoons with each other*. Along the way it has said, each time, what the evidence supports and what it does not.

The cases of these last two chapters carry the same two-sided message. The model's components have support: adaptive pacing, mastery learning, well-designed AI tutors, project-based afternoons, and the open, low-cost Level 2 and Level 3 textbooks that make it affordable. The package as a whole is still being tested, the cautionary cases show how it fails when technology comes before people, and nothing in the evidence guarantees success. The book's goal, in the words of the course description, is to *strengthen the positives and reduce the negatives*, and the way to do that is the one this chapter has practiced: read every story for its benefit and its risk, ask the six questions of every claim, borrow the lessons of the failures, pilot before scaling, and tell families and boards the truth.

## Key Takeaways

- The failures mostly show planning, trust, culture, and procurement failing, not technology: the **LAUSD iPad rollout** (devices before a teaching plan), the **LAUSD Ed chatbot** and **AllHere collapse** (an unchecked vendor), **AltSchool** (no lasting culture), **Student walkouts** at Summit Learning (listen to students), **inBloom** (family trust), the **PowerSchool data breach** (centralized records), and the **Stanford detector study** (redesign, not policing).
- **Why school reforms fail:** technology before pedagogy, the culture absorbing the change, trust not built, and hype outrunning evidence.
- The **Nigeria AI tutoring pilot**, **Tutor CoPilot**, **Estonia's AI Leap**, **High Tech High**, **Finland's phenomenon learning**, and **El Sistema** show what can work; **David Game College**, **Khanmigo**, and **Your Brain on ChatGPT** carry a benefit and a risk together.
- **Evaluating result claims** uses six questions: where did students start, compared with whom, who measured it, has anyone checked it, how big and how long, and what was measured.
- The **affordable Alpha model** adds about \$64 per student per year net of print savings in our illustration, against Alpha's \$40,000 to \$65,000 tuition, with the afternoon staffing as the main cost and the full package still unproven.
- A **two-sided story** and a **graphic novel scene** make the framework concrete; a **school board recommendation** states the ask, the case, the evidence with its level, the risks and responses, the equity analysis, the cost, and the decision rule.
