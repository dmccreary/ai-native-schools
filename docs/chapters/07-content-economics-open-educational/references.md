# References: Content Economics and Open Educational Resources

1. [Open educational resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_educational_resources) - Wikipedia - Defines open educational resources, traces their history from MIT OpenCourseWare onward, and summarizes cost and quality research. It gives readers the shared vocabulary this chapter uses when comparing open and commercial textbooks.

2. [Creative Commons license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_Commons_license) - Wikipedia - Explains the six Creative Commons licenses and what each attribution, share-alike, non-commercial and no-derivatives condition allows. Useful for judging whether an open textbook can legally be adapted for a district.

3. [Textbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textbook) - Wikipedia - Covers the history of the textbook, the publishing industry, state adoption, pricing and the shift to digital formats. It provides background for the chapter's discussion of publisher business models and why content prices stay high.

4. Free Culture: How Big Media Uses Technology and the Law to Lock Down Culture and Control Creativity - Lawrence Lessig - Penguin Press - Lessig, a founder of Creative Commons, argues that copyright defaults block sharing and proposes "some rights reserved" licensing. His argument explains why open licenses, not just free prices, make textbook reuse possible.

5. Disrupting Class: How Disruptive Innovation Will Change the Way the World Learns - Clayton M. Christensen, Michael B. Horn and Curtis W. Johnson - McGraw-Hill - Applies Christensen's disruptive-innovation theory to schooling, showing how cheaper, "good enough" alternatives displace incumbents. The framework clarifies the chapter's argument about content commoditization and publisher disruption.

6. [Definition of Open: The 5R Permissions](https://opencontent.org/definition/) - David Wiley, opencontent.org - The original statement of the 5R permissions: retain, reuse, revise, remix and redistribute. This is the source for the 5R Permissions concept and a practical test for whether a resource is truly open.

7. [About CC Licenses](https://creativecommons.org/share-your-work/cclicenses/) - Creative Commons - The official explanation of each Creative Commons license, its conditions and how to apply it. Teachers and curriculum leaders can use it to check what they may do with an openly licensed textbook or MicroSim.

8. [OpenStax](https://openstax.org/) - Rice University - A nonprofit library of free, peer-reviewed, openly licensed textbooks for high school and college courses. It shows a working example of the free textbook model and the savings that open content can bring to students.

9. [Open Textbook Library](https://open.umn.edu/opentextbooks) - Open Education Network, University of Minnesota - A searchable catalog of open textbooks with faculty reviews that rate quality. Its review process is a useful comparison point for the chapter's OpenBook QR Score and its quality and reusability subscores.

10. [Open Education](https://sparcopen.org/open-education/) - SPARC - SPARC's overview of the open education movement, including policy work, textbook cost advocacy and impact stories. It helps school boards and policymakers see how open content policy is being adopted and funded.
