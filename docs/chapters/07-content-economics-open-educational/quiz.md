# Quiz: Content Economics and Open Educational Resources

Test your understanding of open licenses, the OpenBook QR Score, falling content costs, and per-student cost modeling with these review questions.

---

#### 1. How does the chapter distinguish a free textbook from an open textbook?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Free textbooks are published by nonprofits; open textbooks are published by companies
2. Free textbooks are always printed; open textbooks are always delivered online
3. Free is a statement about price; open is a statement about permissions
4. Free textbooks have been peer reviewed; open textbooks have not yet been reviewed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A free textbook costs students nothing to use. An open textbook carries a license that grants advance permission to retain, reuse, revise, remix, and redistribute it. A PDF posted free with "all rights reserved" is free but not open, because teachers may not edit, translate, or share it. Many open textbooks are also free, which is why the chapter uses "free open textbook" for the combination. Publisher type, format, and peer review do not define either term.

    **Concept Tested:** Free Textbook

    **See:** [Open Educational Resources](index.md#open-educational-resources)

#### 2. Which two of the 5R permissions does a Creative Commons no-derivatives (ND) license withhold?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Revise and remix
2. Retain and reuse
3. Reuse and redistribute
4. Retain and redistribute
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. An ND license allows sharing only unchanged copies, so it withholds revise (adapt, edit, translate) and remix (combine with other materials). Retain and reuse are granted by every Creative Commons license, and ND licenses still allow redistribution of unchanged copies. The chapter warns that NC and ND licenses can block exactly the things teachers most want to do, such as fixing errors and adapting a book for their students.

    **Concept Tested:** 5R Permissions

    **See:** [Worked Example: Which License Grants Which Rights?](index.md#worked-example-which-license-grants-which-rights)

#### 3. Why is the OpenBook QR Score always reported with both subscores, as in "QR 72 (Q 40 / R 32)"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Because the two subscores are weighted differently, so the total must be adjusted
2. Because a single total can hide which half, quality or reusability, is weak
3. Because reviewers disagree about the total but always agree on the subscores
4. Because only the quality subscore counts toward the adoption band
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The score has ten metrics of ten points each, five for quality and five for reusability, and both halves count equally toward the 0 to 100 total. Two books with nearly the same total can be good for different jobs: in the chapter's example, Open Algebra is better to hand students, while FreeMath Complete is better raw material to adapt. A bare total would hide that difference. The subscores are not weighted differently, and the band uses the full total.

    **Concept Tested:** OpenBook QR Score

    **See:** [Worked Example: Comparing Two Open Textbooks](index.md#worked-example-comparing-two-open-textbooks)

#### 4. A reviewer scores an open textbook's quality metrics at 8, 7, 9, 6, and 7, and its reusability metrics at 9, 8, 8, 9, and 8. How should the score be reported?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. QR 79 (Q 42 / R 37), in the Fair band
2. QR 79, in the Excellent band, with no subscores needed
3. QR 37 (Q 37 / R 42), in the Weak band
4. QR 79 (Q 37 / R 42), in the Good band
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The quality subscore is 8 + 7 + 9 + 6 + 7 = 37, the reusability subscore is 9 + 8 + 8 + 9 + 8 = 42, and the total is 79. A total from 75 to 89 falls in the Good band. The first option swaps the subscores and uses the wrong band. The third reports only the quality half as the total. The chapter insists the score always carries both subscores, so dropping them is also wrong.

    **Concept Tested:** Quality Subscore

    **See:** [The OpenBook QR Score: Judging an Open Textbook](index.md#the-openbook-qr-score-judging-an-open-textbook)

#### 5. A district wants to build a course by combining a CC BY textbook with a CC BY-NC set of simulations, and then sell the course to other districts. What does the chapter say about this plan?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The combination is allowed, but the result must be CC BY-NC, so it may not be sold
2. The combination is allowed and may be sold, because CC BY permits anything with credit
3. The combination is not allowed, because CC BY and CC BY-NC are incompatible
4. The combination is allowed only if the district removes the authors' names
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter's compatibility table shows that CC BY combined with CC BY-NC is allowed, but the result must be licensed CC BY-NC, so it may not be sold. The CC BY permission on one part does not remove the non-commercial condition on the other. Removing names violates the attribution element every license requires. A district that plans to sell a course should prefer CC BY and CC BY-SA sources and avoid NC and ND ones.

    **Concept Tested:** Creative Commons License

    **See:** [Worked Example: Can We Combine These?](index.md#worked-example-can-we-combine-these)

#### 6. In the chapter's illustration, the AI generation cost of a chapter falls a thousandfold, yet the total cost per chapter drops by less than 2 percent. Why?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Because hosting costs rise as more students read the chapter
2. Because AI providers raise prices for educational customers each year
3. Because human review time dominates the cost and does not fall with AI prices
4. Because the chapter must be reprinted on paper for students without devices
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Generation cost falls from \$20 to a few cents, but review stays at about \$1,100 per chapter, so the total barely moves. The cost floor is human time. The chapter says the real prospect for further savings is better tools that cut review hours, and warns that skipping review moves the cost into errors that students learn. The chapter shows hosting costs are tiny and makes no claim that AI prices rise.

    **Concept Tested:** Falling Production Cost

    **See:** [Worked Example: The Floor Under Falling Costs](index.md#worked-example-the-floor-under-falling-costs)

#### 7. A publisher proposes \$500,000 a year: \$150,000 for a digital textbook license, \$200,000 for an adaptive platform, \$100,000 for teacher training, and \$50,000 for support and data protection. Following the chapter's method, what should the district do next?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Reject the proposal, since open textbooks make every line item unnecessary
2. Accept the proposal, since most of the cost is not for content and cannot be replaced
3. Ask the publisher to cut the license price until it matches the other line items
4. Note that 30 percent pays for content with open equivalents; weigh the other services
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Only the \$150,000 license, 30 percent, pays for content that has an open equivalent. The rest pays for value beyond content: platform, training, and support. The chapter's method, as in Mr. Patel's \$900,000 breakdown, is to ask which of those services the district needs, which it can get elsewhere or build itself, and which are worth the price. Rejecting everything ignores real needs like training and data protection, while accepting everything skips the comparison.

    **Concept Tested:** Value Beyond Content

    **See:** [Worked Example: What Is the \$900,000 Paying For?](index.md#worked-example-what-is-the-900000-paying-for)

#### 8. Why does the chapter say Mr. Patel's "cost per student, all in" question cannot be answered by comparing software prices alone?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Because software prices change too often to be compared year to year
2. Because if devices must be bought, they are the largest cost at every digital level
3. Because print textbooks are cheaper than every digital level once training is counted
4. Because software vendors refuse to quote prices per student
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Total cost of ownership includes devices, training, support, and data protection, not just the software. In the chapter's model, a \$75-a-year device share is about 77 to 93 percent of the total at each digital level when devices must be purchased. A school that already owns devices gets Level 2 and Level 3 at a small fraction of the print price; a school that must buy them sees the gap close. Print is not cheaper than every digital level when devices are already owned.

    **Concept Tested:** Total Cost of Ownership

    **See:** [Worked Example: The Table Mr. Patel Asked For](index.md#worked-example-the-table-mr-patel-asked-for)

#### 9. What does content commoditization mean in this chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Publishers merging so that fewer companies control more of the textbook market
2. Schools buying content in bulk to receive a lower price per student
3. A product becoming interchangeable so its price falls toward production cost
4. Teachers selling lesson plans they wrote to other teachers through online stores
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Content commoditization is the process by which a product becomes interchangeable with competitors and its price falls toward its cost of production, because buyers can no longer tell it apart on quality. When AI makes content cheap to produce and open licenses make it free to copy, Level 1 and Level 2 content tends toward a commodity. Serving one more student a digital page costs about a quarter of a cent in the chapter's example. Bulk discounts and mergers are different market effects.

    **Concept Tested:** Content Commoditization

    **See:** [Content Commoditization and Publisher Disruption](index.md#content-commoditization-and-publisher-disruption)

#### 10. A superintendent tells families that the district's new interactive program will cost "a penny a day per student." Using the chapter's cost model, what is the main problem with this statement?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. It covers only content and hosting; a full program costs 3 to 13 cents before devices
2. It is far too high, since content now costs nothing at all to produce or review
3. It applies only to Level 4 chatbot textbooks, not to interactive textbooks
4. It is the cost of devices alone, so content and hosting would be extra
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The penny-per-day vision, about \$1.80 per student per year, covers content and hosting. Adding training, records, governance, and chat brings a full program to about 3 to 13 cents a day before devices, and a new device adds about 42 cents a day. The chapter warns that a district promising a penny a day and delivering a bill of 40 cents will lose trust. Content is not free to produce, because human review costs remain.

    **Concept Tested:** Penny-Per-Day Vision

    **See:** [Worked Example: The Penny and the Real Price](index.md#worked-example-the-penny-and-the-real-price)
