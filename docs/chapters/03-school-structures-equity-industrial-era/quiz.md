# Quiz: School Structures, Equity, and the Industrial-Era Legacy

Test your understanding of why schools are organized the way they are, how equity gaps arise, and how to weigh the quality of evidence with these review questions.

---

#### 1. How did the Carnegie Foundation define the Carnegie unit in 1906?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. A passing score on a standardized end-of-course exam in one subject
2. A set of learning outcomes that a student must master to earn a credit
3. One semester of attendance in any course approved by the district
4. 120 hours of class time in one subject over a school year
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Carnegie unit is a standard measure of a high school course defined as 120 hours of class time in one subject over a school year. It was created to decide pension eligibility and to give colleges a common way to judge transcripts, and most U.S. high schools still award credits this way. It measures time, not mastery, which is why the chapter groups it with seat time as a stand-in for learning rather than a measure of it.

    **Concept Tested:** Carnegie Unit

    **See:** [Carnegie Unit](index.md#carnegie-unit)

#### 2. What is the main weakness of using seat time to measure instruction?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. It is difficult for schools to count and audit accurately
2. A student who already knows the material and a lost student earn the same credit
3. It requires every student to take a standardized test before advancing
4. It gives fast learners more credit than slower learners for the same course
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Seat time assumes a fixed number of hours in class produces a fixed amount of learning. The chapter notes that it is simple to count and audit, so the first option reverses its strength. Its weakness is that it is a poor stand-in for learning: a student who already knows the material and a student who is lost both earn the same seat time. Seat time does not involve testing, and it gives everyone the same credit regardless of speed.

    **Concept Tested:** Seat Time

    **See:** [Seat Time](index.md#seat-time)

#### 3. Which description fits a charter school?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. A publicly funded school run independently of the district under a results contract
2. A privately funded school that charges tuition and answers only to its board of trustees
3. A very small mixed-age school led by one or a few adults in a home or rented space
4. A district school that delivers all of its instruction online to students at home
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A charter school is publicly funded and free to students, but it is run independently of the local district under a charter that sets out its mission and results. Its authorizer can close it if it fails to meet the terms. The second option describes a private school, the third describes a microschool, and the fourth describes a virtual school. The chapter notes some virtual schools are run as charters, but online delivery is not what defines a charter.

    **Concept Tested:** Charter School

    **See:** [Charter School](index.md#charter-school)

#### 4. A school has 45 hours of tutoring for three students who start at different scores. What would an equitable allocation look like, as opposed to an equal one?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Give each student 15 hours so that no one receives more tutoring than anyone else
2. Give all 45 hours to the highest scorer, who is most likely to benefit
3. Give the most hours to the student furthest from the goal, none to one who met it
4. Let each student choose how many hours to use until the 45 hours run out
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Educational equity means matching resources to need so each student can reach the same high standard. In the chapter's example, an equitable share gave 30 hours to the student furthest behind, 15 to the middle student, and none to the student already at the goal, bringing everyone to 80. Giving 15 hours to each is equality, which leaves the gaps between students intact and sends hours to a student who has already reached the goal.

    **Concept Tested:** Educational Equity

    **See:** [Worked Example: Equal Is Not Equitable](index.md#worked-example-equal-is-not-equitable)

#### 5. A middle school tutoring program produces an effect of 0.10 standard deviations over a year. Typical annual growth in middle school is about 0.3 standard deviations. How should a principal describe the program's impact?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. As too small to matter, since anything under 0.2 is negligible
2. As roughly one-third of an additional year of typical growth
3. As about three additional years of typical growth
4. As exactly equal to one additional year of typical growth
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Learning gains can be compared with a typical year of growth by dividing: 0.10 / 0.3 is about one-third, or roughly three months of extra learning. The chapter uses this method to show why researchers treat effects of 0.1 and 0.2 as meaningful, so calling it negligible is the tempting mistake. The three-year answer divides the wrong way. The same effect would be worth much less in early elementary grades, where typical growth is near 1.0.

    **Concept Tested:** Learning Gains

    **See:** [Worked Example: How Big Is a Gain?](index.md#worked-example-how-big-is-a-gain)

#### 6. Why do many researchers prefer to describe achievement gaps in terms of opportunity?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Because test scores no longer show any differences between groups of students
2. Because opportunity is easier to measure than academic performance on tests
3. Because federal law requires schools to report opportunity instead of results
4. Because the word "gap" can suggest the difference lies in the students themselves
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter notes that the word "gap" can mislead by suggesting the difference lies in the students. Most researchers prefer to describe differences in opportunity, such as access to experienced teachers, funding, safe schools, and resources at home, which produce the differences in results. Group differences in scores still exist and have widened in some cases, as the 2022 NAEP results showed, so the first option is false.

    **Concept Tested:** Achievement Gap

    **See:** [Achievement Gap](index.md#achievement-gap)

#### 7. What is a preprint?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. A research paper made public before it has gone through peer review
2. A vendor's summary of results published alongside a product brochure
3. A peer-reviewed article that has been accepted but not yet printed
4. An early draft of a study shared privately with the funding agency
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A preprint is a research paper made publicly available, on servers such as arXiv, SSRN, or EdArXiv, before peer review. Preprints let findings spread quickly in fast-moving fields like AI, but nobody has yet vetted them, so the chapter calls a preprint a lead rather than a verdict. A vendor summary is a self-reported result, and an accepted article has already passed peer review. The chapter notes the book's AI forecasts rest partly on preprints.

    **Concept Tested:** Preprint

    **See:** [Judging the Quality of Evidence](index.md#judging-the-quality-of-evidence)

#### 8. A vendor says its AI tool "saves teachers six hours a week." A teacher's weekly audit shows six hours saved on planning and grading, but four new hours spent checking AI output and reading the dashboard. What conclusion does the chapter's reasoning support?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The vendor's claim is confirmed, because six hours were saved on planning and grading
2. The tool should be dropped, because any added task means it increases workload
3. The net saving is about two hours, and the dashboard is a clear place to improve the tool
4. The audit is unreliable, because teachers cannot track their own hours accurately
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter warns that a time-saving tool can move work rather than remove it, and advises keeping a tool only if total hours go down. Six hours saved minus four added is a net saving of two hours, smaller than the headline but still real. As in Ms. Rivera's audit, the added categories show where the tool can improve. Accepting the gross figure ignores the added tasks, and dropping the tool ignores that the total did fall.

    **Concept Tested:** Teacher Workload

    **See:** [Worked Example: A Weekly Hours Audit](index.md#worked-example-a-weekly-hours-audit)

#### 9. What constraint was the industrial-era school model designed to handle?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. A surplus of teachers who needed to be assigned to specialized subjects
2. Parents' demand for a curriculum shaped around each individual child's interests
3. Federal accountability rules that required annual testing of every student
4. A scarcity of teachers and information as many new children entered school
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The model took shape between about 1840 and 1920, when growing cities and compulsory attendance laws brought in many children who had never been to school. The challenge was to teach hundreds of children with few teachers and few resources, and age grouping, one teacher per room, a standard curriculum, and a bell schedule were an efficient answer. Federal annual testing came much later, with No Child Left Behind in 2002, and the model left little room for individual interests.

    **Concept Tested:** Industrial-Era School Model

    **See:** [The Industrial-Era School Model](index.md#the-industrial-era-school-model)

#### 10. A vendor pays for a study of its own product. Under what condition can the study still count as an independent evaluation?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. If the contract guarantees the results will be published whatever they show
2. If the vendor's own staff collect the data but outside staff write the report
3. If the results are posted as a preprint before the product is sold
4. If the vendor reports the results to the school board in a public meeting
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. An independent evaluation is conducted by researchers with no financial or reputational stake in the results. The chapter says a vendor-paid study can still be independent if the contract guarantees publication regardless of the outcome, which removes the temptation to bury bad news. Vendor staff collecting the data keeps a stakeholder in control of measures and samples. Posting a preprint or presenting to a board does not change who produced the evidence.

    **Concept Tested:** Independent Evaluation

    **See:** [Judging the Quality of Evidence](index.md#judging-the-quality-of-evidence)
