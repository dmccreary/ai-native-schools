## Why Does the Bell Ring?

In the middle of Ms. Rivera's lesson on unit rates, the bell rings. Fifty minutes are up. Half the class has just understood the idea and wants to keep going; the other half is still lost, and the bell does not care. Maya, who has been quietly stuck for twenty minutes, asks the question every student has asked at some point: "Why do we even have bells?"

It is a better question than it sounds. The bell, the fifty-minute period, the grade levels, the credit hours that add up to a diploma: nearly everything about how a school is organized was designed to solve a problem, and most of those problems are a century old. Understanding the problems explains why schools look the way they do. It also shows which features are essential and which are leftovers that technology could change.

## The Industrial-Era School Model

The **industrial-era school model** is the design of schooling that took shape in the United States between roughly 1840 and 1920, when mass production was transforming the economy. It organizes children the way a factory organizes work: students are grouped by age into batches, move together through a fixed sequence of stages, spend set periods on each subject, and are moved on by a signal such as a bell.

The reasons were practical. Cities were growing fast, and compulsory attendance laws brought in children who had never been to school. Reformers such as Horace Mann, who led the Massachusetts Board of Education from 1837, drew on European models, including Prussian ones, though historians debate how much. The challenge was to teach hundreds of children with a small number of teachers and few resources. Grouping by age, one teacher per room, a standard curriculum, and a common schedule were an efficient answer.

The education historian Ellwood Cubberley put the idea bluntly in 1916, describing schools as factories in which raw products, children, were to be shaped and made ready for life according to specifications. The historians David Tyack and Larry Cuban, whose phrase "grammar of schooling" appeared in Chapter 1, showed that this structure then proved extraordinarily durable. Reforms came and went, and the basic grammar stayed.

It is worth being fair to the model. It educated tens of millions of children, raised literacy dramatically, and gave every child a seat. Its weakness is not that it was foolish. It is that it was designed for scarcity of teachers and information, and it treats every child in a batch the same way.

| Feature | The problem it solved | The cost it brought |
|---|---|---|
| Age-based grouping | Easy to assign hundreds of children to classes | Students of different readiness share one lesson |
| One teacher per room | Affordable supervision and instruction | One adult must serve every learner's pace |
| Fixed periods and a bell | Coordinating many classes and teachers | Learning is cut off when time runs out |
| Standard curriculum | Common content for all | Little room for individual interests or pace |
| Time-based credit | A simple way to certify completion | Time spent stands in for what was learned |

### Bell Schedule

A **bell schedule** is the timetable that divides the school day into fixed blocks, announced by a bell or tone, for classes, passing time, lunch, and other activities. A typical middle school has six to eight periods of 45 to 55 minutes. A block schedule uses fewer, longer periods, often 80 to 90 minutes, to reduce transitions.

The bell schedule solves a coordination problem: dozens of teachers and hundreds of students must be in the right rooms at the same time. It is also one of the hardest parts of school to change, since bus routes, lunch service, teacher contracts, and athletics are built around it. Proposals to restructure the day have to begin with the schedule.

### Age-Based Grade Levels

**Age-based grade levels** are the practice of grouping students into yearly grades mainly by birth date, so that all children born within a twelve-month window attend the same grade. The first American school organized this way is usually said to be the Quincy Grammar School in Boston, which opened in 1848. Age grouping is convenient to administer and fair in a rough sense. Its weakness, as Chapter 1 showed with the class of 28 students, is that age is a poor guide to readiness: students in the same grade can differ by several grade levels in what they know.

### Seat Time

**Seat time** is the amount of time a student is physically present in a class, used as the measure of how much instruction has been received. The assumption behind it is that a fixed number of hours in a classroom produces a fixed amount of learning. Seat time is simple to count and audit, and it is also a weak stand-in for learning. A student who already knows the material and a student who is lost both earn the same seat time.

### Carnegie Unit

The **Carnegie unit** is a standard measure of a high school course, defined in 1906 by the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching as 120 hours of class time in one subject over a school year. It was created to decide which colleges and high schools were eligible for pensions and to give colleges a common way to judge a high school transcript. Most U.S. high schools still award credits on this basis, and a typical diploma requires roughly two dozen credits. A 2015 Carnegie Foundation report called it a century-old standard in a changing education landscape.

### Worked Example: What Fixed Time Does to a Class

Suppose a course is set at 150 hours, about one 50-minute period on each of 180 school days, and 20 students need different amounts of time to master it. The table lists the hours each student actually needs. These numbers are illustrative.

| Student group | Hours needed to master | Number of students |
|---|---|---|
| Fast | 70, 80, 90, 95, 100 | 5 |
| Typical | 105, 110, 115, 120, 125, 130, 135, 140, 150 | 9 |
| Slower | 160, 170, 185, 200, 230, 260 | 6 |

Under a fixed 150-hour course, the 14 students who need 150 hours or fewer master the course. The six slower students do not. They get a grade, perhaps a D or F, and the class moves on, leaving a hole in their foundation. Meanwhile the fast and typical students spend hours waiting. The idle hours, counting 150 minus each mastering student's needed hours, come to

\[
80 + 70 + 60 + 55 + 50 + 45 + 40 + 35 + 30 + 25 + 20 + 15 + 10 + 0 = 535 \text{ hours}.
\]

The six slower students would need \( 10 + 20 + 35 + 50 + 80 + 110 = 305 \) more hours between them. If time were allowed to vary and mastery held fixed, every student would master the course, and the total time would be \( 2{,}770 \) hours, less than the \( 20 \times 150 = 3{,}000 \) hours the fixed course uses, with no one left behind. This arithmetic is the whole case for mastery-based approaches, which Chapter 11 develops.

## Education Policy, School Boards, and Standardized Testing

The structures above were not chosen once and left alone. They are kept in place, and sometimes changed, by public decisions.

### Education Policy

**Education policy** is the set of laws, regulations, funding rules, and official decisions that govern how schools operate. In the United States, the Constitution does not mention education, so under the Tenth Amendment it is mainly a state responsibility. States set graduation requirements, teacher licensing, and testing rules, and they delegate daily operations to local school districts. The federal government influences schools mostly through money and civil-rights law.

A few federal milestones shape what schools do today. *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) ruled racial segregation in public schools unconstitutional. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 created Title I funding for schools serving low-income students. The 1975 law that became the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act guaranteed students with disabilities a free, appropriate public education. The No Child Left Behind Act of 2002 required annual testing and accountability for results, and the Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015, which you met in Chapter 2, returned much of that authority to the states.

### School Board

Chapter 2 introduced the **school board** as the elected body that governs a district. Here its role in policy is the point: the board adopts the written policies that decide, for example, the bell schedule's length, the grading rules, and what data may be shared with vendors. Boards meet in public, most states require advance notice and a chance for public comment, and a new technology policy has to survive that process. A leader who wants to change a structure should expect to take the question to a public meeting.

### Standardized Testing

**Standardized testing** is assessment in which every student answers the same or equivalent questions under the same conditions and is scored by the same rules, so results can be compared across students, schools, and years. Large-scale standardized tests grew from early twentieth-century intelligence testing, and the SAT was first administered in 1926. No Child Left Behind made them the center of public school accountability.

Standardized tests are the usual way randomized trials and large studies measure results, and the yardstick is the same for everyone. The cost is that the tests measure only a slice of what schools teach, and tying high stakes to them invites the distortions that Donald Campbell described and Chapter 2 covered. A reasonable stance is to value them as one gauge and to avoid treating them as the whole instrument panel.

## Achievement Gaps, Equity, and Learning Gains

Structures have consequences, and the most important consequences are unequal. This section defines three terms used constantly in discussions of fairness.

### Achievement Gap

An **achievement gap** is a persistent difference in average academic performance between groups of students, such as groups defined by family income, race and ethnicity, language background, or disability. It extends the individual learning gap from Chapter 1 to groups. The Stanford sociologist Sean Reardon found that the gap in test scores between children from high-income and low-income families has grown since the 1970s and is now larger than the gap between Black and white students. The 2022 National Assessment of Educational Progress showed the largest declines in mathematics scores in its history, and the declines were steepest among lower-performing students, widening existing gaps.

The word "gap" can mislead, because it suggests the difference lies in the students. Most researchers prefer to describe differences in *opportunity*, such as access to experienced teachers, funding, safe schools, and resources at home, which produce the differences in results.

### Educational Equity

**Educational equity** is the principle that each student should receive the resources and support they need to reach the same high standards, which means that resources are matched to need rather than split evenly. It is different from *equality*, which gives every student the same thing. If three students start at different points, an equal share of tutoring leaves the gaps between them intact, while an equitable share gives more to the student who has further to go.

Equity has several dimensions: funding, teacher quality, access to courses, discipline practices, and technology at home and in school. The digital divide became visible in 2020, when school closures revealed that millions of students lacked a reliable internet connection or a device. Equity is a recurring theme of this book because the AI-native model could narrow gaps by giving every student a patient tutor, or widen them if only well-resourced schools can afford one.

### Learning Gains

**Learning gains**, also called *growth*, measure how much a student's learning increases over a period, as opposed to *proficiency*, which measures where the student stands at one moment. A school whose students start far behind and catch up quickly may show high gains and still low proficiency, and the reverse can also be true. Gains are usually expressed in standard deviations, the same unit as the effect size from Chapter 2, which lets you compare an intervention with a year of ordinary schooling.

A well-known benchmark study by Carolyn Hill and colleagues in 2008 estimated typical annual gains on standardized tests. They are large in early elementary grades, roughly one standard deviation per year, and smaller later, roughly 0.3 standard deviations per year in the middle school grades and below 0.2 by high school. These are approximate and vary by subject and test.

### Worked Example: How Big Is a Gain?

Suppose a tutoring program in seventh grade produces an effect of \( d = 0.15 \) over the school year, a modest effect by Chapter 2's standards. A typical seventh grader gains about 0.3 standard deviations in a year of ordinary schooling. The program's effect is therefore

\[
\frac{0.15}{0.3} = 0.5
\]

or about half an additional year of typical growth. Measured this way, a "small" effect is worth about four or five additional months of learning, which is why education researchers treat effects of 0.1 and 0.2 as meaningful. The comparison also shows a limit: the same 0.15 in grade 2, where typical annual growth is around 1.0, is only 15 percent of a year.

### Worked Example: Equal Is Not Equitable

A school has 45 hours of tutoring to share among three students. Each hour is assumed to add one point to a student's score, which is a deliberate simplification. The goal is for every student to reach a score of 80.

| Student | Starting score | Equal share (15 hours each): final score | Equitable share: hours | Equitable share: final score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 80 | 95 | 0 | 80 |
| B | 65 | 80 | 15 | 80 |
| C | 50 | 65 | 30 | 80 |

The equal split leaves student C 15 points below the goal and sends 15 hours to student A, who has already reached it. The gap between the highest and lowest scores is \( 95 - 65 = 30 \) points. The equitable split gets everyone to 80, and the gap is zero. Real learning does not work this neatly, as returns vary by student and some needs, like sleep and food, cannot be met with tutoring, but the principle holds: matching resources to need can close gaps that equal shares leave open.

## Alternatives to the Traditional School

Not every school follows the industrial-era model. Three alternatives already enroll large numbers of students, and each takes the pieces of the model apart in a different way.

### Charter School

A **charter school** is a publicly funded school that is run independently of the local school district under a contract, called a charter, that sets out its mission and the results it must achieve. If a charter school fails to meet the terms, its authorizer, which may be a district, a state agency, or a university, can close it. Minnesota passed the first charter school law in 1991. Charter schools now enroll roughly seven percent of U.S. public school students, a bit under four million children.

Charters are free to students, and they have more freedom than district schools over their schedules, curriculum, and hiring. Research on results is mixed, and it depends on the kind of charter. Studies by Stanford's CREDO, which compare charter students with similar students in nearby district schools, have found strong gains for urban charter schools in some cities and weaker or negative results elsewhere. The same research found large negative effects for *full-time online* charter schools in one national study, which leads to the next alternative.

### Virtual School

A **virtual school** delivers most or all instruction over the internet, with students working from home or another location rather than in a classroom. Florida Virtual School, which began in 1997, was among the first state-run programs, and many states now have online options, some run as charter schools. A virtual school is different from the *emergency remote learning* of 2020, when classroom teaching was hastily moved online. A virtual school is designed for online delivery from the start.

Virtual schools offer flexibility for students who cannot attend in person, such as those with health conditions or unusual schedules. The evidence for average students has been weak: the 2015 CREDO study of online charter schools found that students lost the equivalent of about 180 days of learning in mathematics compared with similar students in physical schools, though there is likely a different picture for blended models that pair online work with in-person support. Chapter 22 returns to this point when it examines Unbound Academy, an online charter in Arizona that uses adaptive software without in-person afternoons.

### Microschool

A **microschool** is a very small school, often five to fifteen students of mixed ages, led by one or a few adults in a home, church, or small rented space. The model gained popularity during the pandemic as families formed "learning pods." Some microschools follow a curriculum from a network, such as Acton Academy or Prenda, and some use adaptive software for core academics and adult "guides" for projects. Several states, including Arizona, have begun to fund families directly through education savings accounts that can pay for microschool tuition.

Microschools take the opposite approach to the industrial model: tiny groups, mixed ages, flexible schedules. They are also a small and largely unstudied part of the system, so most claims about them rest on the self-reported results discussed later in this chapter.

## The History of Educational Technology

Alternatives to the school model have always come with new technology, and the technology has often been oversold. **History of EdTech** here means the sequence of tools, from film to teaching machines to computers to AI, that have been introduced into schools with the promise of changing teaching. Chapter 1 sketched the main waves in a table. Here is the longer view.

In 1913, Thomas Edison is reported to have predicted that motion pictures would make textbooks obsolete in schools. In 1924 Sidney Pressey built a machine to automate multiple-choice drill, and in 1954 B. F. Skinner proposed teaching machines that gave immediate feedback. Closed-circuit television in Hagerstown, Maryland, in 1956 tested whether televised lessons could reach many classrooms at once. The University of Illinois launched the PLATO computer-based system in 1960. The Apple II reached schools in 1977. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 created the E-rate program to subsidize school internet access. Khan Academy became a nonprofit in 2008, and 2012 was dubbed the "year of the MOOC," the massive open online course. In 2013, the Los Angeles Unified School District launched an iPad rollout that was soon dogged by problems. School closures in March 2020 moved nearly every school online overnight, and ChatGPT was released on November 30, 2022.

The pattern from Chapter 1, in which technology arrives with enormous promises and is then absorbed into the old grammar of schooling, repeats across this history. What is new about AI is not that the promises are being made; it is that the underlying capability keeps improving, a point Chapters 4 and 5 take up with data.

## The Teacher's Side: Workload and Burnout

Every structure in this chapter lands on teachers, and the people who teach in it carry the weight.

**Teacher workload** is the total of the work a teacher does in a week, including classroom teaching, planning, grading, communicating with families, meetings, and administrative tasks. Teaching time is a minority of it. A 2022 RAND Corporation survey found that teachers reported working about 53 hours a week on average, roughly nine hours more than other working adults. The unseen tasks, planning and grading, make up much of the difference, as the Chapter 1 arithmetic of feedback for 140 students suggested.

**Teacher burnout** is a state of emotional, physical, and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged job stress, marked by exhaustion, cynicism, and a reduced sense of accomplishment. Gallup's 2022 survey found K-12 workers reported the highest rate of burnout of any occupation it studied, with about four in ten saying they felt burned out always or very often. Burnout is a main reason teachers leave, and teacher turnover is expensive for schools and harms students.

AI has a stake in both. A 2025 Gallup survey sponsored by the Walton Family Foundation found that teachers who used AI tools weekly reported saving several hours a week, mostly on planning and administrative tasks. That is a real benefit. The risks are also real: AI-generated materials can contain mistakes teachers have to catch, a new dashboard can add to the monitoring burden rather than reduce it, and a tool imposed without teacher input can feel like one more demand.

!!! mascot-warning "A time-saving tool can still cost time"
    ![Indigo warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Watch for tools that move work rather than remove it, for example a dashboard that tells a teacher about thirty students' needs without telling them what to do. Before adopting anything, ask teachers to track their hours for a week with and without it, and keep the tool only if the total goes down.

## Judging the Quality of Evidence

Chapter 2 taught you to ask how groups in a study were formed. A second set of questions concerns *who produced the evidence and whether anyone checked it*. This book relies on all three kinds of source defined here, so it is worth knowing how to weigh each.

An **independent evaluation** is a study conducted by researchers who did not build, sell, or purchase the product being studied and who have no financial or reputational stake in its results. The independence matters because anyone with a stake, even an honest one, is tempted to make decisions about measures, samples, and reporting that favor a good result. A study paid for by a vendor can still be independent if the contract guarantees that the results will be published whatever they show.

**Self-reported results** are claims about outcomes made by the people who run, build, or sell a program, using data they collected and analyzed themselves. They are not necessarily false, and they may be accurate. But they cannot be checked, and they are produced by someone with an interest. A school reporting that its students grew faster than national norms is a self-report. So is a vendor's white paper.

A **preprint** is a research paper made publicly available before it has gone through peer review, the process in which independent experts evaluate a manuscript before a journal publishes it. Servers such as arXiv, SSRN, and EdArXiv host preprints. Preprints let findings spread quickly, which matters for fast-moving fields like AI. The cost is that nobody has yet vetted them, so the claims may contain errors. Peer review is imperfect too: it catches many problems and misses others. A preprint is a lead to be read carefully, not a verdict.

The AI forecasts and the five-level model of intelligent textbooks in this book rest partly on preprints, which is why you will see Chapters 4 through 6 say so, and weigh them accordingly.

!!! mascot-tip "A quick three-part check"
    ![Indigo giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    For any result, ask three questions: Who ran the study? Did anyone outside check it? Was there a comparison group? A "no" to all three means treat the claim as a lead, and look for something stronger before you act.

### Worked Example: Rating Three Sources

Three documents arrive on Ms. Okafor's desk, all about the same program.

## Answering Maya's Question

Maya asked why the bell rings. Now there is a real answer. The bell exists because the industrial-era school model needed to move hundreds of children through a building on a common schedule. The fifty-minute period, the grade level, and the Carnegie credit all trace back to the same logic: batch the children, fix the time, measure the hours. That logic solved real problems of scale and still does the job of coordinating a large building.

It also has costs, which are the costs of fixed time and fixed pace. The arithmetic of the 20-student class shows what they are: idle hours for some, unmastered material for others. These costs fall unevenly, which is why the equity question runs through the whole chapter. Alternatives like charter, virtual, and microschools each loosen one piece of the model, with uneven results, and the history of educational technology shows that tools alone rarely loosen the grammar. The new factor this book examines is a technology whose capability keeps improving, which may make it possible to hold mastery fixed and let time vary.

The chapter's story has both sides. The benefit: ideas like mastery-based pacing could reduce wasted hours and close gaps. The risk: tools that save a few hours for teachers while adding others, evidence that comes from vendors rather than independent studies, and gaps that widen if only the best-resourced schools get the best tools. The response is the pair of habits you practiced: match resources to need, and ask who produced the evidence and who checked it.

## Key Takeaways

- The **industrial-era school model** grouped children into batches, moved them through fixed stages, and coordinated them by clock; each feature solved a real problem and each has a cost.
- The **bell schedule**, **age-based grade levels**, **seat time**, and the **Carnegie unit** (120 hours per credit) all treat time and age as stand-ins for learning.
- In a fixed-length course, some students finish early and wait while others do not finish; in our 20-student example, 535 hours sit idle and 6 students miss mastery.
- **Education policy** is made mainly by states and districts; the **school board** adopts the policies that govern schedules, grading, and data; **standardized testing** gives a common yardstick but measures only part of learning.
- An **achievement gap** is a group difference in results that usually reflects differences in opportunity; **educational equity** matches resources to need rather than splitting them equally.
- **Learning gains** measure growth in standard deviations, which lets you compare an intervention with a typical year, roughly 0.3 in middle school.
- **Charter**, **virtual**, and **microschools** loosen different parts of the traditional model, with mixed or thin evidence.
- The **history of EdTech** shows a century of big promises absorbed into old structures.
- **Teacher workload** (about 53 hours a week) and **teacher burnout** make a teacher's time the scarcest resource; a tool counts only if it reduces net hours.
- Weigh evidence by who produced it and who checked it: an **independent evaluation** carries more weight than **self-reported results**, and a **preprint** is a lead until peer review.
