# Quiz: Microstrategies for Leading Change

Test your understanding of the small, concrete moves that make change stick and how to match each one to the person and stakeholder in front of you with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What are the three parts of the Heaths' Switch framework?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Unfreeze the habit, change the practice, refreeze the routine
2. Direct the rider, motivate the elephant, shape the path
3. Build awareness, create desire, reinforce the change
4. Find the champion, run the pilot, scale the results
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Chip and Dan Heath picture a rider (the rational, planning side) on an elephant (the stronger emotional side) on a path (the situation). Directing the rider gives clarity, motivating the elephant engages emotion, and shaping the path makes the right behavior easier. A change succeeds when all three are addressed. The first option is Lewin's model, the third draws on ADKAR, and the fourth lists microstrategies rather than the framework.

    **Concept Tested:** Switch Framework

    **See:** [The Switch Framework](index.md#the-switch-framework)

#### 2. What is positive deviance?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. A teacher who openly refuses to adopt a new practice at all
2. An effect in which early success leads to unrealistic expectations
3. A method of importing proven practices from other countries
4. Spreading the practices of people who beat their peers with the same resources
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Positive deviance looks for individuals or groups who achieve better results than their peers with the same resources, then spreads their practices. In the best-known case, Jerry Sternin of Save the Children found that mothers of well-nourished children in poor Vietnamese villages added small shrimp, crabs, and sweet-potato greens to meals, and spreading these free, local practices reduced malnutrition. The chapter notes this is more believable and cheaper than importing practices from outside, which is what the third option describes.

    **Concept Tested:** Positive Deviance

    **See:** [Bright Spots and Positive Deviance](index.md#bright-spots-and-positive-deviance)

#### 3. A teacher says, "I want to use the new textbook, and I know what to do, but the student logins keep failing." In the Switch framework, what kind of fix does this call for?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Direct the rider, with a script of the first week's moves
2. Motivate the elephant, with a student's breakthrough story
3. Shape the path, by fixing the login so it works the first time
4. Grow identity, by telling the teacher "we are coaches now"
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter's diagnostic says a teacher who "doesn't know what to do" needs a rider fix, one who "knows but doesn't care" needs an elephant fix, and one who "wants to, but the login keeps failing" needs a path fix. This teacher has both clarity and motivation; the situation is the obstacle. The chapter warns that more of the wrong kind of help does nothing, so a script or a motivating story would miss the real problem.

    **Concept Tested:** Shape the Path

    **See:** [The Switch Framework](index.md#the-switch-framework)

#### 4. Why does shrinking the first step work, according to the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. A tiny step costs little, brings a success, and shifts how people see themselves
2. A tiny step lets the leader skip the training and support the change would otherwise need
3. A tiny step guarantees the person will adopt the full model within a week
4. A tiny step keeps the change secret until the plan is ready to announce
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Shrinking the first step makes the first action so small that it is easy, even a little silly, to refuse, such as "try this ten-minute activity once." It works on the elephant: low effort, a quick success, and a new self-image ("I'm someone who tries this"). It also gives the rider real information about the change. In the ladder of first steps, the teacher reaches a full lesson by week 6 without ever being asked to "adopt the new model." It is not a substitute for support or a guarantee.

    **Concept Tested:** Shrinking the First Step

    **See:** [Shrinking the First Step and Compounding Small Changes](index.md#shrinking-the-first-step-and-compounding-small-changes)

#### 5. A teacher improves one teaching skill by 2 percent a week for 26 weeks, with each week building on the last. About how much better is the skill at the end, and how does that compare with simply adding the weekly gains?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. About 26 percent, the same as adding the weekly gains
2. About 52 percent, the same as adding the weekly gains
3. About 40 percent, less than adding up the weekly gains
4. About 67 percent, more than adding the weekly gains
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Compounding multiplies the gains: \( 1.02^{26} \approx 1.67 \), or about 67 percent better. Simply adding 2 percent for 26 weeks gives 52 percent, which is less, because compounding builds each gain on the one before. The chapter's own example, 1 percent a week for 52 weeks, compounds to about 68 percent versus a sum of 52. It cautions that real skills do not improve at a constant rate, but small, repeated steps beat large, rare ones because they are more likely to be done.

    **Concept Tested:** Compounding Small Changes

    **See:** [Shrinking the First Step and Compounding Small Changes](index.md#shrinking-the-first-step-and-compounding-small-changes)

#### 6. What did the 2018 review by Kraft, Blazar, and Hogan find about instructional coaching?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Coaching had no measurable effect on instruction or student achievement
2. Coaching improved instruction by about 0.5 SD and achievement by about 0.18 SD
3. Coaching worked only when delivered online to large groups of teachers
4. Coaching improved achievement by about two standard deviations, matching tutoring
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The review of 60 studies found that coaching improved teachers' instruction by about half a standard deviation and student achievement by about 0.18 standard deviations, with larger effects for in-person coaching that was not spread too widely. The chapter calls coaching the best-supported form of professional development, because it supplies practice and feedback in the teacher's own classroom and addresses CBAM task concerns and ADKAR ability. The third option reverses the finding about delivery.

    **Concept Tested:** Instructional Coaching

    **See:** [Coaching, Observation, and Learning Communities](index.md#coaching-observation-and-learning-communities)

#### 7. A teacher who has heard about the new morning model asks, "But what does it actually look like in a real classroom?" Which microstrategy best matches this concern?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Share early visible results in a professional learning community
2. Schedule instructional coaching to fix logistics problems
3. Arrange a peer observation or a visit to a pilot classroom
4. Ask the teacher to lead a planning team for the next pilot
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. "What does it actually look like?" is an informational concern, stage 1 in CBAM. The chapter's matching table pairs it with peer observation or a pilot-classroom visit, which supply observability, one of Rogers' five attributes. Sharing results in a PLC fits a consequence concern (stage 4), coaching on logistics fits a task concern (stage 3), and leading a planning team suits a collaboration concern or an early adopter. Matching the move to the concern is the point.

    **Concept Tested:** Matching Strategy to Concern

    **See:** [Matching the Strategy to the Person](index.md#matching-the-strategy-to-the-person)

#### 8. A group of parents raises concerns about student privacy and over-reliance on AI. How does the chapter suggest a leader should treat these concerns?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. As legitimate concerns that may point to real risks the leaders have not seen
2. As resistance to be overcome with a stronger presentation of the benefits
3. As a sign that the parents lack awareness and need more training
4. As concerns to be addressed only after the program has launched
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Jeffrey Ford and Laurie Ford argued that resistance can be a resource, because people who push back often see real problems leaders do not. A legitimate concern has a real basis in evidence or experience. The chapter lists privacy, over-reliance, screen time, thin evidence, and unequal access as legitimate concerns about AI, and warns that treating them as resistance throws away the people who noticed the real risks. Waiting until after launch ignores the advice to communicate before rumors.

    **Concept Tested:** Legitimate Concern

    **See:** [Resistance, Concerns, and Job Fears](index.md#resistance-concerns-and-job-fears)

#### 9. A veteran teacher says, "I'm afraid this means my job won't exist in five years." Which principal reply best reflects the chapter's guidance?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. "Don't worry. Nobody here is going to lose their job, I promise."
2. "That's a fair worry. Here's what the plan says about your role, and what I can promise."
3. "You're overreacting. The plan clearly says that teachers will still be needed here."
4. "Let's talk about it later, once the pilot results come in this spring."
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Responding with empathy means listening, acknowledging the feeling and its reason, asking to understand more, and then giving facts, options, or a commitment. The chapter's model reply does this and adds what the plan assumes, what can and cannot be promised, and what the teacher would want in writing. A blanket promise feels kind but destroys trust if it cannot be kept. Calling the teacher an overreactor is dismissive, and postponing leaves the fear unanswered.

    **Concept Tested:** Fear of Job Loss

    **See:** [Worked Example: Four Replies to a Worried Teacher](index.md#worked-example-four-replies-to-a-worried-teacher)

#### 10. A superintendent wants to wait until the AI-native plan is fully finished before telling the teacher union or families, so that she can "present a polished plan." What does the chapter's analysis predict, and what should she do instead?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The polished plan will win support, so waiting is the safest approach
2. Families will not notice the change, so only the union needs early notice
3. The union has no role in schedule changes, so only the families need to be told
4. Rumors and opposition are likely; she should share drafts early and agree terms in writing
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter says to communicate before rumors, because rumors are far harder to correct than a clear early statement, and silence is read as secrecy. A leader who waits for the plan to be perfect lets the rumor mill write it. Union engagement means sharing plans before they are final, asking for concerns, and agreeing on workload, evaluation, and data, often in a written memorandum. A union that learns of a change from the newspaper will oppose it, with good reason; unions often hold contractual rights over the work day.

    **Concept Tested:** Union Engagement

    **See:** [Stakeholders: Families, Unions, the Board, and Students](index.md#stakeholders-families-unions-the-board-and-students)
