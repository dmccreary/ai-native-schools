# Quiz: Evidence, Motivation, and the Stakeholders of Schooling

Test your understanding of how educators judge evidence, what drives student motivation, and who shares a school decision with these review questions.

---

#### 1. Under the ESSA evidence levels described in this chapter, what does an intervention need to qualify as "strong" evidence?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. A satisfaction survey in which most teachers rate the program highly
2. A well-designed study comparing users with a chosen comparison group
3. At least one well-designed, well-implemented study with random assignment
4. A logical explanation based on research, with an evaluation already planned
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The strong level requires at least one well-designed, well-implemented experimental study in which students were randomly assigned to groups. A study with a chosen comparison group but no random assignment is moderate evidence, and a research-based rationale with a planned evaluation is the lowest ESSA level. A satisfaction survey falls in the chapter's extra bin of claims that show no evidence of effect. The ladder ranks study design, not the size of the claim.

    **Concept Tested:** Evidence-Based Practice

    **See:** [Evidence-Based Practice: Judging What Works](index.md#evidence-based-practice-judging-what-works)

#### 2. Why is random assignment the central feature of a randomized controlled trial?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. It makes the two groups alike on average, even in factors nobody measured
2. It guarantees that the treatment group will score higher than the control group
3. It allows students to choose the group that best fits their learning style
4. It lets researchers study a whole district without needing a control group
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. When assignment is by chance, the two groups start out alike on average in prior knowledge, motivation, family support, and even unmeasured factors, so a later difference is more likely to be caused by the intervention. This removes selection bias, the problem that arises when motivated students opt in. Randomization does not guarantee a result, and letting students choose their group is exactly what creates selection bias. Every RCT needs a control group.

    **Concept Tested:** Randomized Controlled Trial

    **See:** [Randomized Controlled Trials](index.md#randomized-controlled-trials)

#### 3. By Matthew Kraft's benchmarks for well-designed education studies on standardized achievement, what counts as a large effect size?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Anything above 0.80
2. Anything above 0.50
3. Anything above 0.40
4. Anything above 0.20
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Kraft's 2020 review suggests that below 0.05 is small, 0.05 to 0.20 is medium, and above 0.20 is large for real interventions measured on standardized tests in real schools. The 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 cutoffs come from Jacob Cohen's rough guides for lab psychology, and 0.40 is John Hattie's contested hinge point. By Kraft's standard, a vendor's claim of 0.8 should prompt questions about study design.

    **Concept Tested:** Effect Size

    **See:** [Effect Size](index.md#effect-size)

#### 4. In a randomized pilot, the treatment group's mean score is 72 and the control group's is 68. The pooled standard deviation is 20. What is the effect size, and how would the chapter describe it?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. About 4.0, an implausibly large effect that suggests a flawed study
2. About 0.2, a large effect by education benchmarks for real schools
3. About 0.2, a trivial effect that is not worth acting on
4. About 0.4, a medium effect by Cohen's lab psychology guides
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Cohen's *d* is the difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation: (72 - 68) / 20 = 0.2. The chapter notes that by Kraft's benchmarks an effect above 0.20 is large, and that "even an effect of 0.2 is a major result" in real schools. Calling it trivial applies Cohen's lab-psychology labels, where 0.2 is "small." The 4.0 answer forgets to divide by the standard deviation.

    **Concept Tested:** Effect Size

    **See:** [Effect Size](index.md#effect-size)

#### 5. A randomized trial shows a math program raised scores on average. What does a follow-up case study add that the trial cannot?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Proof that the program will work in every other school in the district
2. A larger sample size that makes the average effect more precise
3. An account of how and why the program worked or failed in particular classrooms
4. A replacement for the trial, since case studies are the stronger design
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. An RCT answers whether something worked on average; a case study examines how and why it worked, or did not, in a particular setting, using interviews, observation, and documents. In the MathPath example, the case study found that gains depended on how teachers used the dashboard, which the average hid. One case cannot prove a general rule, and the chapter says the sound approach is to use both, not to replace one with the other.

    **Concept Tested:** Case Study Method

    **See:** [Case Study Method](index.md#case-study-method)

#### 6. A diagnostic covers a simple chain: concept 5 (multiplying fractions) depends on concepts 1 and 2; concept 6 (ratios) depends on concept 3; concept 7 (unit rates) depends on 5 and 6. A student passes 1, 2, 3, and 6 but fails 5 and 7. Where should the teacher start?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Concept 7, because unit rates is the skill the class is currently studying
2. Concept 6, because ratios is the most recent prerequisite of unit rates
3. Concept 1, because reteaching from the bottom of the chain is always safest
4. Concept 5, because it failed while all of its own prerequisites passed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter defines the root gap as a failed concept whose own prerequisites all passed. Concept 5 failed while 1 and 2 passed, so it is the root. Concept 7 also failed, but one of its prerequisites (5) failed too, which makes it a symptom; reteaching a symptom changes nothing. Concept 6 passed, so it needs no work, and reteaching concept 1 wastes time on something the student already knows.

    **Concept Tested:** Prerequisite Concept

    **See:** [Worked Example: Maya's Missing Stair](index.md#worked-example-mayas-missing-stair)

#### 7. According to self-determination theory, which three needs help intrinsic motivation grow?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Autonomy, competence, and relatedness
2. Curiosity, reward, and recognition
3. Challenge, feedback, and repetition
4. Structure, competition, and praise
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's self-determination theory holds that intrinsic motivation grows when students have a sense of choice (autonomy), a sense of being able to succeed (competence), and a sense of connection to others (relatedness). Reward and recognition are extrinsic motivators, and the chapter's discussion of the overjustification effect shows that adding rewards to an activity a student already enjoys can weaken intrinsic motivation.

    **Concept Tested:** Intrinsic Motivation

    **See:** [Intrinsic Motivation](index.md#intrinsic-motivation)

#### 8. Three students in the same class all have their heads down and pencils untouched. Why does the chapter warn against applying one fix to all of them?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Because boredom is a behavior problem that each family must address at home
2. Because boredom can come from work that is too easy, too hard, or seen as pointless
3. Because bored students are usually distracted by devices brought from home
4. Because boredom disappears on its own once students move to self-paced software
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Drawing on Reinhard Pekrun's research, the chapter explains that boredom can arise when a task is too easy, when it is too hard and the student has given up, or when the student sees no value in it. The behavior looks the same, but the fixes differ: harder material, a filled prerequisite, or a connection to the student's interests. The wrong fix, such as more challenge for a lost student, makes things worse. The chapter warns against treating bored students as behavior problems.

    **Concept Tested:** Student Boredom

    **See:** [Student Boredom](index.md#student-boredom)

#### 9. According to the chapter, when is struggle productive rather than simply frustrating?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. When the task is hard but reachable and the student has prior knowledge to use
2. When the student is left entirely alone, with no hints, until they find the answer
3. When the tool reveals the answer quickly so the student can keep moving forward
4. When failure on the task carries a grade penalty that raises the stakes
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Struggle is productive when the task is hard but reachable, the student has some prior knowledge to work with, errors are treated as normal, and the teacher later consolidates what was learned. Leaving a student alone with no support produces frustration, revealing the answer removes the struggle and the learning, and punishing failure makes struggle unproductive. The hint ladder example shows the goal is the smallest help that keeps the student moving.

    **Concept Tested:** Productive Struggle

    **See:** [Productive Struggle](index.md#productive-struggle)

#### 10. A parent says an AI-native school day is harmful because it adds about 100 minutes of screen time. Which response is best supported by the chapter's analysis?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The parent is right, because research shows all screen minutes harm well-being equally
2. The concern does not apply, because adaptive practice is not counted as screen time
3. The answer depends on what the minutes are used for and replace, including time at home
4. The school should reduce adaptive practice until total screen time matches the old day
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter argues that "screen time" lumps together very different activities and that purpose and context matter more than the total. The added school minutes replace passive listening, and in the worked example the home figure is larger than the school figure in either design, so the school should talk with families about evenings too. The research on harm is weaker and more mixed than headlines suggest, and adaptive practice does count as screen time.

    **Concept Tested:** Screen Time
