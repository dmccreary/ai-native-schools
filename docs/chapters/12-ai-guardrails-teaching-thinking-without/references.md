# References: AI Guardrails: Teaching Thinking Without Over-Reliance

1. [Computational thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_thinking) - Wikipedia - Defines computational thinking and its parts: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithmic thinking. It gives the vocabulary for the chapter's skills that AI can help teach without doing the thinking for students.

2. [Socratic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_method) - Wikipedia - Explains teaching through questions that lead students to examine their own reasoning. It is the model behind AI tutors that give hints and questions instead of answers.

3. [Hallucination (artificial intelligence)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination_(artificial_intelligence)) - Wikipedia - Describes why language models produce confident but false statements, with examples and mitigation methods. It explains the confidently wrong answers that make open chatbot access risky for students.

4. Why Don't Students Like School? A Cognitive Scientist Answers Questions About How the Mind Works and What It Means for the Classroom - Daniel T. Willingham - Jossey-Bass - Willingham's principle that "memory is the residue of thought" explains why students who let AI do the thinking remember little. This grounds the chapter's concern about over-reliance and cognitive offloading.

5. Mindstorms: Children, Computers, and Powerful Ideas - Seymour Papert - Basic Books - Papert argued that children should program computers rather than be programmed by them, using them as tools for thinking. His idea frames guided AI as a partner that builds student reasoning instead of replacing it.

6. [Computational Thinking](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~15110-s13/Wing06-ct.pdf) - Jeannette M. Wing, Communications of the ACM - Wing's short 2006 essay argues that computational thinking is a basic skill for everyone, not only programmers. It is the source that brought the idea into K-12 schools.

7. [Introduction to computational thinking](https://www.bbc.co.uk/bitesize/guides/zp92mp3/revision/1) - BBC Bitesize - A student-friendly introduction to decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithms with everyday examples. Teachers can use it to explain the four skills before asking students to apply them with an AI tutor.

8. [Without Guardrails, Generative AI Can Harm Education](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education) - Knowledge at Wharton - Reports a study of nearly 1,000 Turkish high school math students: open ChatGPT boosted practice scores but lowered exam scores, while a hint-giving tutor avoided most of the harm. Key evidence for guided AI.

9. [The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/) - Microsoft Research - A survey of 319 knowledge workers finding that more trust in AI went with less critical thinking. It suggests habits of over-reliance form early, so schools should teach students to question AI output.

10. [Guidance for generative AI in education and research](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research) - UNESCO - Global guidance calling for data privacy protection, age limits on independent chatbot use and human-centred design of AI in schools. It supports the chapter's case for age-appropriate guardrails and selective chatbot use.
