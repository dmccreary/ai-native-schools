#### 1. What tuition does the chapter report for Alpha School?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. About \$40,000 a year in Austin and about \$65,000 at newer campuses
2. About \$65,000 a year at every campus in the network
3. About \$15,600 a year, the same as public per-pupil spending
4. About \$40,000 a year at newer campuses and about \$65,000 in Austin
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Alpha School charges about \$40,000 a year at its original Austin campus and about \$65,000 a year at newer campuses such as Boston, Miami, and New York. The chapter stresses that each figure applies to its own campuses and that the higher figure should not be given as the price for every campus. The fourth option reverses the two. Compared with public per-pupil spending of about \$15,600, the tuition is about 2.6 to 4.2 times as much.

#### 2. According to the chapter, when is a composite story, such as the story of Maya and Lincoln Middle School, honest?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. When its outcomes match the results of a published study
2. When it includes a real student's name and the name of the school
3. When it is told with images as an illustrated story
4. When it is clearly labeled as fiction, not passed off as a real case
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. A composite story is a fictional account assembled from patterns in many real situations, with invented characters, used to make an idea concrete. The chapter says it is honest when labeled and dishonest when passed off as a real case, and that no quotation or outcome attached to a composite should be taken as fact about a real person. Adding a real name would make it misleading. An illustrated story can be real or composite, so images do not decide honesty.

#### 3. To manage the risk of a vendor data breach, a district buys cyber insurance and adds a contract clause making the vendor responsible for breach costs. Which form of risk mitigation is this?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Avoid
2. Accept
3. Transfer
4. Reduce
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter names four forms of risk mitigation: avoid the risk entirely, reduce its likelihood or harm, transfer it (for example, to an insurer or through a contract), or accept it knowingly. Insurance and a contract clause shift the cost of a breach to others, which is transfer. Data minimization or multi-factor authentication would reduce the risk, not transfer it. Not using the product at all would avoid it. A school chooses among the four for each risk.

    **Concept Tested:** Risk Mitigation

    **See:** [A Framework for Reading Cases](index.md#a-framework-for-reading-cases)

#### 4. Unbound Academy uses Alpha School's 2 Hour Learning software but without the in-person afternoon. What principle does the book draw from comparing the two?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Software is not the model: adults, projects, and people produced the results too
2. Online schools always outperform in-person schools when they use the same software
3. The software works only when students pay tuition, which raises motivation
4. Two hours of software is too little, so the morning should be lengthened
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The same software, used without the adult guides and the in-person afternoon, did not carry over its results: Unbound's early reported proficiency was below state averages. The book calls this principle "software is not the model." A school that buys the software and omits the afternoon has bought a fraction of what it saw. The chapter does not claim online schools do better, and it does not attribute the difference to tuition or the length of the morning.

    **Concept Tested:** Software Is Not the Model

    **See:** [Case 2: Unbound Academy and the Software Is Not the Model](index.md#case-2-unbound-academy-and-the-software-is-not-the-model)

#### 5. A pilot plan includes a 120-minute morning screen cap, a hint-first tutor, a signed data agreement, loaner devices with subgroup reports, a teacher roles statement, and a weekly review of tutor logs for errors. Checked against the chapter's pilot risk checklist, which risk is not covered?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Wrong AI answers
2. Unstructured afternoon
3. Handing thinking over to AI
4. Fear of job loss
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The checklist has one line for each of the seven risks the course description names: screen time, handing thinking over to AI, wrong AI answers, privacy, cost and equity, fear of job loss, and unstructured afternoons. The plan covers the first six, including wrong answers through the log review, but says nothing about the afternoon. The checklist requires the SAFE practices and trained adults, checked with an afternoon quality rating, before the pilot starts.

#### 6. The Turkish high school study found harm from open chatbot access, while the Harvard physics study found benefit from a designed AI tutor. What does the chapter conclude from reading them together, and what limit applies?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. AI tutors work for college students but harm high school students, so ages should be separated
2. More powerful models help and weaker ones harm, so schools should buy the strongest model
3. Both studies prove a compressed morning works for all students in every setting
4. How the AI is designed decides help or harm, but each study covers only one setting
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Turkish study used the same model in each condition, and the hint-first version removed most of the harm; the Harvard tutor was built on proven teaching practices rather than as an open chatbot. Together they show design over raw AI power. Both are randomized, but each has limits: the Turkish study covers one school and short-term math, and the Harvard study one course at one university with well-prepared students. The first option confuses setting with design.

    **Concept Tested:** Design Over Raw AI Power

    **See:** [Case 4: The Harvard Physics AI Tutor](index.md#case-4-the-harvard-physics-ai-tutor)

#### 7. Reading Summit Learning with the risk-and-response framework, which risk does the case most clearly show?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Screen time and too little contact with teachers
2. Wrong AI answers delivered with confidence
3. Fear of job loss among teachers
4. Privacy breaches of student data
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. In 2018 and 2019 students in Brooklyn walked out and families in Kansas protested Summit Learning over long hours on screens and too little contact with teachers. The appendix calls it the clearest real example of the screen-time risk. The case shows a technology-led model without a human afternoon, in which students felt isolated and families lost trust. It is the reason the book keeps the morning short and the afternoon social, and hears students and families early.

    **Concept Tested:** Summit Learning

    **See:** [Case 5: Summit Learning](index.md#case-5-summit-learning)

#### 8. A district gives students an open chat tutor for homework. Homework scores rise, but teachers notice that unit test scores without the tutor fall. The district switches to a hint-first tutor and adds in-class assessments that show process. How should this story be entered in the risk-and-response framework?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Benefit: lower costs. Risk: privacy. Response: a data agreement. Lesson: contracts.
2. Benefit: higher scores. Risk: screen time. Response: shorter sessions. Lesson: limits.
3. Benefit: better homework. Risk: offloaded thinking. Response: hint-first tutor. Lesson: design.
4. Benefit: time saved. Risk: job loss. Response: a roles statement. Lesson: reassure.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The framework has four columns: the story's benefit, the risk it also shows, the response, and the lesson. Here the benefit is better homework performance, the risk is handing thinking over to AI, shown by falling scores without the tool, the response is a hint-first tutor and process-based assessment, and the lesson matches the Turkish study: design matters more than power. The other options name risks the story does not show; in this story, test scores fell rather than rose.

    **Concept Tested:** Risk-and-Response Framework

    **See:** [A Framework for Reading Cases](index.md#a-framework-for-reading-cases)

#### 9. Unbound Academy reportedly showed about 10 percent of students proficient in math and 28 percent in English language arts, while claiming students learn 2.8 times faster on its own growth tests. The reports do not say where students started. What can a careful reader conclude?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. The program clearly works, since students learn 2.8 times faster
2. The program clearly fails, since proficiency is far below the state average
3. The program works for English but not for math, based on the two scores
4. Little about the program's effect, without baselines and independent evaluation
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Proficiency shows where students stand at one moment, not how much they grew, and the reports give no starting points. The 2.8-times figure comes from the school's own growth tests, a self-reported result. The chapter's response is to hold claims to the evidence standard of Chapter 2: ask for starting points and an independent evaluation. Concluding that it clearly works or clearly fails reads more into the figures than they support.

    **Concept Tested:** Unbound Academy

    **See:** [Case 2: Unbound Academy and the Software Is Not the Model](index.md#case-2-unbound-academy-and-the-software-is-not-the-model)

#### 10. At a board meeting, a member says, "Alpha School proves the two-hour model works. We should adopt it district-wide next year." Which response is best supported by the chapter's reading of the cases?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

1. Agree, since Alpha's success shows the model will work at any price and in any school
2. Disagree in part: Alpha's results are self-reported, so pilot with independent evaluation
3. Disagree completely, since Unbound's results prove the model cannot work in public schools
4. Agree, as long as the district buys the same adaptive software that Alpha uses
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter's honest reading is that the components have support while the package is still being tested: the strongest randomized evidence concerns tutors, and the evidence for whole-school models is self-reported or early. Alpha's results come from the school, with no independent evaluation in the book's sources, and at private-school prices. The chapter warns against treating one vivid case as proof in either direction, so Unbound does not prove failure, and buying the software alone ignores that software is not the model.
