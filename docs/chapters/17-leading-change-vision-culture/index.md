## Lewin: Unfreeze, Change, Refreeze

**Lewin's change model** describes change in three stages, and it gives the first overall shape to the work. Kurt Lewin proposed it in 1947. His central image is a block of ice: to give it a new shape you must first melt it, then reshape it, then freeze it again.

The **unfreeze stage** is the preparation of an organization for change by loosening the habits, beliefs, and routines that hold the present way in place. People do not change while they are comfortable. Unfreezing creates motivation by showing a gap between how things are and how they should be, and by building enough psychological safety that people can let go. The **change stage** is the period of actually moving to the new way of working: learning, trying, making mistakes, and adjusting. It is uncertain and uncomfortable, and it is where the implementation dip of Chapter 16 lives. The **refreeze stage** is the stabilization of the new way so that it becomes the normal way, supported by policies, routines, rewards, and habits so that people do not slide back.

Lewin also gave the field a tool for the unfreezing stage, *force field analysis*. It pictures any situation as a balance between *driving forces*, which push toward change, and *restraining forces*, which push against it. Lewin's most useful insight is practical: pushing harder on the driving forces tends to raise the restraining forces too, as people dig in, whereas *reducing* the restraining forces lets change happen with less tension.

Critics note that the three-stage picture can mislead in a world where change never stops. In schools, where one reform follows another, "refreezing" can become ossifying. A modern reading treats refreezing lightly: make the new practice stable, while keeping a habit of learning and adjusting, which is Senge's learning organization from Chapter 16.

## Kotter: From Urgency to Anchoring

John Kotter's eight steps give leaders a sequence. The sequence matters because Kotter's research found that organizations often skipped early steps, such as building urgency, in their hurry to reach later ones, and the skipped steps came back to cause failure. Here are the eight, with what each means for a school.

**Sense of urgency** is the shared belief, among enough people, that change is needed now and that staying the same is riskier than changing. Without it, people have no reason to do the hard work. Urgency is not panic: it comes from facing real facts, such as Chapter 1's finding that in a class of 28 only 13 students are in reach of a single lesson. Kotter argued that a large majority of leaders must be honestly convinced before going further.

A **guiding coalition** is a group of people with enough power, credibility, expertise, and trust to lead the change. In a school it should include the principal, respected teachers, a union representative, a family representative, and technical staff, and it should include voices that are skeptical. A coalition made only of enthusiasts will not be believed by the rest.

A **change vision** is a clear, compelling picture of the future the change is meant to create, simple enough to explain in a few minutes. The book's own vision is "mornings with AI, afternoons with each other." A good vision says what will be different for students, and why it is worth the difficulty. **Communicating the vision** is repeating it through every channel and in actions as well as words, far more often than seems necessary, because people hear it through the noise of their own concerns. Kotter observed that leaders typically under-communicate by a large factor.

**Removing obstacles** is clearing the barriers, in structures, systems, and skills, that stand between people and the change: a bell schedule that blocks the afternoon, a contract term that blocks a new role, a login system that does not work, a supervisor who undermines the effort. Lewin's lesson applies here: removing a barrier is more effective than pushing people over it.

**Short-term wins** are visible, unambiguous successes achieved early, which show that the effort is working and build momentum. They need to be real, visible, and clearly linked to the change. A pilot classroom's gains, a student exhibition, or a teacher's saved evening count. **Building on change** is using the credibility from early wins to take on bigger problems, instead of declaring victory too soon. Kotter treated premature victory as one of the most common mistakes. **Anchoring in culture** is making the new way of working part of "the way we do things here," by embedding it in hiring, orientation, evaluation, schedules, and stories, so that it survives the departure of its champions.

## Vision as a Shared Thing: Senge's Shared Vision and Personal Mastery

Kotter says a vision should be clear and communicated. Peter Senge adds a harder point: a vision that is only announced is not shared. **Shared vision**, one of Senge's five disciplines, is a picture of the future that people throughout the organization truly hold and want, built through conversation and not handed down. The difference is between *compliance*, in which people go along with what they were told, and *commitment*, in which they want it. Compliance produces the minimum and fades when the leader leaves. Commitment produces initiative. A principal can write a vision statement in an afternoon, but a shared vision takes months of listening, in which teachers' hopes shape the picture.

**Personal mastery**, another of Senge's disciplines, is each person's commitment to their own continual growth and to what they truly care about. It matters for change because an organization learns only as fast as its people do, and because people who are growing, and not being merely managed, bring energy and honesty to the work. A school that wants teachers to become coaches, as the book proposes, needs to support the teacher's own development as a person, not only to train them on a tool.

### Worked Example: Compliance or Commitment?

Two principals announce the same vision. A teacher's reactions differ.

| | Principal A | Principal B |
|---|---|---|
| How the vision was made | Written by the principal and emailed | Built over six weeks in small-group conversations with teachers, students, and parents |
| A veteran teacher's reaction | "Another thing from the office; I'll do what's required" | "Some of my own ideas are in it; I want to see it work" |
| Behavior after six months | Minimum use; some quiet resistance | Teachers propose improvements |
| Type | Compliance | Commitment |

The extra five weeks of conversation produced the difference. It is slower at the start and much faster after.

## Bridges: Endings and Losses

William Bridges made a distinction that changes how a leader sees resistance. The **change** is the external event, such as a new schedule; the **transition** is the internal psychological process of coming to terms with it. A leader can announce the change on Monday, but the transition takes much longer. **Bridges' transition model** describes the transition as having three phases, which the next chapter covers fully, and it begins with the one leaders most often skip.

**Endings and losses** are the first phase of a transition, in which people must let go of the old way and grieve what the change takes from them. Bridges argued that every change, even a welcome one, involves loss, and that people cannot begin anything new until they have an ending. The losses are often not the obvious ones. A teacher losing the lecture format may lose her identity as an expert, the competence she has built, her routines, her relationships with colleagues in the old structure, and her sense of control. Leaders who skip the ending and talk only about the exciting new beginning are puzzled when people do not respond.

### Worked Example: A Loss Inventory

Ms. Okafor meets with the seventh-grade math team and lists what the compressed morning takes from them.

| Kind of loss | What a teacher might feel |
|---|---|
| Identity | "I am the person who explains this well to a whole class" |
| Competence | "I was good at this, and now I'm a beginner again" |
| Routines | "Fifty minutes of my own lesson, five times a day, is gone" |
| Relationships | "I'm no longer at the front; will students still need me?" |
| Control | "The software decides the pace now" |
| Meaning | "Why did I spend years learning something that's being replaced?" |

She does not argue with any of it. She acknowledges each, asks the team what part of their craft they most want to keep, and builds that into the new role: the coach who knows exactly when a student needs a human explanation. Naming the losses is what lets the team start to look ahead.

## Schein: Culture Beneath the Surface

**Schein's culture model** describes an organization's culture in three layers. At the surface are *artifacts*: what you can see and hear, such as the building, the schedule, the dress, the language, and the rituals. In the middle are *espoused values*: what the organization says it believes, such as "we value collaboration." At the base are *basic assumptions*: the unspoken, taken-for-granted beliefs that really drive behavior, such as "a good teacher works alone." When the values people state disagree with the assumptions they hold, the assumptions win, which explains why reforms announced at the level of artifacts and values often fail. Chapter 18 treats the three levels and culture assessment in detail.

Two kinds of cultural material show up early in a change and are worth naming now. **Shared beliefs and norms** are the beliefs held in common and the unwritten rules for behavior: that teachers do not enter each other's rooms uninvited, that parents are informed and not consulted, that the schedule is not to be touched. **School rituals** are the repeated, symbolic events and routines that express and reinforce a school's culture: morning announcements, assemblies, awards, staff meetings, the first day and the last. Rituals matter to change in two ways. They are a source of resistance when a change disrupts a cherished one. And they are a tool, because a new ritual, such as a student exhibition night, builds the new culture in the open.

## ADKAR: The First Three Elements

**The ADKAR model**, introduced in Chapter 16, gives each person's path through a change in five elements. The first three are the early-stage ones, and they are the ones a leader must secure before training can help.

**ADKAR Awareness** is a person's understanding of *why* the change is needed and what will happen if it does not occur. Awareness is created by honest, repeated communication of the reasons and the evidence, not by an announcement of what will happen. **ADKAR Desire** is a person's wish to take part and support the change. It is the hardest element to create, because it cannot be ordered. It grows from the person's own motives: what the change means for them, their trust in leadership, their values, and their sense of being heard. **ADKAR Knowledge** is knowing *how* to change: what to do differently, and the skills and information to do it. Knowledge is what training and job aids supply, and it is useless without awareness and desire.

| Element | The person's question | What builds it | A sign it is missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | "Why are we doing this?" | Honest explanation of the reasons and the evidence | "I don't see why we're changing" |
| Desire | "Do I want to take part?" | Involvement, listening, sponsors, addressing "what's in it for me" | "I understand why, but I don't want to" |
| Knowledge | "How do I do it?" | Training, examples, job aids | "I'm willing, but I don't know where to start" |

### Worked Example: Reading Three Teachers

| Teacher says | Missing element | First move |
|---|---|---|
| "I wasn't at the meeting; what is this about?" | Awareness | A short, direct explanation with the data |

Training the first two would waste their time. Chapter 18 returns to Ability and Reinforcement.

## CBAM: Concerns at the Start

**The Concerns-Based Adoption Model** (CBAM), introduced in Chapter 16, begins with the observation that teachers going through a change move through predictable *concerns*, and that leaders help most by answering the concern a person actually has. **Stages of concern** are the seven kinds of concern, labeled 0 to 6, that people have about an innovation as they get to know it: awareness (0), informational (1), personal (2), management (3), consequence (4), collaboration (5), and refocusing (6). A person may have several at once, and concerns usually move from earlier to later stages as the person gains experience. The stages are measured with a questionnaire, the Stages of Concern Questionnaire, and an interview.

**Self concerns** are the first three stages, in which the person's concern is about *themselves* in relation to the innovation:

- **Stage 0, awareness:** little concern or involvement. "I'm not thinking about this."
- **Stage 1, informational:** a wish to know more about what it is. "What exactly is it, and how does it work?"
- **Stage 2, personal:** concern about what it will mean for oneself. "How will this affect me? Will I be able to do it? Will I be judged?"

Concerns of the first three stages are not signs of resistance. They are the natural start of anyone's learning, and they need answers, not persuasion. A person at stage 2 who is told "it will be great for the students" has not been answered, since the question was about themselves. Chapter 18 covers the later stages, which are about the *task* (stage 3) and about *impact* on students and on colleagues (stages 4 to 6).

An **innovation configuration** is a description, usually a map, of what an innovation looks like in practice, listing its essential components and, for each, the range of ways it may be carried out from the ideal to the unacceptable. It exists because two teachers can both say they are "using the new textbook" while doing very different things. An innovation configuration map defines what the school means by "using it," so that a leader can see whether the innovation is actually happening.

### Worked Example: An Innovation Configuration Map

An illustrative map for the adaptive morning, with three components.

| Component | Ideal | Acceptable | Unacceptable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pacing | Students move at their own pace, with teacher check-ins | Students move at their own pace, with little teacher contact | Whole class forced to move at one pace |
| Mastery checks | Mastery rule applied; teacher reviews each dashboard flag | Mastery rule applied; teacher reviews weekly | Students move on regardless of mastery |
| Teacher dashboard | Used daily to pull small groups | Used weekly | Never opened |

With this map, "using it" is no longer a yes or no. A teacher who lets students pace themselves but never opens the dashboard is acceptable on one component and unacceptable on another. The map tells the coach exactly where to help.

## Lewin's Refreeze and Kotter's Anchoring Revisited

The early stages end where the late ones begin. Lewin's refreeze and Kotter's anchoring in culture say the same thing in different words: a change has not happened until it is part of the normal way of working. The two ideas bring this chapter's themes together. Unfreezing needs urgency, a coalition, and a vision that people share. The change stage needs obstacles removed and early wins. Refreezing, or anchoring, needs the new way to be written into hiring, routines, and rituals.

!!! mascot-thinking "A change isn't done until it's ordinary"
    ![Indigo thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Notice how the two models agree: the finish line is not the launch, it's the day nobody calls it "the new way" anymore. That's why rituals matter. A new ritual, like an exhibition night, turns a change into a tradition people defend.

## Diagnosing Where a School Stands

Ms. Okafor's Monday morning now has a diagnostic. For the district as a whole she asks whether there is urgency, a coalition, a vision, and obstacles that have been cleared. For the culture she reads artifacts, stated values, and the assumptions beneath them. For each group of teachers she asks which ADKAR element is missing and which concern they hold. In the room of 400, she now sees the veteran at the back grieving a loss, the young teacher at stage 2 worrying about being judged, the department chair weighing the schedule, and the teacher who has not heard at all, who lacks awareness.

The story has both sides. The benefit: early-stage tools that tell a leader where to start with each group and each person. The risks: moving to the new vision before people have grieved the old one, mistaking early questions for resistance, announcing a vision no one shares, and building on a culture that contradicts the plan. The response: listen first, diagnose by person and by stage, answer the question each person is actually asking, and treat the culture as something to read before you try to change it.

!!! mascot-tip "Answer the question they asked"
    ![Indigo giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    When a teacher asks "Will I be evaluated on this?", resist the urge to reply "it's great for kids." Answer the question about them first, directly and honestly. People can hear about students only after their own concern has been heard.

## Key Takeaways

- **Lewin's change model** has three stages: the **unfreeze stage** (create motivation, loosen habits), the **change stage** (learning and the dip), and the **refreeze stage** (stabilize). Force field analysis shows that reducing restraining forces beats pushing driving forces.
- **Kotter's steps**, in order: a **sense of urgency**, a **guiding coalition**, a **change vision**, **communicating the vision**, **removing obstacles**, **short-term wins**, **building on change**, and **anchoring in culture**.
- A **shared vision** is built with people and produces commitment; a vision that is only announced produces compliance. **Personal mastery** is the growth of each person.
- **Bridges** distinguishes change from transition; **endings and losses** come first, and leaders who skip them are puzzled by the lack of response.
- **Schein's culture model** reads artifacts, espoused values, and basic assumptions; **shared beliefs and norms** and **school rituals** are early clues and tools.
- **ADKAR Awareness**, **Desire**, and **Knowledge** are the first three elements; training only helps when the first two are present.
- **Stages of concern** begin with **self concerns** (awareness, informational, personal), which are not resistance and need answers; an **innovation configuration** map defines what "using it" means.
