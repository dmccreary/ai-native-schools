## About This MicroSim

Each of six project descriptions is strong on every essential element but one. You read the description and name the element that is missing. Because every description sounds good, you cannot judge by overall impression: you have to check each element in turn and look for what is absent.

The elements follow the PBLWorks model described in the chapter. The sim merges the seven elements to six by combining the challenging question with sustained inquiry. The projects are fictional, written for this chapter.

| Element | Definition |
|---|---|
| Challenging question | An open-ended driving question that cannot be answered by looking it up |
| Authenticity | The work connects to real people, data, or problems |
| Student voice and choice | Students make real decisions about the topic, method, or product |
| Reflection | Students think and talk about what they learned and how |
| Critique and revision | Students get feedback and improve their work |
| Public product | Students present their work to an audience beyond the class |

## How to Use

- Read the project on the card. The progress strip shows which project you are on and your running score, "First-try correct".
- Choose one of the six elements with the buttons. If your first answer is wrong, a hint appears and you choose again. Only your first answer counts toward the score.
- **Show definitions** opens a table of the elements. Reading it is exploration, not evidence of mastery.
- **Next** moves on after you read the feedback. On the results screen, **Previous missed** and **Next missed** page through the ones you missed, and **Play again** reshuffles the projects for a practice run.

### Good to Know

- Mastery is 5 of 6 correct on the first try, on the first run. Practice runs give the same feedback but do not change the first-run score.
- The projects are shuffled once per run. Nothing moves until you act, and the MicroSim does not store your answers.

## Lesson Plan

### Learning Objective

The learner will attribute the weakness of each of six project descriptions to the one essential element it lacks, correctly attributing at least five of six on the first attempt. (Bloom level: Analyze.)

### Audience

Teachers, school administrators, school board members, education policymakers, and parents.

### Duration

10-15 minutes

### Prerequisites

- The seven essential elements of a project, the driving question, and the public exhibition, from the section "Projects" in Chapter 14.

### Activities

1. **Predict (2 min).** Before starting, list the six elements on paper. Which one do you think school projects most often skip?
2. **Attribute (8 min).** Work through the six projects. For each one, check the elements one at a time before you choose.
3. **Discuss (5 min).** Pick a project you have seen in a real school. Which element was weakest, and what one change would add it?

### Assessment

Ask learners to answer without the MicroSim, then check each answer by using it.

1. Students build a model to a teacher's exact plans and show it at a fair. Which element is missing? *(Student voice and choice. Every decision was made by the teacher.)*
2. A project uses invented numbers from a worksheet but is otherwise strong. Which element is missing? *(Authenticity. The data and the problem are not real.)*
3. Does producing a product make a project a good project? *(No. A project can produce something and still lack a driving question, feedback, or reflection.)*
4. Students present to the city council but never discuss what they learned. What is missing? *(Reflection.)*
