    Glossary of Terms¶

    2 Hour Learning¶

    The name of Alpha School's model and adaptive academic software, in which students spend about two hours a day on core academics.

    The same software runs at Unbound Academy, where results without in-person afternoons have been weaker.

    See also: Alpha School, Unbound Academy, Two-to-Three-Hour Learning

    50 Percent Time Horizon¶

    The task length, in human working time, at which an AI model succeeds on half of the attempted tasks, used by METR as its main capability measure.

    Example: A model that succeeds on half of the 30-minute tasks, more often on shorter tasks and less often on longer ones, has a 50 percent time horizon of about 30 minutes.

    See also: Task Horizon, Task Success Rate

    5R Permissions¶

    The five rights that define openness in educational resources: to retain, reuse, revise, remix, and redistribute the material.

    Example: A teacher keeps a copy of an open chapter, revises it, combines it with another, and shares the result with her district.

    See also: Open Educational Resource, Reusability Subscore

    A/B Testing¶

    Comparing two versions of a design by randomly assigning users to each and measuring which produces better results.

    Example: Half of students see hints after one wrong answer and half after two, and the system compares learning.

    See also: Randomized Controlled Trial, Program Evaluation

    Abstraction¶

    Focusing on the information that matters for a problem while setting aside details that do not.

    See also: Computational Thinking, Decomposition

    Academic Integrity¶

    Honesty and responsibility in academic work, including doing one's own work and giving credit for others' ideas.

    See also: Redesign Not Policing, AI Cheating Detector

    Academic Morning¶

    The first part of the AI-native school day, in which students work on core academic subjects through adaptive, mastery-based materials.

    Example: From 8:30 to 11:00, students work through math and reading in their adaptive textbooks with teachers circulating.

    See also: Compressed Academic Morning, Morning-Afternoon Model

    Achievement Gap¶

    A persistent difference in average academic performance between groups of students, such as groups defined by income, race, language, or disability.

    Example: A district finds that students from low-income families score on average well below their classmates on the state math test.

    See also: Educational Equity, Closing or Widening Gaps

    Active Listening¶

    Giving full attention to a speaker, checking understanding, and responding in ways that show the speaker's message was received.

    Example: A student restates a teammate's idea, "So you think the bridge needs a wider base?", before adding his own.

    See also: Communication Skills, Empathy

    Activity-Skill Matching¶

    Choosing afternoon activities deliberately so that, across a student's week, they build the full range of human skills rather than whatever happens to be available.

    Example: A school makes sure each student's week includes one team sport, one art, and one building project.

    See also: Pro-Social Activity, Afternoon Program

    Actor Verb Object¶

    The basic three-part structure of an xAPI statement: who did something, what they did, and what they did it to.

    See also: xAPI Statement

    Adaptation Without Live AI¶

    Personalizing learning with stored records, stated rules, and a learning graph, instead of running an AI model for each student interaction.

    Example: A rule that unlocks the next concept when a student reaches the mastery threshold needs no AI at run time.

    See also: Low-Cost Adaptive Design, Next-Concept Selection

    Adaptive Learning¶

    Instruction in which the content, sequence, difficulty, or pace changes in response to each student's demonstrated performance.

    Example: A student who answers three fraction questions correctly moves on, while one who misses them gets a review lesson.

    See also: Level 3 Adaptive Textbook, Mastery Learning

    ADKAR Ability¶

    The fourth element of the ADKAR model: being able to apply knowledge in actual daily work at the required level of performance.

    Example: A teacher can run small-group coaching smoothly while the rest of the class works in the adaptive textbook.

    See also: ADKAR Model, ADKAR Reinforcement

    ADKAR Awareness¶

    The first element of the ADKAR model: understanding why a change is needed and what risks come from not changing.

    Example: Teachers see data on how much class time is spent re-teaching material students already know.

    See also: ADKAR Model, ADKAR Desire

    ADKAR Desire¶

    The second element of the ADKAR model: a personal willingness to support and take part in a change.

    Example: A teacher becomes keen once she sees the new schedule gives her afternoons coaching the debate team.

    See also: ADKAR Model, ADKAR Knowledge

    ADKAR Knowledge¶

    The third element of the ADKAR model: knowing how to change, including the skills, processes, and information required.

    Example: Teachers attend workshops on reading mastery dashboards.

    See also: ADKAR Model, ADKAR Ability

    ADKAR Model¶

    Prosci's model of individual change, which holds that each person needs awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, and reinforcement, in that order, for a change to succeed.

    Example: A coach finds a teacher who knows how to use the new tool but does not yet want to, and works on desire.

    See also: ADKAR Awareness, ADKAR Reinforcement

    ADKAR Reinforcement¶

    The fifth element of the ADKAR model: the recognition, rewards, and structures that keep a change in place and prevent a return to old habits.

    Example: The principal highlights coaching successes at staff meetings and builds them into evaluations.

    See also: ADKAR Model, Refreeze Stage

    Adoption Readiness¶

    The degree to which a person, school, or system is prepared to take up an innovation, in terms of skills, resources, attitudes, and infrastructure.

    Example: A school with reliable Wi-Fi, trained staff, and a privacy policy is more ready than one without.

    See also: School Readiness Assessment, Governance Readiness

    Affordable Alpha Model¶

    The book's design for offering a short adaptive morning and a people-centered afternoon to every student at a public school budget, using open textbooks, MicroSims, learning graphs, xAPI, and a Learning Record Store instead of costly live AI.

    Example: A public district builds its morning on free Level 2 and Level 3 textbooks and spends its savings on afternoon staff.

    See also: Low-Cost Adaptive Design, Cost Barrier to AI Schools, Alpha School

    Afternoon Program¶

    The planned set of activities that fill the second part of the AI-native school day, after the academic morning.

    Example: A program offering robotics, orchestra, soccer, and a community garden project on rotating days.

    See also: Pro-Social Afternoon, Afternoon Program Quality

    Afternoon Program Quality¶

    How well the afternoon's activities achieve their goals, judged by clear purposes, trained adults, participation, safety, and evidence of skill growth.

    Example: A school reviews teamwork rubric scores and attendance each term to decide which activities to keep.

    See also: Structured Afternoon, Teamwork Rubric

    Afternoon Specialist Staff¶

    Adults other than classroom teachers, such as artists, musicians, coaches, makers, and community experts, who lead afternoon activities, often part time or under contract.

    They need background checks, training, and clear lines of supervision.

    See also: Staffing Model, Community Partnership

    Age-Appropriate AI Design¶

    The practice of fitting an AI system's content, interactions, data collection, and defaults to the developmental stage of the children who use it.

    Example: A tutor for eight-year-olds uses short sentences, avoids persuasive nudges, and sets privacy protections on by default.

    See also: Responsible AI with Minors

    Age-Based Grade Levels¶

    The practice of grouping students into classes by birth year, so that children of the same age study the same material at the same pace regardless of what each already knows.

    Example: A ten-year-old who already reads at an eighth-grade level is still placed in fifth-grade reading because of her age.

    Contrast with: Self-Paced Learning, Mastery Learning

    AI Agent¶

    An AI system that plans and carries out a sequence of actions toward a goal, using tools such as web search, files, or software, with limited human direction at each step.

    Example: An agent asked to build a quiz finds the chapter, writes the questions, formats them, and saves the file.

    See also: Long-Running Task, Task Horizon

    AI Benchmark¶

    A standardized set of tasks or questions used to measure and compare the performance of AI systems.

    Example: A benchmark of thousands of math word problems on which each new model's accuracy is reported.

    See also: Benchmark Task Suite, METR

    AI Capability¶

    The range and difficulty of tasks an AI system can complete correctly, as measured by tests, benchmarks, or real-world use.

    Example: METR measures AI capability by the length of tasks, in human working time, that a model can complete.

    See also: AI Benchmark, Task Horizon

    AI Cheating Detector¶

    Software that claims to estimate whether a piece of writing was produced by AI rather than by a student.

    Example: A teacher runs essays through a detector that labels one as "likely AI-written."

    See also: Detector False Positives, Stanford Detector Study

    AI Coaching Teachers¶

    Using AI to help teachers improve their practice, for example by offering live suggestions during tutoring or feedback on lesson plans.

    Example: In the Tutor CoPilot study, live AI suggestions to human tutors raised student mastery, most for the weakest tutors.

    See also: Tutor CoPilot, Instructional Coaching

    AI Energy Use¶

    The electricity consumed by training and running AI models.

    It matters because energy is a possible limit on AI growth and because a school's choice of AI tool has an environmental footprint.

    See also: Data Center Energy, Energy-Efficient Learning

    AI Guardrails¶

    Design limits built into an AI system to keep its behavior safe and suited to its purpose, such as refusing to give final answers or blocking inappropriate topics.

    Example: A math tutor that responds to "just tell me the answer" with a hint about the next step.

    See also: Hints Instead of Answers, Guided AI vs Open AI

    AI Hallucination¶

    Output from an AI system that is stated fluently and confidently but is false, invented, or unsupported by its sources.

    Example: A chatbot cites a research paper with a plausible title and authors that does not exist.

    See also: Confidently Wrong Answers, AI Tutor Failures

    AI in Education¶

    The use of artificial intelligence to support teaching, learning, assessment, and school operations.

    Example: AI tutors, automated feedback on writing, and adaptive practice systems are uses of AI in education.

    See also: AI Tutor, Intelligent Textbook

    AI Inference Cost¶

    The cost of running a trained AI model to produce an output, such as answering one question, usually charged per unit of text processed.

    Example: A district estimates the yearly cost of letting 12,000 students chat with a tutor for an hour each day.

    See also: Falling AI Costs, Selective Chatbot Use

    AI Literacy¶

    The knowledge and skills needed to understand what AI systems are, how they work in broad terms, what they can and cannot do, and how to use them responsibly.

    Example: Students learn to check a chatbot's answer against a reliable source before using it.

    See also: Staff AI Literacy, AI Hallucination

    AI Prediction for Schools¶

    The book's forecast that free, adaptive, interactive textbooks will soon be available to almost every student, freeing much of the school day now spent on direct instruction.

    Example: A principal uses the prediction to ask what her school would do with three extra hours each afternoon.

    See also: Three-to-Four-Year Forecast, Freed-Up School Time

    AI Tutor¶

    A software system that uses AI to guide a student through learning by explaining, asking questions, giving feedback, and adjusting to the student's responses.

    Example: The Harvard physics tutor guided students through problems step by step using proven teaching practices.

    See also: AI Tutor Strengths, AI Tutor Failures, Khanmigo

    AI Tutor Failures¶

    The ways an AI tutor can harm learning, such as giving wrong answers, doing the student's thinking for them, encouraging over-reliance, or being little used.

    Example: In the Turkish study, an open chatbot raised practice scores but lowered later exam scores.

    See also: Confidently Wrong Answers, Over-Reliance on AI

    AI Tutor Strengths¶

    The advantages an AI tutor offers, such as unlimited patience, availability at any hour, instant feedback, and the ability to adjust explanations to each student.

    Example: A student asks the same question five ways at 9 p.m. and gets a fresh explanation each time.

    See also: AI Tutor, Skills AI Teaches Well

    AI-First School¶

    A school that puts AI technology at the center of how it delivers instruction, often described by how much AI software students use.

    Example: Alpha School, where students spend about two hours a day on adaptive academic software.

    See also: AI-Native School, Benefits of AI-First Schools, Risks of AI-First Schools

    AI-Generated Content¶

    Text, images, questions, simulations, or other material produced in whole or in part by an AI system.

    In this book, such content is produced once and checked by people before students see it.

    See also: Generative AI, Generate Once Serve Many

    AI-Native School¶

    A school designed from the start around the expectation of capable, low-cost AI, using it to shorten academic instruction and reinvesting the freed time in human, social learning.

    Example: A new public school that opens with a two-and-a-half-hour adaptive morning and a staffed afternoon program.

    Contrast with: AI-First School

    See also: Morning-Afternoon Model

    AI-Native School Day¶

    The daily schedule that results from the morning-afternoon model, including the adaptive morning, screen limits, a curiosity block, and the afternoon program.

    Example: 8:30 to 11:00 adaptive academics with breaks, 11:00 to 11:30 curiosity block, lunch, then two hours of projects and arts.

    See also: Morning-Afternoon Model, Schedule Redesign

    AI-Powered Marketing Claims¶

    Promotional statements that a product uses AI or is "AI-powered," often without saying what the AI does or what level of capability it delivers.

    Example: A flashcard app labeled "AI-powered" that only shuffles cards in a fixed order.

    See also: Vendor Level Claim, Hype Cycle

    Algorithmic Bias¶

    Systematic and unfair differences in how a computer system treats or affects different groups of people, arising from its data, design, or use.

    Example: AI writing detectors flag essays by English-language learners as AI-written far more often than essays by native speakers.

    See also: Algorithmic Bias Auditing, Bias Against ELL Writers

    Algorithmic Bias Auditing¶

    Systematic testing of an automated system to find whether its outcomes differ unfairly across groups of users.

    Example: A district checks whether an adaptive system moves boys and girls, or English learners and native speakers, ahead at similar rates when their scores are similar.

    See also: Algorithmic Bias, Algorithmic Transparency

    Algorithmic Thinking¶

    Designing a clear, ordered series of steps that solves a problem or completes a task and can be followed the same way each time.

    See also: Computational Thinking

    Algorithmic Transparency¶

    The degree to which the rules, data, and reasoning behind an automated decision are disclosed and understandable to the people affected.

    Example: A vendor publishes the mastery threshold and next-concept rules its adaptive system uses.

    See also: Algorithmic Bias Auditing, Adaptation Without Live AI

    AllHere Collapse¶

    The 2024 failure of AllHere, the startup building LA Unified's "Ed" chatbot, within months of launch, followed by fraud charges against its founder in November 2024.

    It shows why schools need vendor evaluation and procurement rules before buying AI products.

    See also: LAUSD Ed Chatbot, Vendor Risk

    Alpha Adult Guides¶

    The adults at Alpha School who support students' motivation, effort, and progress in the morning and lead workshops and projects in the afternoon, instead of lecturing.

    Their role raises the question of how much of Alpha's results come from the adults rather than the software.

    See also: Learning Guide, Alpha School

    Alpha School¶

    A private school network, begun in Austin, Texas, in 2014, whose students spend about two hours a day on adaptive academic software and the rest of the day on life-skills workshops and projects led by adult guides.

    It shows the morning-afternoon structure can work, but at private-school prices.

    See also: Alpha Tuition Range, Alpha Adult Guides, 2 Hour Learning

    Alpha Tuition Range¶

    Alpha School's yearly tuition, about 40,000 dollars at the original Austin campus to about 65,000 dollars at newer campuses such as Boston, Miami, and New York.

    The range shows why the book seeks a lower-cost version of the same structure.

    See also: Alpha School, Cost Barrier to AI Schools, Affordable Alpha Model

    AltSchool¶

    A network of "personalized learning" microschools launched in Silicon Valley in 2013 on its own software, which raised more than 170 million dollars, closed its schools within a few years, and shifted to selling software.

    Its lesson is that technology-led personalization without a lasting school culture does not last.

    See also: Software Is Not the Model, Microschool

    Anchoring in Culture¶

    Making a change last by connecting new practices to the organization's values, stories, hiring, and leadership, the final Kotter step.

    Example: The school hires new teachers specifically for coaching skill and celebrates the afternoon program in its traditions.

    See also: Kotter's Eight Steps, Sustaining Change

    Applying Thinking on Teams¶

    Using decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithmic thinking, evidence evaluation, and argument analysis inside group work, so a team decides with reasons rather than by volume.

    Example: A team settles a design dispute by testing both versions and comparing the data.

    See also: Team Problem Solving, Computational Thinking

    Argument Analysis¶

    Breaking an argument into its claim, reasons, and evidence, and checking whether the reasoning holds.

    Example: A debate team identifies the hidden assumption in the other side's main point.

    See also: Critical Thinking, Evidence Evaluation

    Artificial Intelligence¶

    The field of computing concerned with building systems that perform tasks normally associated with human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognizing patterns, reasoning, and making decisions.

    See also: Machine Learning, Generative AI

    Arts Education¶

    Instruction in music, dance, theater, visual arts, and other art forms, covering both creating and appreciating them.

    See also: Music Education, Visual Arts

    Assessment Redesign¶

    Changing what, how, and when student learning is assessed to fit new goals and tools.

    Example: A school replaces take-home essays with in-class writing, oral defenses, and portfolios.

    See also: Redesign Not Policing, Combined Assessment Plan

    Basic Assumptions¶

    In Schein's model, the deepest layer of culture: unspoken, taken-for-granted beliefs that guide behavior without being questioned.

    Example: Staff assume that a quiet classroom is a well-managed one.

    See also: Schein's Culture Model, Mental Models

    Beachhead Strategy¶

    Focusing first on one narrow, well-defined group of adopters and serving it completely before expanding to others.

    Example: A district starts with ninth-grade algebra in two schools and expands only after success there.

    See also: Crossing the Chasm, Pilot Program

    Behavioral Data¶

    Information about how a person acts while using a system, such as clicks, pauses, time on task, and patterns of activity.

    See also: Detailed Learning History, Data Minimization

    Bell Schedule¶

    The fixed timetable of periods, passing times, and breaks that structures a school day, usually signaled by bells or tones.

    See also: Block Scheduling, Schedule Redesign

    Belonging¶

    The feeling of being accepted, valued, and included as a member of a group or community.

    Example: A student who joins the school orchestra says it is the first place she feels she fits in.

    See also: Human Connection, El Sistema

    Benchmark Task Suite¶

    The specific collection of tasks that a benchmark runs, ideally spanning many difficulties and lengths and each with a clear way to judge success.

    Example: METR's suite ranges from tasks a person finishes in seconds to tasks that take many hours.

    See also: AI Benchmark, METR

    Benefits of AI-First Schools¶

    The positive results AI-centered schools can produce, such as faster mastery, personalized pacing, more time for projects and relationships, and support for teachers.

    Example: The Harvard physics tutor helped students learn more in less time than an active-learning class.

    See also: Risks of AI-First Schools, Strengthen the Positives

    Bias Against ELL Writers¶

    The finding that AI-writing detectors are more likely to flag the writing of English-language learners as AI-generated than the writing of native speakers.

    Example: In a 2023 Stanford study, more than half of non-native speakers' essays were wrongly labeled as AI-written on average.

    See also: Stanford Detector Study, Algorithmic Bias

    Block Scheduling¶

    A schedule in which the day is divided into fewer, longer class periods, often 80 to 120 minutes, instead of many short ones.

    See also: Schedule Redesign, Bell Schedule

    Bloom's Taxonomy¶

    A hierarchy of cognitive processes, revised in 2001, that orders learning goals from remembering and understanding through applying, analyzing, and evaluating to creating.

    Example: A teacher writes one quiz question asking students to recall a definition and another asking them to evaluate two competing arguments.

    See also: Higher-Order Thinking, Learning Outcome

    Bloom's Two-Sigma Problem¶

    Benjamin Bloom's 1984 finding that students tutored one-on-one with mastery methods performed about two standard deviations better than students in conventional classes, and his challenge to find group methods that come close.

    Example: AI tutors are often presented as a possible answer to this challenge.

    See also: One-on-One Tutoring, Effect Size

    Board Presentation¶

    A formal presentation to a school board explaining a proposal, its evidence, costs, and risks, usually to seek approval.

    Example: A principal presents pilot results and a cost model to the board before requesting expansion.

    See also: School Board Recommendation, School Board

    Bridges' Transition Model¶

    William Bridges' model of the inner, psychological process people go through during change: endings, a neutral zone, and new beginnings.

    Example: A principal holds a session to honor the old schedule before introducing the new one.

    See also: Endings and Losses, Neutral Zone, Change Versus Transition

    Bright Spots¶

    Examples of success already happening within a system, which can be studied and copied.

    Example: A principal finds the one classroom where afternoon projects are working well and asks that teacher to share her methods.

    See also: Positive Deviance, Direct the Rider

    Browser-Based Simulation¶

    An interactive model that runs entirely inside a web browser, requiring no installation and often working offline once loaded.

    Example: A circuit simulation that works the same on a Chromebook, a tablet, and a home laptop.

    See also: MicroSim, Web Browser

    Budget Reallocation¶

    Moving funds from existing expenses to new priorities without increasing the total budget.

    Example: A district shifts textbook purchase money to pay afternoon specialist staff after adopting free open textbooks.

    See also: Public School Funding, Cost Modeling

    Building on Change¶

    Using the credibility gained from early wins to tackle bigger problems, instead of declaring victory too soon.

    Example: After the pilot succeeds in one grade, the school uses its results to redesign the whole schedule.

    See also: Short-Term Wins, Scaling a Pilot

    Capacity Building¶

    Developing the knowledge, skills, resources, and relationships that people and organizations need to carry out a change.

    Example: A district funds two years of coaching for every teacher moving to the new model.

    See also: Professional Development, Instructional Coaching

    Carnegie Unit¶

    A standard measure of high school credit, defined as about 120 hours of class contact time in one subject over a school year, that ties credit to time spent rather than mastery.

    See also: Seat Time

    Case Study Method¶

    An approach to research and teaching that examines one real situation in depth, describing what happened and drawing transferable lessons from it.

    Example: Studying the inBloom shutdown to understand why student data projects need family trust.

    See also: Real Case Study, Lesson From a Case

    Causal Loop Diagram¶

    A diagram that shows how variables in a system influence one another, using arrows marked as increasing or decreasing, to reveal feedback loops.

    Example: A diagram linking teacher workload, burnout, turnover, and new-teacher training needs.

    See also: Feedback Loop, Systems Thinking

    Centralized Student Records¶

    Student information for many schools or districts stored together in one system or by one provider.

    Centralizing records can simplify management but makes the store a larger target.

    See also: PowerSchool Data Breach, Data Breach

    Change Agent¶

    A person who deliberately works to bring about change within an organization by influencing others' decisions and actions.

    Example: An instructional coach hired to help teachers move to the morning-afternoon model.

    See also: Opinion Leader, Guiding Coalition

    Change Fatigue¶

    Exhaustion, cynicism, and disengagement that develop when people face too many changes, too often, without seeing results.

    Example: Teachers greet a new initiative with "this too shall pass" after five programs in four years.

    See also: Coherence Framework, Teacher Burnout

    Change Stage¶

    The middle stage of Lewin's model, in which people learn and try out new behaviors, processes, and ways of thinking.

    Example: Teachers run the new morning schedule for a semester with coaching support.

    See also: Lewin's Change Model, Implementation Dip

    Change Versus Transition¶

    Bridges' distinction between change, the external event or new situation, and transition, the internal psychological process people go through to adjust to it.

    Example: The new schedule starts on September 1 (change), but teachers may take a year to feel at home in it (transition).

    See also: Bridges' Transition Model

    Change Vision¶

    A clear, concise description of the future a change effort aims to create, used to guide decisions and motivate people, the third of Kotter's steps.

    Example: "By 2029 every student will finish core academics by noon and spend afternoons building, performing, and serving."

    See also: Communicating the Vision, Kotter's Eight Steps

    Changing Teacher Role¶

    The shift in a teacher's main work from delivering content to coaching, mentoring, and facilitating, as adaptive materials take on more of instruction.

    Example: A former lecturer now spends mornings coaching small groups and afternoons leading a robotics team.

    See also: Teacher as Coach, Learning Guide, Reduced Direct Instruction

    Charter School¶

    A publicly funded, tuition-free school that operates independently of the local district under a contract, or charter, granting it freedom over methods in exchange for accountability for results.

    Example: Unbound Academy is an online charter school in Arizona approved by the state charter board.

    See also: Virtual School, Unbound Academy

    Chatbot¶

    A software program that carries on a conversation with a user in natural language, through text or speech.

    See also: Level 4 Chatbot Textbook, Conversational Tutoring

    Choosing a Change Model¶

    Selecting the model of change, or the combination of models, that fits a particular school's size, culture, history, and type of change.

    Example: A small school uses ADKAR to support individual teachers and Kotter's steps to organize the overall plan.

    See also: Kotter's Eight Steps, ADKAR Model

    Classification Framework¶

    A structured scheme that sorts items into defined categories using clear criteria, so different people sort the same item the same way.

    See also: Five-Level Textbook Model, SAE J3016 Standard

    Classroom¶

    The physical or virtual room in which a group of students and one or more adults meet for instruction and learning activities.

    See also: School, Direct Instruction

    Closing or Widening Gaps¶

    The question of whether a change makes the difference in outcomes between groups of students smaller or larger.

    A tool that helps everyone on average can still widen gaps if it helps advantaged students more.

    See also: Achievement Gap, Equity of Access

    Clubs and Extracurriculars¶

    Organized activities outside regular academic classes, such as clubs, teams, publications, and student government.

    See also: Afternoon Program

    Coding Club¶

    A group in which students meet to learn programming and build software projects together.

    See also: Robotics Club, Clubs and Extracurriculars

    Cognitive Offloading¶

    Using an external tool or resource to reduce the mental effort a task requires, such as writing a list or asking an AI to solve a problem.

    It can free the mind for other work, but it can also prevent the effort that builds learning.

    See also: Over-Reliance on AI, Your Brain on ChatGPT

    Coherence Framework¶

    Fullan and Quinn's model for aligning a school system around a small number of connected priorities, collaborative cultures, deep learning, and shared accountability.

    Example: A district drops five unrelated initiatives to focus on the morning-afternoon model and its supports.

    See also: Fullan's Educational Change, Change Fatigue

    Collaboration¶

    Working jointly with others toward a common outcome, sharing ideas, effort, and responsibility.

    See also: Teamwork, Shared Goals

    Combined Assessment Plan¶

    A school's written plan for how its different forms of assessment fit together: what is measured, with what tool, how often, and who sees the results.

    Example: Morning mastery data, afternoon teamwork rubrics, portfolios, and required state tests in one plan.

    See also: Assessment Redesign, Mastery-Based Assessment

    Communicating Before Rumors¶

    Telling families and staff about a change before they hear about it elsewhere, because rumors are far harder to correct than a clear first statement.

    Example: The principal sends a letter about the pilot the week before it appears on the board agenda.

    See also: Family Communication, Community Communication

    Communicating the Vision¶

    Repeatedly sharing a change vision through every available channel, and through leaders' own behavior, so it is widely understood and accepted.

    Example: The principal explains the new model at every staff meeting, family night, and newsletter for a year.

    See also: Change Vision, Family Communication

    Communication Skills¶

    The abilities needed to exchange ideas clearly and effectively with others, including speaking, writing, listening, and reading nonverbal cues.

    Example: A student explains her team's design to visiting parents and answers their questions.

    See also: Active Listening, Public Exhibition

    Community Communication¶

    Sharing information about a school's plans and results with the wider public, including residents, local media, businesses, and community groups.

    Example: A district hosts a public forum and posts a plain-language summary of its AI pilot online.

    See also: Family Communication, Board Presentation

    Community Partnership¶

    A working relationship between a school and outside organizations, such as businesses, nonprofits, museums, or colleges, that share resources or expertise.

    Example: A local theater company sends actors to lead the school's afternoon drama program.

    See also: Afternoon Specialist Staff, Service Learning

    Compatibility¶

    The degree to which an innovation is seen as consistent with the existing values, experiences, and needs of potential adopters.

    Example: A tool that works inside the learning management system teachers already use fits their routines.

    See also: Five Innovation Attributes

    Complexity¶

    The degree to which an innovation is seen as difficult to understand and use, which slows its adoption.

    See also: Five Innovation Attributes

    Composite Story¶

    A fictional account assembled from patterns seen in many real situations, with invented characters, used to make an idea concrete.

    Example: The story of Maya, an invented student whose day combines experiences reported in many schools.

    Contrast with: Real Case Study

    See also: Two-Sided Story

    Compounding Small Changes¶

    The way a series of modest improvements, each building on the last, adds up over time to a large total change.

    Example: Improving by one percent a week compounds to about 68 percent better over a year.

    Level 4 Cost Per Student¶

    The yearly cost per student of a chatbot textbook, which adds the ongoing cost of running a large language model for each student conversation.

    See also: AI Inference Cost, Selective Chatbot Use

    Level 5 Autonomous Textbook¶

    Lewin's Change Model¶

    Kurt Lewin's three-stage model of change: unfreezing current habits, moving to new ones, and refreezing the new habits so they last.

    Maker Space¶

    Managing Expectations¶

    Mastery Data¶

    Evidence, drawn from a student's stored learning record, of which concepts the student has demonstrated they know.

    The level of performance a student needs to reach to be considered as having learned a concept, such as a minimum score or a number of correct answers in a row.

    Assessment that judges whether a student has reached a defined level of understanding on each skill or concept, rather than averaging scores over time.

    Answering resistance by first listening to and acknowledging people's feelings and concerns, before explaining or persuading.

    Reusability Subscore¶

    The half of the OpenBook QR Score, from 0 to 50, formed by summing five metrics that show how easily others can find, change, and reuse a resource.

    Skills AI Teaches Well¶

    Abilities with clear standards for correct work that can be practiced many times in small steps with instant, specific feedback, such as arithmetic, grammar, vocabulary, and coding syntax.

    Social Constructivism¶

    A theory of learning, associated with Lev Vygotsky, that holds understanding develops through interaction with other people and the shared language and culture of a group.

    Socratic Questioning¶

    A teaching method in which a tutor leads a student to understanding through a sequence of questions that prompt the student to examine and reason, rather than by telling.

    The lesson that adaptive software alone does not reproduce a school model's results without the culture, adults, community, and afternoon program around it.

    Sources of Resistance¶

    The underlying reasons people oppose a change, such as fear of loss, lack of understanding, past bad experiences, disagreement with goals, or overload.

    Example: A teacher opposes the new schedule because a previous reform cost her two years of extra work and then vanished.

    See also: Resistance to Change, Change Fatigue

    Spaced Practice¶

    A study strategy in which practice on a skill or topic is spread out over time, with gaps between sessions, rather than crowded into one session.

    Example: An adaptive textbook brings back fraction problems two days, one week, and one month after a student first masters them.

    See also: Retrieval Practice

    Sports and Athletics¶

    Organized physical activities and competitions that build fitness, skill, and discipline.

    See also: Team Sports

    Staff AI Literacy¶

    The understanding that teachers, administrators, and other school staff need about what AI tools can and cannot do, and how to use them responsibly with students.

    Example: All staff complete training on spotting AI hallucinations and on the district's data rules.

    See also: AI Literacy, Professional Development

    Staffing Model¶

    The plan for which roles a school employs, how many of each, and how their time is assigned across students and activities.

    Example: A school employs academic coaches for the morning and a mix of teachers and part-time specialists for the afternoon.

    See also: Afternoon Specialist Staff, Hiring for New Roles

    Stages of Concern¶

    The CBAM sequence of feelings and questions people have about an innovation, moving roughly from self concerns to task concerns to impact concerns.

    Example: A teacher first asks "What will this mean for my job?", later "How do I fit it in?", and finally "Is it helping students?"

    See also: Self Concerns, Task Concerns, Impact Concerns

    Stakeholder¶

    Any person or group with an interest in, or influence over, a change.

    Example: Students, teachers, parents, administrators, board members, unions, and local employers are stakeholders in a schedule redesign.

    See also: Stakeholder Engagement, Stakeholder Adoption Map

    Stakeholder Adoption Map¶

    A simple chart listing each interested group, where it stands on the adoption curve, and what its main concerns are, used to plan engagement.

    Example: A map shows parents of high achievers as early majority worried about rigor, and the union as late majority worried about jobs.

    See also: Stakeholder, Technology Adoption Curve, Matching Strategy to Adopter

    Stakeholder Engagement¶

    Involving interested people and groups in planning and carrying out a change, so they shape it, understand it, and support it.

    Example: A design committee of teachers, parents, and students helps plan the afternoon program.

    See also: Stakeholder, Student Voice

    Standardized Testing¶

    Assessment in which all test takers answer the same or equivalent questions under the same conditions and are scored the same way, so results can be compared across students, schools, and years.

    See also: Summative Assessment, Achievement Gap

    Stanford Detector Study¶

    A 2023 study by Weixin Liang and colleagues at Stanford finding that popular AI-writing detectors often wrongly flagged essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated.

    Its lesson is that fighting AI can create equity problems, so schools redesign assignments instead of relying on detection.

    See also: Bias Against ELL Writers, Redesign Not Policing

    Static Website¶

    A website made of prebuilt pages that are delivered to every visitor exactly as stored, without being generated by a server program for each request.

    Example: An intelligent textbook built with MkDocs and published as static pages on GitHub Pages.

    See also: Web Page Hosting, Generate Once Serve Many

    Storytelling¶

    Using narratives about people and events to explain ideas, convey values, and persuade.

    This book uses real and composite stories to show both the benefits and risks of AI-first schools.

    See also: Real Case Study, Composite Story

    Strengthen the Positives¶

    The book's goal of deliberately reinforcing and spreading the benefits that AI-first schooling can bring.

    Example: A school that sees gains from guided tutoring extends it to more subjects.

    See also: Reduce the Negatives, Benefits of AI-First Schools

    Structured Afternoon¶

    An afternoon with clear goals for each activity, a planned sequence, trained adults, and regular reflection, so students know what they are doing and why.

    Example: A robotics block with a weekly build goal, a coach, and a five-minute reflection at the end.

    Contrast with: Unstructured Afternoon Drift

    See also: Afternoon Program Quality

    Student¶

    A person enrolled in a school or course of study who is expected to acquire knowledge and skills under the guidance of teachers and other adults.

    See also: Student Agency, Student Voice

    Student Agency¶

    A student's capacity and opportunity to make meaningful choices about what, how, and when they learn, and to take responsibility for those choices.

    Example: A student picks which of three science projects to pursue and sets her own weekly mastery goals.

    See also: Student Goal Setting, Student Voice

    Student Boredom¶

    A state of low interest and restlessness that arises when learning tasks feel too easy, too hard, repetitive, or disconnected from a student's interests.

    Example: A student who already understands percentages sits through a week of lessons on them and stops paying attention.

    Contrast with: Student Engagement, Curiosity

    Student Data¶

    Any information collected about a student, including identity, enrollment, grades, attendance, learning activity, and behavior.

    See also: Personally Identifiable Data, Student Data Privacy

    Student Data Privacy¶

    The protection of student information from collection, use, or disclosure beyond what is necessary, lawful, and agreed to by families.

    Example: A district contract forbids a vendor from selling or using student data for advertising.

    See also: FERPA, Data Governance, Privacy Inflection Point

    Student Engagement¶

    The degree of attention, interest, effort, and emotional investment that a student brings to learning activities.

    Example: Students who keep working on a bridge design through lunch because they want to see whether it holds show high engagement.

    See also: Intrinsic Motivation, Student Boredom

    Student Goal Setting¶

    The process in which a student, often with an adult, chooses specific learning targets and a timeline for reaching them.

    Example: A student sets a goal to master all ten linear-equation concepts by the end of the month.

    See also: Student Agency, Progress Tracking

    Student Progress Dashboard¶

    A visual display that summarizes a student's mastery, activity, and progress toward goals for the student, teachers, or families.

    Example: A screen showing which concepts a student has mastered in green and which need review in yellow.

    See also: Progress Tracking, Learning Analytics

    Student Voice¶

    Students' views, preferences, and input on their education, and the opportunities they have to influence decisions.

    Example: The Summit Learning walkouts showed students telling their districts what was wrong with long hours on screens.

    See also: Student Walkouts, Student Agency

    Student Walkouts¶

    Protests in which students leave class or school; in this book, the 2018-2019 Brooklyn walkouts against Summit Learning over long screen hours and too little contact with teachers.

    The lesson for leaders is to listen to student voice before it turns into protest.

    See also: Summit Learning, Student Voice

    Student-Controlled Data¶

    An arrangement in which students, and their families for younger students, decide who can view, use, or keep their learning records.

    Example: A family grants a summer tutoring program access to the child's mastery data for three months.

    See also: Data Ownership, Learning Record Store

    Success Measures¶

    The indicators, set in advance, used to judge whether a program met its goals.

    Example: Mastery rates, attendance, family satisfaction, and teamwork rubric scores.

    See also: Pilot Goals, Program Evaluation

    Summative Assessment¶

    An evaluation given at the end of a unit, course, or year to measure what students have learned, usually recorded as a grade or score.

    Contrast with: Formative Assessment

    See also: Standardized Testing

    Summit Learning¶

    A personalized-learning platform backed by Facebook engineers and rolled out to many schools, which in 2018 and 2019 drew student walkouts in Brooklyn and family protests in Kansas over screen time and little teacher contact.

    Its lesson is that the AI morning needs to stay short and the afternoon truly with people.

    See also: Student Walkouts, Morning Screen-Time Limits

    Sustaining Change¶

    Keeping a new practice in place and improving over years, after initial enthusiasm, funding, and leadership attention fade.

    Example: A school keeps its afternoon program running after the principal who started it leaves.

    See also: Policy Embedding, Anchoring in Culture

    Switch Framework¶

    Chip and Dan Heath's model of change, which says lasting change requires directing the rational mind (the Rider), motivating the emotional mind (the Elephant), and shaping the environment (the Path).

    Example: A principal gives clear steps, shares a moving student story, and rearranges the schedule to make the change easier.

    See also: Direct the Rider, Motivate the Elephant, Shape the Path

    Systems Thinking¶

    A way of understanding a situation by looking at how its parts interact over time, including feedback and delays, rather than at each part alone.

    Example: A board sees that cutting arts programs lowered attendance, which lowered funding, which led to further cuts.

    See also: Feedback Loop, Causal Loop Diagram

    Task Concerns¶

    CBAM concerns focused on the practical management of an innovation, such as time, logistics, and materials.

    Example: "How do I run coaching groups while the rest of the class works?"

    See also: Stages of Concern

    Task Horizon¶

    The length of task, measured in how long it takes a skilled human, that an AI system can complete successfully.

    Example: If a model reliably finishes tasks that take an expert one hour, its task horizon is about one hour.

    See also: 50 Percent Time Horizon, Human Working Time

    Task Success Rate¶

    The share of attempted tasks that an AI system completes correctly, usually reported as a percentage.

    See also: 50 Percent Time Horizon

    Teacher¶

    A professional who plans learning experiences, explains ideas, guides practice, assesses progress, and supports the growth of students.

    In an AI-native school the teacher's role shifts from lecturer toward coach, mentor, and facilitator.

    See also: Changing Teacher Role, Teacher as Coach, Teacher Workload

    Teacher as Coach¶

    A teacher role focused on helping students set goals, practice, and improve through specific feedback and encouragement, much as a sports coach does.

    Example: A teacher reviews a student's mastery data and helps her plan how to tackle her weakest concept.

    See also: Changing Teacher Role, Teacher as Mentor

    Teacher as Facilitator¶

    A teacher role focused on organizing activities, resources, and group processes so students learn through their own work and discussion.

    Example: A teacher sets up a project, assigns team roles, and steps in only when groups get stuck.

    See also: Changing Teacher Role, Project-Based Learning

    Teacher as Mentor¶

    A teacher role built on a long-term relationship in which the adult guides and supports a student's growth as a person as well as a learner.

    Example: An advisory teacher who stays with the same students for three years.

    See also: Teacher as Coach, Human Connection

    Teacher Burnout¶

    A state of emotional, physical, and mental exhaustion in teachers caused by prolonged stress, high workload, and limited support, often leading to reduced effectiveness or leaving the profession.

    Example: A district notices that a third of its new teachers leave within three years, citing exhaustion.

    See also: Teacher Workload, Change Fatigue

    Teacher Champion¶

    A teacher who supports an innovation early, uses it well, and helps colleagues adopt it through example and encouragement.

    Example: A respected math teacher who runs the pilot and invites others to visit her classroom.

    See also: Opinion Leader, Pilot Classroom

    Teacher Union¶

    An organization that represents teachers in bargaining with school districts over pay, working conditions, and job protections.

    See also: Union Engagement, Fear of Job Loss

    Teacher Workload¶

    The total amount of work required of a teacher, including teaching, planning, grading, communicating with families, meetings, and administrative duties.

    Example: A teacher who spends six hours each weekend grading essays has a heavy workload that AI-assisted feedback could reduce.

    See also: Teacher Burnout, AI Coaching Teachers

    Teaching Thinking with AI¶

    Using AI tutors and tools to help students practice thinking skills, such as breaking down problems and evaluating evidence, rather than only to deliver content.

    Example: An AI tutor asks a student to split a word problem into steps before solving any of them.

    See also: Computational Thinking, Skills AI Teaches Well

    Team Learning¶

    The process by which a group develops its shared capacity through dialogue, reflection, and joint practice.

    Example: A grade-level team meets weekly to watch videos of each other's coaching and discuss what worked.

    See also: Professional Learning Community, Learning Organization

    Team Problem Solving¶

    The process in which a group defines a problem, generates options, chooses a solution, and tests it together.

    Example: A team whose model car keeps veering left tests the wheels one by one to find the cause.

    See also: Applying Thinking on Teams, Collaboration

    Team Roles¶

    The distinct responsibilities assigned to members of a group, such as leader, recorder, builder, or presenter, so work is divided and coordinated.

    See also: Teamwork

    Team Sports¶

    Sports in which players work together as a team toward a shared objective against an opposing team.

    See also: Sports and Athletics, Teamwork

    Teamwork¶

    The coordinated effort of a group whose members take on different roles and depend on each other to reach a common goal.

    See also: Collaboration, Team Roles, Teamwork Rubric

    Teamwork Rubric¶

    A scoring guide that describes levels of performance on collaboration skills, such as contributing, listening, resolving conflict, and meeting commitments.

    Example: Students and coaches rate each team member from "beginning" to "exemplary" on five teamwork criteria.

    See also: Teamwork, Afternoon Program Quality

    Technology Adoption Curve¶

    A bell-shaped model dividing adopters of a new technology into innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards, according to when they take it up.

    Example: A principal sorts her staff along the curve to plan who to invite to a pilot first.

    See also: S-Curve of Adoption, Innovators

    Textbook¶

    A book or organized set of materials written to teach a subject, arranged in a sequence for use by students in a course.

    See also: Print Textbook, Intelligent Textbook

    Textbook Price¶

    The amount a school or student pays to buy or license a textbook or its digital access.

    See also: Free Textbook, Cost Per Student

    Textbook Publisher¶

    A company that develops, produces, markets, and sells textbooks and related instructional materials to schools and students.

    See also: Publisher Business Model, Publisher Disruption

    Textbook Quality¶

    The degree to which a textbook is accurate, clear, well organized, accessible, aligned to learning goals, and effective at helping students learn.

    Example: A review team checks a book for factual errors, readability, and whether its practice questions match the standards.

    See also: OpenBook QR Score, Quality Subscore

    The Chasm¶

    Geoffrey Moore's term for the gap between early adopters, who accept an incomplete product for its promise, and the early majority, who want a proven, complete solution.

    Example: An AI tutor loved by a few enthusiastic teachers stalls when ordinary classrooms find it hard to set up.

    See also: Crossing the Chasm, Pragmatist Buyers

    Theater¶

    The art of performing stories live before an audience through acting, speech, movement, and staging.

    Example: A school play in which students act, build sets, run lights, and manage the stage.

    See also: Arts Education, Teamwork

    Three-to-Four-Year Forecast¶

    The book's central prediction that within three to four years almost every student will have access to a free, high-quality, interactive textbook that adapts to their needs.

    Example: A board planning a schedule change for 2029 uses this forecast to ask what the morning should look like by then.

    See also: AI Prediction for Schools, Planning Window

    Total Cost of Ownership¶

    The full cost of a technology over its life, including purchase, setup, training, support, maintenance, data handling, and eventual replacement or exit.

    Example: A cheap tablet program becomes expensive once cases, repairs, management software, and replacement in year four are included.

    See also: Cost Modeling, Vendor Lock-In

    Trend Extrapolation¶

    Forecasting future values by extending a pattern observed in past data, on the assumption that the pattern continues.

    Example: Extending the five-month doubling line on a log-scale chart to estimate AI task horizons in 2029.

    See also: Forecast Uncertainty, Prediction Assumptions

    Trialability¶

    The degree to which an innovation can be tried on a limited basis before committing to it fully.

    Example: A teacher tries one MicroSim in one lesson before deciding whether to use more.

    See also: Five Innovation Attributes, Pilot Classroom

    Trough of Disillusionment¶

    The stage of the hype cycle when a technology fails to meet inflated expectations and interest falls sharply.

    Example: After early failures, a district cancels its AI pilot and stops talking about AI altogether.

    See also: Hype Cycle, Plateau of Productivity

    Trust Paradox¶

    The tension in personalized learning that the system needs more student data to serve students well, yet families share more only when they trust it, and trust grows when the system holds back.

    The book's resolution is to collect little, say what is held, and build trust through consistent action.

    Example: A school that asks for less data at first gains families' confidence and later earns permission for more.

    See also: Family Trust, Data Minimization

    Turkish High School Study¶

    A 2024 Wharton study (Bastani et al.) in which Turkish high school students with open GPT-4 access did better on practice but about 17 percent worse on later unaided exams, while a hints-only version removed most of the harm.

    It is the book's thesis in one experiment: design determines whether AI helps or hurts.

    See also: Hints Instead of Answers, Guided AI vs Open AI, Design Over Raw AI Power

    Tutor CoPilot¶

    A 2024 Stanford study of a tool that gave human tutors live AI suggestions during sessions, which improved student mastery, especially for the least experienced tutors, at small cost per tutor.

    Its lesson is that AI that makes people better at helping students works well.

    See also: AI Coaching Teachers, Teacher as Coach

    Two-Sided Story¶

    A short composite narrative that shows, in one story, a benefit growing stronger and a risk being reduced, along with the school's response.

    Example: A story of one day in an AI-native school in which a student nearly over-relies on the tutor and a coach redirects her.

    See also: Composite Story, Risk-and-Response Framework

    Two-to-Three-Hour Learning¶

    The claim, central to the book, that students using interactive adaptive textbooks can reach today's learning results in two to three hours of academic work a day.

    Example: A school plans its academic morning around two and a half hours of mastery-based work.

    See also: Compressed Academic Morning, 2 Hour Learning

    Unbound Academy¶

    A free online Arizona charter school for grades 4-8, approved by a 4-3 state board vote, that uses Alpha's 2 Hour Learning software without in-person afternoons or adult guides; reported early proficiency was below state averages.

    Its lesson is that the morning software alone is not the whole model.

    See also: 2 Hour Learning, Software Is Not the Model, Virtual School

    Unfreeze Stage¶

    The first stage of Lewin's model, in which an organization loosens the habits, beliefs, and routines that hold the present way in place, by showing a gap and building psychological safety.

    Example: Staff study their own data showing students bored through hours of repeated lessons.

    See also: Lewin's Change Model, Sense of Urgency

    Union Engagement¶

    Working with a teachers' union early and openly on the design and terms of a change.

    Example: The district invites union leaders onto the planning committee for the new schedule.

    See also: Teacher Union, Stakeholder Engagement

    Universal Textbook Access¶

    The condition in which every student can use a high-quality textbook for every course, regardless of family income or school budget.

    Example: A state provides free interactive textbooks for all core high school subjects to every public school student.

    See also: Penny-Per-Day Vision, Equity of Access

    Unstructured Afternoon Drift¶

    The slide of afternoon time into unplanned free time, passive supervision, or screens, in which adults watch students but nothing is taught.

    The book calls it the main risk of the whole model.

    Contrast with: Structured Afternoon

    See also: Freed-Up School Time

    Value Beyond Content¶

    What remains worth paying for once instructional content is cheap, such as quality assurance, alignment to standards, teacher support, assessment, data services, and integration.

    Example: A district pays a vendor for teacher training and alignment reviews while using free open textbooks.

    See also: Publisher Business Model, Whole Product

    Vendor Evaluation¶

    Assessing a supplier's product, financial stability, track record, security, and terms before buying from it.

    Example: A district checks a startup's funding, references, and security audit before signing.

    See also: Vendor Risk, Contract Due Diligence

    Vendor Level Claim¶

    A company's own statement of which level its product belongs to.

    Because the vendor has an interest in the claim, schools test it through product level assessment instead of accepting it.

    See also: Product Level Assessment, Self-Reported Results

    Vendor Lock-In¶

    A situation in which a school becomes so dependent on one supplier's product, data formats, or contracts that switching is costly or impractical.

    Example: A district cannot change platforms because years of student records exist only in one vendor's format.

    See also: Data Portability, Total Cost of Ownership

    Vendor Risk¶

    The possibility that a supplier will fail, mishandle data, change terms, or otherwise harm the school that depends on it.

    Example: The collapse of AllHere left LA Unified with an unfinished chatbot and questions about student data.

    See also: Vendor Evaluation, AllHere Collapse

    Virtual School¶

    A school that delivers instruction mainly or entirely online, with students learning from home or another location instead of attending a physical building.

    Example: A statewide virtual school where students log in each morning to video lessons and online assignments.

    See also: Charter School, Unbound Academy

    Visual Arts¶

    Art forms created mainly to be seen, such as drawing, painting, sculpture, photography, and design.

    See also: Arts Education

    Web Browser¶

    A software application, such as Chrome, Safari, Edge, or Firefox, used to find, display, and interact with pages on the World Wide Web.

    See also: Browser-Based Simulation, Static Website

    Web Page Hosting¶

    The service of storing web pages on a server connected to the internet so that browsers can retrieve them.

    See also: Static Website, Level 2 Cost Per Student

    Whole Product¶

    The complete set of products and services a buyer needs to get the promised result, including training, support, integration, and content, beyond the core technology.

    Example: An adaptive textbook plus teacher training, LMS integration, a privacy agreement, and help-desk support.

    See also: Crossing the Chasm, Value Beyond Content

    Whole-Organization Change¶

    Change that alters an organization's structures, roles, culture, and practices together, rather than one part at a time.

    Example: A school redesigns its schedule, staffing, assessment, and culture to adopt the morning-afternoon model.

    See also: Holistic Plus Micro Change, Organizational Change

    Why School Reforms Fail¶

    The book's synthesis of recurring causes of failed reforms: technology before pedagogy, the grammar of schooling absorbing change, trust not built, and rushed or poorly governed adoption.

    Example: The LA Unified iPad rollout started with devices instead of a teaching plan.

    See also: Reform Without Real Change, Grammar of Schooling

    xAPI¶

    The Experience API, a standard for recording learning activities as simple statements that can be stored and shared across different software systems.

    Example: A MicroSim sends a record each time a student completes a challenge, which any compliant store can accept.

    See also: xAPI Statement, Learning Record Store

    xAPI Statement¶

    A single record of a learning event, written in the form "actor, verb, object," such as "Maya completed the fractions quiz."

    Example: "Student 4471 answered question 3 of the slope MicroSim correctly at 9:42 a.m."

    See also: Actor Verb Object, xAPI

    Your Brain on ChatGPT¶

    A 2025 MIT Media Lab study, released as a preprint, in which students who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed lower brain engagement on EEG and remembered less of their writing than those who wrote alone.

    It is small, but it supports placing writing, debate, and making in the people-centered afternoon.

    See also: Cognitive Offloading, Preprint

    Zone of Proximal Development¶

    Vygotsky's term for the range of tasks a learner cannot yet do alone but can do with help from a more skilled person.

    Example: A student who cannot yet balance a chemical equation alone succeeds when a peer reminds her to count atoms on each side.

    See also: Social Constructivism, Productive Struggle
