    Evidence, Motivation, and the Stakeholders of Schooling¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers how educators judge what works — evidence-based practice, randomized controlled trials, effect size, and case study methods — alongside the motivational side of learning: curiosity, boredom, engagement, and productive struggle. It also introduces the people involved in a school decision: parents, principals, administrators, and school boards. Readers finish able to weigh a claim about an educational intervention and name the stakeholders it affects.

    A Vendor, a Principal, and a Number¶

    On a Tuesday in October, a sales representative visits Lincoln Middle School, the composite school where Maya from Chapter 1 is a seventh grader. The representative hands the principal, Ms. Okafor, a glossy brochure for an AI-assisted math program called MathPath. It says three things: "Effect size of 0.8," "94% of teachers love it," and "Evidence-based." The district is about to decide whether to buy it for every middle school.

    Ms. Okafor has three problems at once. She needs to know whether those three claims mean anything. She needs to know whether a program that helps some students will actually engage Maya, who hides her confusion, and Jordan, who is bored. And she needs to know who has to be consulted before anything is bought: the teachers, the families, the district office, the school board.

    This chapter gives you the vocabulary to follow her through all three problems. It has three parts: how to judge evidence, how motivation and engagement work, and who the stakeholders of a school decision are. It matters now because AI products will arrive in schools far faster than the research that evaluates them, and the people making the decisions will mostly be educators and board members, not statisticians.

    Before you sign anything

    By the end of this chapter you'll be able to read a vendor brochure the way a careful principal does, and say which of its claims you'd trust and which you'd test. You'll also know exactly who needs a seat at the table before a school adopts anything new. Let's find the balance between healthy skepticism and open-minded curiosity.

    We start with the setting every example in this chapter lives inside.

    K-12 Education: The Setting¶

    K-12 education is the span of publicly and privately provided schooling from kindergarten (usually age five) through twelfth grade (usually age seventeen or eighteen), roughly thirteen years of schooling. The "K" stands for kindergarten and the "12" for the final high school grade. In the United States it is commonly divided into three bands, though the boundaries differ by district.

    Band Typical grades Typical ages Typical structure
    Elementary K–5 5–11 One teacher for most subjects; one classroom
    Middle 6–8 11–14 Subject teachers; periods; a bell schedule
    High 9–12 14–18 Subject teachers; credits toward graduation; electives

    Almost every state requires children to attend school for part of this span, though the starting and ending ages vary. K-12 is also the scope of this book. We do not cover preschool, higher education, or corporate training. The reason to keep the scope narrow is that the decision-makers, the money, and the rules that govern K-12 schools differ sharply from those that govern a university.

    Worked Example: What Thirteen Years Add Up To¶

    A student who attends school from kindergarten through twelfth grade spends, using the Chapter 1 figures of about 180 days a year and about 6.5 hours a day:

    \[ 13 \text{ years} \times 180 \text{ days} = 2{,}340 \text{ days} \qquad 2{,}340 \times 6.5 \text{ hours} \approx 15{,}200 \text{ hours} \]

    At the Chapter 1 per-pupil figure of about $15,600 a year, the public cost of those thirteen years is about \( 13 \times \$15{,}600 = \$202{,}800 \), or roughly $200,000 per student. Those two numbers, 15,200 hours and $200,000, frame every decision in this book. An idea that saves even ten percent of the hours or improves the use of the money is worth a serious look. An idea that costs more than it returns is not.

    Evidence-Based Practice: Judging What Works¶

    When a salesperson says a program is "research-based" or "proven," what are they claiming? Often very little. This section defines the terms researchers and policymakers use to separate strong claims from weak ones.

    Evidence-based practice is the use of the best available research evidence, combined with professional judgment and knowledge of the local students and setting, to decide what to do. Three parts matter: research evidence about what has worked elsewhere, professional judgment from teachers who know their students, and context such as the students' ages, languages, and prior knowledge. Drop any one of them and the approach fails. Research alone ignores the classroom in front of you. Judgment alone is a collection of habits that may never have been tested.

    The phrase gained legal force in the United States through the Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) of 2015, which ties some federal school funds to the use of interventions backed by evidence. ESSA defines four levels of evidence, and the What Works Clearinghouse, run by the U.S. Department of Education's Institute of Education Sciences, reviews studies against standards of this kind. This book adds a fifth bin for claims that are not evidence of effect at all.

    Level What the evidence looks like Plain-language meaning
    Strong At least one well-designed, well-implemented experimental study (random assignment) Students were randomly placed in groups, and the program group did better
    Moderate At least one well-designed quasi-experimental study (a comparison group, but no random assignment) A comparison group existed, but it was chosen, not drawn by lot
    Promising At least one well-designed correlational study with statistical controls for who chose the program Users did better after adjusting for obvious differences
    Demonstrates a rationale A logical explanation based on research, with an evaluation planned or under way A good reason to expect it to work, but no results yet
    No evidence of effect Testimonials, satisfaction surveys, usage counts, uncontrolled before-and-after gains Doesn't show that the program caused learning

    A few things stand out. First, the ladder is about the design of the study, not the size of the claim. A modest effect found by random assignment is stronger evidence than a huge effect from a satisfaction survey. Second, the lowest bin is not a criticism of teachers' opinions, which matter for other reasons. It simply says an opinion is not a measurement of learning.

    Worked Example: Reading the MathPath Brochure¶

    Return to the three claims in Ms. Okafor's brochure and assign each to a level.

    Claim Level Why
    "94% of teachers love it" No evidence of effect A satisfaction survey says teachers like the program, not that students learned more
    "Evidence-based" Unknown until asked The label alone is not a study; the question is which study, of what design
    "Effect size of 0.8" Unknown until asked A size means nothing until you know how it was measured and compared

    The right next step is not to reject MathPath but to ask three questions of any vendor: Who was compared with whom? How were they chosen? What was measured, and by whom? The next two sections explain why those questions matter.

    Randomized Controlled Trials¶

    A randomized controlled trial (RCT) is a study design in which participants are assigned by chance, like a coin flip, to either a treatment group that receives the intervention or a control group that does not, and the two groups are then compared on the same measure. The randomness is the whole point. If assignment is truly by chance, then on average the two groups start out alike in prior knowledge, motivation, family support, and every other factor, including factors nobody thought to measure. Any later difference is then much more likely to be caused by the intervention.

    Compare this with letting students choose. In a school that offers an optional AI tutor, the students who sign up are likely to be more motivated, better organized, or have more supportive homes. If those students score higher, you cannot tell whether the tutor helped or whether motivated students would have done well anyway. This problem is called selection bias, and randomization is the main cure for it.

    The most famous education RCT in the United States is Project STAR, run in Tennessee from 1985 to 1989. About 11,600 students in kindergarten through third grade were randomly assigned to small classes of 13 to 17 students or regular classes of 22 to 25, and the small classes produced modest but lasting benefits. RCTs are also now appearing in the AI-tutoring literature the course description mentions: the Turkish high school study that found open chatbot access harmed later exam scores, and a Nigerian after-school tutoring pilot with large gains, were both designed as randomized comparisons.

    RCTs have limits. They are expensive, slow, and sometimes impossible: you cannot randomly assign children to different school funding levels. They tell you what happened on average in the studied setting, which may not match your school. And a poorly run RCT, with many students dropping out or the control group getting the treatment anyway, can be less trustworthy than its label suggests.

    Worked Example: A Pilot That Randomizes¶

    Lincoln Middle has 280 seventh graders. Instead of buying MathPath for everyone, Ms. Okafor runs a six-week pilot. A coin flip, done by a computer, assigns 140 students to use MathPath for 30 minutes a day in addition to regular class, and 140 to continue as normal. All 280 take the same 50-point test at the start and again after six weeks. Suppose the results are:

    Group Students Mean score at six weeks Standard deviation
    MathPath (treatment) 140 34 10
    Regular class (control) 140 30 10

    The treatment group scored four points higher. Is that a real effect or chance? If the groups were drawn by lot, the random wobble in the difference of two group averages is about

    \[ 10 \times \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{140} + \tfrac{1}{140}} \approx 1.2 \text{ points}, \]

    so a four-point gap is about three times larger than chance alone would usually produce. That makes a real effect likely. But how big is the effect, and is four points a lot? Answering that question is the job of effect size.

    Effect Size¶

    Effect size is a standardized measure of how large the difference between two groups is, expressed in units of the spread of scores rather than in raw points. The most common form is Cohen's d: the difference between the two group averages divided by the pooled standard deviation. The standard deviation measures how widely students' scores vary around the average, so d tells you how many "typical spreads" the groups differ by.

    \[ d = \frac{\bar{x}_{\text{treatment}} - \bar{x}_{\text{control}}}{s_{\text{pooled}}} \]

    Standardizing is what lets you compare a 4-point gain on a 50-point test with a 40-point gain on a 500-point test. In the MathPath pilot, \( d = (34 - 30)/10 = 0.4 \).

    To make d intuitive, translate it into a percentile. If scores are roughly bell-shaped, then at \( d = 0.4 \) the average treated student scores above about 66 percent of the control group. The table below summarizes a few reference points; each follows from the same bell-curve arithmetic.

    Effect size d The average treated student scores higher than this share of the control group
    0.0 50%
    0.1 54%
    0.2 58%
    0.4 66%
    0.5 69%
    0.8 79%

    Now the claim "effect size of 0.8" has a meaning: the average student on the program would outscore about four in five students who were not on it. That would be spectacular, and that is exactly why a skeptic should ask whether it is believable.

    How big is "big"? Jacob Cohen suggested in 1988 that 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, and 0.8 large, but he meant these as rough guides for lab psychology. In education, researchers have learned that real interventions, tested on standardized tests in real schools, rarely produce effects that large. Matthew Kraft's 2020 review of education studies suggests that for well-designed studies of standardized achievement, an effect below 0.05 is small, 0.05 to 0.20 is medium, and above 0.20 is large. By that standard, even an effect of 0.2 is a major result, and a claim of 0.8 should prompt questions about the study's design before celebration. John Hattie's widely cited syntheses use 0.40 as a "hinge point" for a worthwhile effect, but that number has drawn criticism because it mixes studies of very different designs and quality.

    Significant is not the same as big

    Notice that "is the effect real?" and "is the effect large?" are different questions. A huge study can find a real but tiny effect; a tiny study can find a large one by luck. Always ask for both the size and how the groups were formed.

    Worked Example: Is 0.8 Believable?¶

    Suppose the MathPath brochure's 0.8 comes from a study. Walk through what you would ask:

    1. Design. Was assignment random? If students chose MathPath, selection bias could create a large "effect" with no learning gain at all.
    2. Outcome measure. Was the test the standard state test, or a test the vendor wrote about exactly the skills MathPath practices? A test written to match the program inflates effects.
    3. Time and size. A two-week pilot with 30 students may give a big number that does not hold up in 3,000 students over a year.
    4. Who ran it. An independent evaluator is more credible than the company's own team, which has a stake in the result.

    The pilot at Lincoln Middle, with its random assignment, school-wide test, and independent scoring, would produce a number you can trust. That number, 0.4, is lower than 0.8, and it is still a strong result by Kraft's benchmarks.

    Case Study Method¶

    An RCT answers "did it work, on average?" The case study method answers a different question: how and why did it work, or not work, in a particular place? A case study is an in-depth examination of one or a few real situations, such as a school, a classroom, or a district, using interviews, observation, documents, and data, with attention to the context that numbers leave out. The term also names a teaching method, used in business and law schools, in which students analyze a real case and argue about what to do. This book uses both senses: it studies cases, and it asks you to reason about them.

    Cases are strong where RCTs are weak. A case can show that a program worked beautifully in one school because the principal protected teachers' time, and failed in another because the tablets arrived in January. It can surface problems nobody thought to measure. Its weakness is that one story does not prove a general rule: a vivid success may be an exception. The sound approach is to use both. The course description's stories of Alpha School, Unbound Academy, and the Los Angeles "Ed" chatbot are cases; the effect sizes in the research are the statistics; and a careful reader looks for where the two agree.

    Worked Example: A Case Study of the Pilot¶

    After the MathPath pilot, Ms. Okafor asks a district researcher to do a short case study. The researcher interviews six teachers, observes four classes, and reads the usage logs. The findings:

    • What worked: Students who got stuck received a hint within seconds, and teachers could see on a dashboard who needed help.
    • What did not: In two classrooms the tablets were used only on Fridays, because teachers lacked planning time to use the dashboard.
    • Context: The classrooms with the largest gains had a teacher who stopped the lesson each day to address the most common error the dashboard showed.

    The RCT said the program helped on average. The case study says the average hides a difference in how teachers used it. Both are needed to decide what to do next.

    Prior Knowledge and Prerequisite Concepts¶

    Evidence tells you whether something works on average. Learning, as Chapter 1 stressed, happens inside one learner at a time, and what that learner already knows shapes everything.

    Prior knowledge is the set of facts, skills, concepts, and beliefs a learner already holds when they begin a new lesson. The educational psychologist David Ausubel wrote in 1968 that the single most important factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows, and that teachers should find this out and teach accordingly. That remains one of the best-supported ideas in the field, and it explains much of the learning gap you met in Chapter 1.

    Prior knowledge has three effects. It enables new learning: a student who knows what a fraction is can learn to add fractions. It organizes memory: new facts attach to existing ones, which is why experts remember more of a new fact in their field than novices do. And it can mislead: prior knowledge sometimes includes wrong ideas. In a well-known 1987 documentary, A Private Universe, graduates and faculty at Harvard were asked why it is hot in summer and cold in winter, and many confidently explained that the Earth is closer to the Sun in summer, which is wrong. A teacher who does not find out about such beliefs may teach on top of them without correcting them.

    A prerequisite concept is a concept that must be understood before another concept can be learned. Prior knowledge is what the student has; a prerequisite is what a given idea requires. A teacher's work is to compare the two. Prerequisites form chains, and the learning graph introduced in Chapter 1 and covered in depth in Chapter 9 is a map of those chains, with every arrow pointing from a concept to what it depends on.

    Worked Example: Maya's Missing Stair¶

    Maya's difficulty with ratios in seventh grade traces back to a chain of prerequisites in mathematics. The table lists a simplified chain of eight concepts and what each depends on.

    # Concept Depends directly on
    1 Whole-number multiplication and division none
    2 Fractions as parts of a whole none
    3 Equivalent fractions 1, 2
    4 Adding and subtracting fractions with unlike denominators 3
    5 Multiplying and dividing fractions 1, 2
    6 Ratios 3
    7 Unit rates 5, 6
    8 Proportional equations 7

    Ms. Rivera sees that Maya fails unit-rate problems (concept 7). Reteaching unit rates again will not help if the gap lies earlier. A short diagnostic gives Maya a few problems on each concept. She passes concepts 1, 2, and 5 but fails concepts 3, 4, and 6. Failing 3 explains the other two: concepts 4 and 6 both depend on equivalent fractions. The problem is not in unit rates, or even ratios; it is two steps down, at equivalent fractions. This is why a diagnosis needs a map and not just a score.

    The practical rule is this: the root gap is a failed concept all of whose own prerequisites passed. A failed concept whose prerequisites also failed is a symptom, not the cause. Fix the root gap and the dependent failures often resolve; reteach a symptom and nothing changes. The sim below lets you practice that diagnosis.

    Lecture-Based Teaching and Immediate Feedback¶

    Two ways of teaching that shape most of a student's day deserve their own definitions: how information reaches the student, and how quickly the student hears back.

    Lecture-Based Teaching¶

    Lecture-based teaching is a mode of instruction in which the teacher presents information orally to the whole class while students mostly listen and take notes. It overlaps with the direct instruction of Chapter 1 but is narrower: direct instruction also includes guided practice, questioning, and checking, whereas a pure lecture is one-way delivery. Lecturing is efficient for the teacher, since one explanation reaches thirty students, and it works well for giving an overview or telling a compelling story. Its weakness is that the teacher cannot see what each listener understood until later.

    The best-known synthesis is a 2014 meta-analysis by Scott Freeman and colleagues in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, which pooled 225 studies of undergraduate science, technology, engineering, and mathematics courses. It found that students in courses with active learning, where students answer questions, solve problems, and discuss during class, earned higher exam scores than students in traditional lecture, by about half a standard deviation. Students in lecture were also more likely to fail: about 34 percent compared with about 22 percent. That evidence comes from college STEM courses, so it should be applied to K-12 with care, but the direction matches what teachers see.

    The lesson is not "never lecture." It is that a stretch of listening should be short, followed quickly by students doing something with the idea, with the teacher checking. A short explanation followed by practice is the "I do, we do, you do" pattern from Chapter 1.

    Immediate Feedback¶

    Immediate feedback is information given to a learner about the correctness of a response, and ideally the reason, within seconds or minutes of the response. It is the opposite of delayed feedback, such as homework returned a week later. Immediate feedback was a central feature of the teaching machines of Chapter 1's history, and it is the capability of computers that most clearly beats a single human teacher with thirty students.

    The research on timing is more nuanced than "faster is always better." Feedback soon after an error stops a student from practicing a mistake, and it matters most for novices learning procedures, where a wrong step repeated twenty times becomes a habit. But very quick feedback that simply shows the answer can short-circuit thinking: a student who clicks "show answer" at the first hint learns little. The most useful feedback tells the student what to try next rather than what the answer is. This is the idea behind the hint-first guardrails Chapter 12 covers.

    Worked Example: Two Homework Cycles¶

    Suppose Maya practices ten unit-rate problems. Compare what happens under two feedback timings.

    Paper homework Interactive practice with immediate hints
    When Maya learns she is wrong Five days later, when the paper is returned Within seconds of each problem
    Problems Maya repeats with the same error All ten (she does not know) About one or two before a hint corrects her
    What the teacher sees A stack of papers; marks, not reasons A dashboard of which step students miss
    Risk The error becomes a habit Students click for the answer instead of thinking

    Notice that the second column is better only if the hints are designed well. The risk in the last row is real, and it is why how an AI tool gives feedback matters as much as how fast.

    A hint ladder keeps the thinking

    When you evaluate any practice tool, ask it for help on a problem and count the steps before it shows the answer. A good tool starts with a nudge ("What is the problem asking for?"), moves to a specific hint, and only then explains. If the first click reveals the answer, the feedback is fast but the learning is not.

    Summative Assessment¶

    Summative assessment, which Chapter 1 contrasted with formative assessment, is a measurement of learning at the end of a period, such as a unit, semester, or year, used to assign a grade, certify mastery, or report results. Familiar examples are final exams, end-of-unit tests, graduation exams, and state accountability tests. Under ESSA, U.S. states must test students in reading and mathematics every year from third through eighth grade and once in high school, and these results are reported to the public by school. The National Assessment of Educational Progress, often called the "Nation's Report Card," samples students nationally to track trends over time.

    Summative tests serve real purposes. They give a common yardstick, allow comparison across schools, and are the standard way the RCTs in the previous section measure results. But they have limits. They arrive too late to help the student who took them. They sample only part of what students learn. And when high stakes are attached, they distort behavior: the social scientist Donald Campbell observed in 1979 that the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it is subject to pressure that corrupts both the indicator and the process it was meant to monitor. Teachers narrowing the curriculum to what the test covers, or "teaching to the test," is a textbook example.

    Worked Example: Two Reports on the Same Student¶

    Compare what two assessments tell Ms. Okafor about Maya's math.

    End-of-year state test Weekly five-question checks
    Type Summative Formative
    When it arrives June, after the school year ends Every Friday
    What it says about Maya "Below grade-level proficiency" "Missed equivalent-fraction questions four weeks in a row"
    What the school can do with it Plan next year's programs Intervene this month
    Risk Too late for Maya; high stakes can narrow teaching Many small tests can feel like constant evaluation

    Both are needed: the first tells the school whether the year succeeded, and the second tells the teacher what to fix while there is still time. An AI-native school depends on getting the second kind of data at scale, a theme Chapter 8 develops.

    Motivation and Engagement¶

    Knowing what works is only part of the story. A program that works on paper does nothing for a student who does not engage with it. This part of the chapter defines the words educators use for the emotional and motivational side of learning.

    Student Engagement¶

    Student engagement is the degree to which students are attentive, interested, and invested in their learning. Researchers Jennifer Fredricks, Phyllis Blumenfeld, and Alison Paris described it in 2004 as having three connected dimensions:

    • Behavioral engagement: participation and effort you can see, such as attending, completing work, and following rules.
    • Emotional engagement: feelings toward school and tasks, such as interest, enjoyment, and a sense of belonging.
    • Cognitive engagement: the mental effort a student invests, such as choosing to work through a hard problem or checking their own understanding.

    The three can come apart. A student can look perfectly on task (behavioral) while feeling nothing (emotional) and thinking about lunch (cognitive). Engagement is also not the same as fun: a laughing, busy class may be engaged with the activity but not with the learning goal.

    Worked Example: Two Students, Three Dimensions¶

    Ms. Rivera observes two students during a unit-rate lesson and records what she sees.

    Dimension Maya Jordan
    Behavioral Sits quietly, copies the examples, turns in work Finishes early, then doodles
    Emotional Anxious; avoids eye contact; says "fine" when asked Looks relaxed; says "this is easy"
    Cognitive Copies steps without checking understanding Thinking about something else
    Overall Looks engaged; is not Looks disengaged; the work is too easy

    Both are at risk, in different ways, and only a teacher who looks at all three dimensions would see it. This is a core promise of well-designed adaptive software: data on what a student actually did reveals cognitive engagement that a quiet face hides.

    Student Boredom¶

    Student boredom is an unpleasant emotional state in which a student finds the activity uninteresting and has difficulty staying attentive. Boredom is one of the most common emotions students report in class. Surveys of high school students over the past two decades have consistently found that a large share say they are bored in class regularly, though exact percentages vary by survey and should be checked at the source before being cited.

    Boredom has more than one cause, and this is the key insight. Psychologist Reinhard Pekrun's research on academic emotions shows that boredom can arise when a task is too easy, when it is too hard and the student has given up, or when the student sees no value in it. The behavior looks the same in all three cases, which is why one fix does not suit all.

    Worked Example: Three Bored Students¶

    In the same classroom, three students show the same behavior: head down, pencil untouched.

    Student Why they are bored What helps
    Jordan Already mastered the material; the work is too easy Move to harder material; skip ahead
    Maya Lost two lessons ago; has given up Fill the missing prerequisite; shrink the first step
    Dev Can do it but asks "when will I ever use this?" Connect the topic to something he cares about

    Whole-class pacing (Chapter 1) gives all three the same lesson. Individually paced software can respond to the first two, but only a teacher or a good project is likely to answer Dev.

    Same face, different cause

    A common trap is treating every bored student as lazy or every disengaged student as a behavior problem. Before you act, ask which of three things is going on: too easy, too hard, or not worth it. Each has a different fix, and the wrong fix, like more challenge for a lost student, makes things worse.

    Intrinsic Motivation¶

    Intrinsic motivation is the drive to do something because it is interesting or satisfying in itself, not for a reward or to avoid punishment. Its counterpart, extrinsic motivation, is doing something for an outside reward such as a grade, a prize, or approval. Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's self-determination theory holds that intrinsic motivation grows when three needs are met: autonomy (a sense of choice), competence (a sense of being able to succeed), and relatedness (a sense of connection to others).

    The most famous result in this area is a 1973 experiment by Mark Lepper, David Greene, and Richard Nisbett. Preschoolers who already liked drawing with felt-tip markers were promised a "good player" award for drawing. Later, in free time, those children drew with markers less than children who had not been promised anything. The reward had changed why they drew, from "because it's fun" to "because I get a prize." This pattern is called the overjustification effect. The takeaway is not that rewards are always harmful: for dull tasks they can help. It is that adding a reward to something a student already enjoys can weaken the enjoyment.

    Worked Example: Points and Badges¶

    A vendor's practice app awards points and badges for every completed problem. Evaluate it against the three needs.

    Need Does the app support it? Risk or fix
    Autonomy Partly: students choose the order of the practice sets Add more choices about what to explore
    Competence Yes: students see a progress bar and mastery marks Make sure the marks reflect real mastery, not just time spent
    Relatedness No: students work alone Pair the app with a short weekly discussion with a classmate
    Overjustification Points may replace the satisfaction of solving Reward progress toward learning goals, and fade the points over time

    The lesson: a feature is not good or bad because it is a badge. It is good if it helps students feel capable and in control, and bad if it becomes the reason they work.

    Curiosity¶

    Curiosity is the desire to learn or know something, usually triggered by noticing a gap in what you know. George Loewenstein's information-gap theory, from 1994, says curiosity is strongest when you know a little about something, enough to realize you do not know the rest. Too little knowledge produces no curiosity, and complete knowledge leaves nothing to be curious about.

    Curiosity also helps memory. In a 2014 brain-imaging study, Matthias Gruber, Bernard Gelman, and Charan Ranganath found that people remembered answers to trivia questions they were curious about better than answers to questions they were not, and that curiosity engaged the brain's reward circuits. In practice, a lesson that opens with a puzzle (Why do puddles disappear on a sunny day?) can prepare students to attend to the explanation.

    Curiosity-Driven Learning¶

    Curiosity-driven learning is an approach in which students pursue their own questions and interests, with the teacher guiding and resourcing rather than dictating the path. It applies the student agency of Chapter 1 to the choice of topic. Examples are inquiry projects, genius hour, and open-ended research questions.

    The evidence needs honest handling. Curiosity-driven approaches can build motivation and ownership, and some, like Sugata Mitra's "Hole in the Wall" experiments in India, where children taught themselves from a public computer, drew worldwide attention. But those results have been disputed, and a well-known 2006 paper by Paul Kirschner, John Sweller, and Richard Clark argued that minimally guided instruction is less effective than guided instruction for novices, who lack the prior knowledge to explore well. The consistent finding is that curiosity works best on top of a foundation: students need enough knowledge to have good questions and enough structure to follow them somewhere.

    Worked Example: Building Curiosity into a Morning¶

    Consider a two-hour morning block that uses adaptive practice for core skills. A school wants to protect time for curiosity without losing the core.

    Segment Minutes What students do
    Core mastery path 90 Adaptive practice with immediate feedback toward grade-level outcomes
    Curiosity block 30 Each student pursues a question of their own, with a teacher check-in
    Total 120

    The curiosity block is \( 30 / 120 = 25\% \) of the morning. The design holds the core fixed so every student gets the foundation, and still gives every student a protected slot for their own questions. The next chapters return to how this fits the afternoon.

    Productive Struggle¶

    Productive struggle is the effortful work a student does to make sense of a problem that is challenging but within reach, before being shown how to solve it. It is not the same as frustration, which is struggle with no way forward. Manu Kapur's research on productive failure, begun in 2008, found that students who first tried to solve a hard problem on their own, even failing, understood the underlying concept better afterward than students who were first taught the procedure. This is the classroom version of the desirable difficulty from Chapter 1.

    Struggle is productive when four things hold: the task is hard but reachable, the student has some prior knowledge to work with, errors are treated as normal, and the teacher later consolidates what was learned. It becomes unproductive when the task needs knowledge the student lacks, when there is no support, or when failure is punished.

    Worked Example: A Hint Ladder¶

    A problem asks: "A 6-pack of juice costs $4.50. What is the price per bottle?" A tool offers four levels of help, and each level removes a little of the struggle.

    Level What the tool says How much struggle is left
    0 Nothing; the student works alone All of it
    1 "What is the problem asking you to find?" Almost all; the student still plans
    2 "Which quantity do you divide by which?" Some; the plan is narrowed
    3 "Divide 4.50 by 6." Little; only the arithmetic remains
    4 "The answer is $0.75." None

    A tool that jumps to level 4 removes the struggle, and with it the learning. A tool that never goes past level 0 produces frustration. The art is to give the smallest help that keeps the student moving. This is the central design question for AI tutors, and Chapter 12 builds on it.

    Struggle is part of the plan

    If a problem is hard and you feel stuck, that doesn't mean you're doing it wrong; it often means the learning is happening. You've already learned to separate performance from lasting learning in Chapter 1, and this is the same idea from the inside. Try the next sim and predict first; being wrong on a prediction is a good kind of struggle.

    The terms in this section fit together through a single idea: how hard the task is compared with what the student already knows. Before the next sim, here is a simple model that this book uses. Rate the challenge of a task and the student's prior knowledge on the same 1-to-10 scale, and call the difference, challenge minus prior knowledge, the gap. This is a deliberate simplification, inspired by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's work on "flow," which found that people are most absorbed when challenge and skill are well matched. The table gives the four states the model predicts. Providing support, such as a hint ladder, lets a student handle one more level of gap before becoming overwhelmed.

    Gap (challenge minus prior knowledge) State without support State with support
    Gap <= −2 Bored Bored
    −1 <= gap <= 1 Engaged Engaged
    2 <= gap <= 3 Productive struggle Productive struggle
    Gap = 4 Overwhelmed Productive struggle
    Gap >= 5 Overwhelmed Overwhelmed

    Screen Time¶

    Screen time is the amount of time a person spends using devices with screens, such as tablets, computers, phones, and televisions. It is one of the most common worries families raise about educational technology, and it deserves a straight answer, because the model in this book puts students in front of a screen for part of the morning.

    The first point is that "screen time" lumps together very different activities. Watching videos passively, scrolling social media, playing a game, solving practice problems with feedback, and writing a story on a laptop all count as screen time, yet they differ greatly in cognitive demand and in their effects. Researchers increasingly separate what a student does from how long the screen is on.

    The second point is that the evidence on harm is weaker and more mixed than headlines suggest. A 2019 analysis of three large datasets by Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski found that the association between digital technology use and adolescent well-being was very small, explaining well under one percent of the variation. That study was correlational, so it cannot show cause, and other research finds larger effects for specific uses such as late-night social media. On learning, the OECD's PISA 2022 results found that students who were distracted by other students' devices in class scored lower in mathematics, and that moderate use of devices for learning went with higher scores than heavy use. The pattern is consistent: purpose and context matter more than the total.

    The balance in this book's design is to be honest about the tradeoff. A two-hour morning on adaptive software is real screen time, and it also replaces hours of passive listening. The afternoon, centered on people, projects, and movement, is largely screen-free.

    Worked Example: Comparing Two Days¶

    The table compares Maya's screen use in two designs, using illustrative numbers.

    Activity Traditional day (minutes on screen) AI-native day (minutes on screen)
    Whole-class lessons 10 (teacher projector, video clips) 0

    Don't judge a screen by its glow

    A common trap is adding up all screen minutes as if they were the same. Instead, sort the minutes into passive, social, and active-learning, and ask what each replaced. If the school day adds two hours of interactive practice, give families the breakdown and a plan for breaks, movement, and an off-screen afternoon.

    The People Involved: Stakeholders of Schooling¶

    Every decision in this chapter, from adopting MathPath to changing the schedule, affects different people differently. The term stakeholder names anyone who is affected by or has influence over a decision. This section covers the ones a school leader hears from first.

    Parent and Family¶

    Parent and family refers to the adults responsible for a child at home: parents, guardians, grandparents, and other caregivers. They are the child's first and longest-lasting educators, and the only stakeholders who know the student across all thirteen years. The education researcher Joyce Epstein described six ways families and schools work together: parenting, communicating, volunteering, supporting learning at home, taking part in decisions, and collaborating with the community.

    Research summarized by Anne Henderson and Karen Mapp in 2002 found that family involvement is associated with better attendance, grades, and attitudes, though most of that evidence is correlational: involved families may differ in other ways. What is clear is that families have real concerns about AI in schools, and that those concerns are often legitimate. The most common are screen time, privacy of the child's data, whether the child will still learn to think, and fairness for children with fewer resources at home.

    Worked Example: A Family Night Handout¶

    Ms. Okafor prepares answers to four common concerns for a parent night about the MathPath pilot.

    Concern What the evidence says What the school will do
    "Too much screen time" Purpose matters more than total minutes; the pilot is 30 active minutes a day Publish the daily schedule; keep afternoons screen-light
    "What happens to my child's data?" Adaptive software needs records of student work; this raises privacy duties (Chapter 10) Share the vendor's data policy and let families ask questions
    "Will my child just get the answers?" It depends on the tool's design; hint-first tools protect thinking Show the hint ladder in a demonstration
    "What if my child falls behind?" Self-paced tools can drift without checks Teachers review progress weekly and call families when a student stalls

    Notice that the right-hand column promises actions, not slogans. It is also an example of this book's pattern: name the risk, then say how the school responds.

    School Administrator and Principal¶

    A school administrator is a staff member who manages a school or school system rather than teaching classes full time. The group includes principals and assistant principals at the school level and, at the district level, the superintendent (the district's chief executive), and directors of curriculum, technology, finance, and special education. Administrators are responsible for budgets, hiring, schedules, safety, compliance with laws, and carrying out the policies the school board sets.

    A principal is the administrator who leads a single school. The principal is both the manager and the instructional leader: setting the school's priorities, hiring and supporting teachers, observing classrooms, and being the main point of contact for families. The importance of the role is well documented. A 2021 review by Jason Grissom, Anna Egalite, and Constance Lindsay for the Wallace Foundation found that a more effective principal raises student achievement by about as much as a few additional months of learning, and has a large effect on whether good teachers stay. A principal rarely controls the budget or the district's contracts, but has great control over whether a new idea survives its first year.

    School Boards¶

    The school board, sometimes called the board of education, is the body of citizens, usually elected, that governs a school district. It typically has five to nine members who set district policy, adopt the annual budget, approve major contracts, and hire and evaluate the superintendent. There are roughly 13,000 school districts in the United States, each with its own board, so the rules and the politics vary a great deal. Board members are often volunteers or paid a small stipend and may have no professional background in education. They hear from every other stakeholder, which makes them the place where a technology decision is finally made public.

    School board Budget, large contracts, policy, hiring the superintendent Voters
    Parent and family Their own child's participation, home learning, consent Themselves; they may elect the board

    Worked Example: The Path of a Purchase¶

    Follow what happens if Ms. Okafor wants to adopt MathPath. A pilot of one grade within her school's budget is within her authority as principal. A district-wide purchase of $900,000 is not. It needs the district technology director to check the vendor's data agreement, the superintendent to recommend it, and the board to vote in public, where parents may speak. At each step a different stakeholder asks a different question: Are the teachers ready? Is the data safe? Is it worth the money? Is it fair to every child? A leader who prepares for all four questions before the first meeting is far more likely to succeed.

    Private School Tuition¶

    Private school tuition is the fee a family pays directly to a private school for a student's education, in place of, or in addition to, the taxes that fund public schools. Roughly one in ten U.S. K-12 students attends a private school. Tuition varies enormously: from a few thousand dollars a year at many religious schools to tens of thousands at the most selective independent schools. Private schools are not governed by a public school board, and they answer mainly to their families and boards of trustees.

    Tuition matters to this book for two reasons. It is the price signal of the market that AI-first schools enter, and it frames the equity question. The best-known AI-first school, Alpha School, charges about $40,000 a year at its original Austin campus and about $65,000 a year at newer campuses such as Boston, Miami, and New York. Compared with the public per-pupil spending of about $15,600 from Chapter 1, that is a very large gap, and it raises the question this book keeps returning to: how much of what works can be offered at a cost a public school can afford?

    Worked Example: Thirteen Years of Tuition¶

    Using the figures above, compare thirteen years of public spending with thirteen years at the two Alpha School price points.

    Model Cost per year Cost over 13 years Multiple of public per-pupil spending
    Public school (per-pupil spending) $15,600 $202,800 1.0
    Alpha School, original Austin campus $40,000 $520,000 \( 40{,}000 / 15{,}600 \approx 2.6 \)
    Alpha School, newer campuses $65,000 $845,000 \( 65{,}000 / 15{,}600 \approx 4.2 \)

    Two cautions are needed. Tuition is a price and per-pupil spending is a cost, so they are not exactly comparable, and families who pay tuition have usually paid property taxes as well. And a high price is not evidence of quality, which is why the evidence skills in this chapter matter: Chapter 22 returns to what Alpha's reported results do and do not show.

    Returning to the Brochure¶

    Ms. Okafor now has what she needs. She did not reject MathPath, and she did not buy it for the district. Here is how the vocabulary of this chapter shaped her decision.

    Her question Chapter 2 concept Her action
    What does "evidence-based" mean here? Evidence-based practice, evidence levels Asked the vendor which study, of what design, measured by whom
    Is 0.8 believable? Effect size, randomized controlled trial Ran a randomized pilot; found d of 0.4, a strong result by education benchmarks
    Does it fit students like Maya and Jordan? Prior knowledge, prerequisites, boredom, productive struggle Checked that the tool diagnoses prerequisites and gives hint ladders
    Why did gains vary by classroom? Case study method Interviewed teachers; found the difference in how the dashboard was used
    Are families comfortable? Screen time, parent and family Held a parent night with a concern-by-concern handout

    This story shows both sides, as every story in this book does. The benefit is real: a modest, trustworthy gain and faster feedback for students like Maya. The risks are also real: gains that depend on teacher use, student drift, data privacy, and cost. The response is a school that asked for evidence, ran its own test, and brought every stakeholder in early.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • K-12 education is about thirteen years and about 15,000 hours per student, at a public cost of roughly $200,000 per student, so any change should be judged against that scale.
    • Evidence-based practice means combining research, professional judgment, and local context; the levels of evidence rank study design, not the size of a claim.
    • A randomized controlled trial assigns students by chance to remove selection bias; effect size expresses the result in standard deviations, and in real schools an effect of 0.2 is already large.
    • The case study method explains how and why something worked in one place, and complements the averages from trials.
    • Prior knowledge is the strongest single influence on new learning; a prerequisite concept is what an idea requires, and the root gap is the failed concept whose own prerequisites passed.
    • Lecture-based teaching works best in short stretches followed by active practice; immediate feedback helps most when it nudges rather than gives away the answer; summative assessment measures the year, and formative checks fix the month.
    • Student engagement has behavioral, emotional, and cognitive parts; student boredom can mean too easy, too hard, or pointless.
    • Intrinsic motivation grows with autonomy, competence, and relatedness; curiosity is strongest at an information gap; curiosity-driven learning works best on top of a foundation; productive struggle is effort on a task within reach, with help scaled to keep it so.
    • Screen time depends on what students do, not only how long; parents and families, principals, school administrators, and school boards each hold different decisions; private school tuition frames the price comparison for AI-first schools.
