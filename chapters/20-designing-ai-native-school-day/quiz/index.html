    Quiz: Designing the AI-Native School Day¶

    Test your understanding of how to design an AI-native school day, from schedules and staffing to assessment, integrity, procurement, and pilots, with these review questions.

    1. How does this book distinguish an AI-native school from an AI-first school?¶

    1. It uses more hours of AI software each day than an AI-first school does
    2. It is always a private school, while an AI-first school is always public
    3. It starts from learning goals and people, then adds the AI that serves them
    4. It uses no AI in the morning and relies on teachers for instruction
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. An AI-first school puts AI technology at the center of instruction and is often described by how much AI students use. An AI-native school starts with a pedagogy and purpose, mastery of the academic core and practice with people, and uses AI where it does that job best, at the lowest level that works and with guardrails. The chapter's point is that "AI-first" describes a tool, and the tool alone is not the model, as the contrast between Alpha School and Unbound Academy shows.

    2. In the chapter's 280-student staffing illustration, how does the AI-native day compare with the traditional day in adult hours?¶

    1. About half as many, since the software handles most instruction
    2. About 10 percent more, with adults coaching and facilitating, not lecturing
    3. Exactly the same, with no change in what the adults do each day
    4. About twice as many, which makes the model unaffordable
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The illustration gives about 65 adult-hours a day for the traditional schedule and about 71.7 for the AI-native one, roughly 10 percent more, because afternoon programs need groups of about 15 to 20 per adult. The model does not save on staff; the change is in what adults do. Its savings come from elsewhere, such as content. The chapter notes the figures show the shape of the change, not a budget, since real pupil-to-teacher ratios are lower.

    3. A school runs an AI detector on 20,000 essays. Four percent were written with AI, and the detector catches 75 percent of those. It wrongly flags 2 percent of human-written essays. About what share of all flagged essays are false accusations?¶

    1. About 2 percent
    2. About 25 percent
    3. About 61 percent
    4. About 39 percent
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. There are 800 AI essays, and the detector flags 600 of them. There are 19,200 human essays, and 2 percent, 384, are wrongly flagged. Total flags are 600 + 384 = 984, and 384 / 984 is about 39 percent false. The 2 percent answer confuses the false-positive rate with the share of accusations that are false, and 61 percent is the share that are correct. The chapter shows how a small-sounding error rate produces many wrongly accused students.

    4. What did the 2023 Stanford study by Liang and colleagues find about AI detectors and English-language learners?¶

    1. More than half of non-native writers' TOEFL essays were wrongly flagged as AI
    2. Detectors flagged native and non-native writers at about the same low rate overall
    3. Detectors were more accurate on non-native writers because their wording is simpler
    4. Detectors caught nearly all AI essays, so their bias was acceptable in practice
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The study tested seven popular detectors and found that, on average, more than half of TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers were wrongly labeled as AI-generated, while essays by U.S. eighth graders were classified almost perfectly. One explanation is that detectors look for predictable text, and second-language writers tend to use simpler, more predictable wording. That simplicity causes the false flags rather than better accuracy. The chapter calls this a clear equity problem and advises redesigning assignments instead of relying on detection.

    5. Using the chapter's weighted scorecard (weights 20, 20, 15, 15, 10, 10, 10 for evidence, privacy, level fit, guardrails, standards, total cost, and viability), a vendor scores 3, 2, 4, 4, 5, 4, and 4. What is the outcome?¶

    1. A score of 70, so the vendor proceeds directly to a district contract
    2. A score of 80, so the vendor proceeds to a one-grade pilot
    3. A score of 70, but the knockout rule stops the purchase because privacy scored 2
    4. A score of 52, so the vendor is rejected permanently by the district
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The weighted score is the sum of weight times score divided by 5: 12 + 8 + 12 + 12 + 10 + 8 + 8 = 70. The knockout rule says a score of 2 or below on privacy or on vendor viability stops the purchase until the problem is resolved, so this vendor cannot proceed regardless of its total. The chapter applies the rule to Vendor A's viability score, calling it the Ed chatbot lesson in a rule: the total does not show some risks. A knockout pauses the purchase; it is not a permanent rejection.

    6. A one-grade pilot uses the chapter's five goals and decision rule. Results: learning gain of 0.18 standard deviations over the comparison section, teacher workload up 3 hours a week, 75 percent family satisfaction, no privacy incident, and no subgroup more than 0.1 standard deviations lower. What does the rule say to do?¶

    1. Scale, since the learning gain beat the 0.15 target
    2. Stop, since one of the five goals was missed
    3. Scale, since no privacy incident occurred and families are satisfied
    4. Extend the pilot a semester and fix the workload problem
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The rule is to scale only if all five goals are met, stop if there is a serious privacy incident or a subgroup is more than 0.1 standard deviations lower, and otherwise extend a semester and fix what was missed. Workload rose by 3 hours against a limit of no more than 2, so not all goals were met, but neither stop condition applies. Scaling on the learning gain alone ignores the rule written in advance, which exists so that the decision rests on evidence, not the mood of the room.

    7. A teacher is applying the chapter's "traffic light" rules for AI use. Which pairing of task and rule fits the chapter's examples?¶

    1. In-class writing sample: green, AI as a full tool with disclosure
    2. Research project: green, AI as a full tool with disclosure
    3. Brainstorming for an essay: red, no AI allowed
    4. Research project: red, no AI allowed
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's traffic light sets red for no AI, as in an in-class writing sample; yellow for AI in specific ways, such as brainstorming or feedback on a draft with notes on what was used; and green for AI as a full tool with disclosure, as in a research project. Clear rules for each task, together with redesigning work so process evidence and oral explanation are part of it, serve academic integrity better than detection. The other pairings reverse the chapter's examples.

    8. A school plans to staff its academic morning with "learning guides" who support motivation and progress but are not subject experts. What design risk does the chapter identify?¶

    1. Guides will cost more than certified teachers, raising the budget
    2. Guides cannot legally supervise students during the school day
    3. A student who needs a human explanation of a hard concept may not get one
    4. Guides will make the morning longer, cutting into the afternoon
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A learning guide, the term some AI-first schools such as Alpha School use, supports students' motivation, effort, and progress in place of delivering content. The chapter calls the choice of non-expert guides a design choice with risks: a student who needs a human explanation of a hard concept needs a person who can give one. It stresses that a teacher's expertise matters more, not less, once software handles routine delivery. The chapter does not claim guides cost more or are legally barred.

    9. A district's new afternoon program charges a $150 activity fee per semester and offers no transportation. Analyzed against the chapter's equity commitments, what is the main problem?¶

    1. The fee is too low to cover partner costs, so programs will be weak
    2. Fees are acceptable as long as the morning software is free to all students
    3. Transportation is needed only for students in the K-2 grade band
    4. A program that costs money recreates the gap; it should be free with transport
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. One of the chapter's five equity-of-access commitments is a free afternoon: no fees for afternoon programs and transportation so every student can take part, because a program that costs money recreates the gap. The model could narrow gaps by giving every student a patient tutor and a rich afternoon, or widen them if only well-resourced students benefit. A free morning does not make up for an afternoon only some families can afford, which is why the second option fails.

    10. A school board weighs two proposals. Proposal 1 is a district-wide AI-first model with four hours a day of adaptive software, supported by a vendor's self-reported results. Proposal 2 is a one-year AI-native pilot in one grade with written goals, an independent scorer, a decision rule, and equity commitments. Which judgment is best supported by the chapter?¶

    1. Proposal 1, because more AI time each day produces faster learning gains
    2. Proposal 2, because it tests the model independently and decides the next step in advance
    3. Proposal 1, because district-wide adoption avoids the cost of running a pilot
    4. Neither, because AI should not be used in schools until it is fully proven
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter recommends staying at the lowest level that works, piloting before scaling, writing the decision rule first, and keeping equity commitments non-negotiable. Proposal 2 does all of these and uses independent evidence, which the AI-native design requires; Proposal 1 relies on self-reported results and commits the whole district at once, the pattern the Ed chatbot case warns against. More AI time is not evidence of benefit, and the book does not argue for waiting until AI is fully proven; it argues for careful pilots.
