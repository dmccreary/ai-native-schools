    Microstrategies for Leading Change¶

Readers finish able to choose a microstrategy suited to a specific teacher's stage of concern.

    What Do I Do on Monday?¶

    After a year of frameworks, Ms. Okafor's assistant principal, Mr. Santos, says what she has been thinking. "I understand Kotter. I understand the neutral zone. But it's Sunday night. What do I actually do tomorrow?"

    It is a fair challenge to everything in the last three chapters. Models describe change. They do not, on their own, tell a leader what to say to a nervous teacher in the hallway on Monday morning. This chapter turns to the small, concrete moves that do. They are cheap, they can start next week, and they work by changing what individual people experience, one at a time.

    The chapter ties the models of Chapters 16 to 18 to practical moves and fits each move to a person's situation: a teacher's stage of concern, a group's place on the adoption curve. It covers teachers, then families, the union, the board, and students, since a school's change belongs to all of them. Along the way it treats resistance as information, not as an obstacle to be overcome.

    From frameworks to Monday morning

    This chapter is the "what do I do tomorrow" chapter: small moves, matched to the person in front of you. You'll finish able to choose a first step for a particular teacher, family, or union representative. Let's find the balance between a bold plan and a modest first step.

    Microstrategies and the Big Plan¶

    A microstrategy is a small, concrete action that a leader or teacher can take now, in a few minutes or hours, which moves one person or one group a little closer to adopting a change. Examples are inviting a colleague to watch a lesson, asking a teacher to try one ten-minute activity, or sharing one student's work at a staff meeting. A microstrategy does not require a budget line or a committee.

    Holistic plus micro change is the combination of a whole-organization plan (the vision, the coalition, the schedule, the policies of Chapters 16 to 18) with a continuing stream of microstrategies that carry it out person by person. Each does what the other cannot. The large plan gives direction and removes structural obstacles, and the microstrategies build the skills, trust, and habits that no plan can order. A school with only the plan has a policy that is ignored. A school with only microstrategies has pleasant moments that never add up.

    Worked Example: Plan and Moves Together¶

    Part of the change Large-scale plan Microstrategies that carry it out
    Teachers adopt the Level 2 textbook Board approves it; schedule changed; training budget A teacher champion shows a lesson; a colleague visits; the coach helps with logins
    Families accept the new morning District policy on data; a family handbook A short phone call from the teacher; a parent night with a demonstration
    The afternoon runs well Staffing plan; partnership agreements A weekly debrief ritual; one student exhibition at the end of the first month

    The Switch Framework¶

    A useful organizing model for microstrategies comes from Chip and Dan Heath's 2010 book Switch: How to Change Things When Change Is Hard. The Switch framework uses an image: a rider sitting on an elephant, on a path. The rider is the rational, planning side of a person. The elephant is the emotional side, much stronger, and the part that tires of effort and balks. The path is the situation around them. A change succeeds when all three are addressed, since a clear-headed rider cannot steer a reluctant elephant, and a willing elephant still struggles on a rocky path.

    Direct the rider is the set of moves that give the rational side clarity: with the bright spots, a script of the critical moves, and a picture of the destination. Motivate the elephant is the set of moves that engage emotion: finding the feeling, shrinking the change, and growing people's identity. Shape the path is the set of moves that change the situation so that the right behavior is easier: tweaking the environment, building habits, and rallying the herd.

    Part What it is Tactics A school example
    Direct the rider Clarity for the thinking mind Follow the bright spots; script the critical moves; point to the destination A one-page script for the first week; a picture of exhibition night
    Motivate the elephant Energy for the feeling mind Find the feeling; shrink the change; grow your people A student's breakthrough story; "try one ten-minute activity"; "we are coaches"
    Shape the path An easier route Tweak the environment; build habits; rally the herd A login that works first time; a standing weekly check; "most of the school is already doing it"

    The most important lesson of the framework is diagnostic. When a change stalls, ask which of the three is the problem. A teacher who "doesn't know what to do" needs a rider fix, a teacher who "knows but doesn't care" needs an elephant fix, and a teacher who "wants to, but the login keeps failing" needs a path fix. More of the wrong kind of help does nothing.

    Bright Spots and Positive Deviance¶

    Bright spots are examples of success already happening within the system, which can be studied and copied. Instead of asking "what's wrong and how do we fix it?" a leader asks "where is this already working, and what are those people doing?" Positive deviance is the same idea as a method: finding individuals or groups who achieve better results than their peers with the same resources, and spreading what they do. The best-known case is from Vietnam in the 1990s, when Jerry Sternin of Save the Children looked for children who were well-nourished in poor villages and found that their mothers added small shrimp, crabs, and sweet-potato greens to meals and fed the children several small meals a day. Spreading those practices, which were free and already local, reduced malnutrition, and the villagers owned them because they were their own.

    In a district, a bright spot is a classroom where the new tool is working with students like yours, run by a teacher with your constraints. Copying what that teacher does is more believable and cheaper than importing a practice from a conference.

    Teacher Champions and Pilot Classrooms¶

    A teacher champion is a respected teacher who tries a change early, shares honestly how it goes, and helps colleagues. Chapter 15's advice was to choose the respected skeptic who changed their mind, not the biggest enthusiast. A champion needs support: protected time, a reduced load elsewhere, and recognition, since the risk of burning out the first adopter is real. A champion who is asked to do everything stops being a model and becomes a cautionary tale.

    A pilot classroom is a single classroom, or a small number, in which a change is tried carefully before it spreads, with a baseline, a plan, support, and a way to measure results. A good pilot has a volunteer teacher, one clear goal, a start and end date (six to eight weeks is common), a baseline measure taken before it starts, support from a coach, a data-privacy plan, and an agreed plan for sharing what is learned. Where possible it is designed like the randomized pilot of Chapter 2, so that its results can be believed by skeptics.

    Early visible results are the quick, concrete, easily seen outcomes that a pilot or champion produces, shared widely, which convince others that the change works. They have to be real and related to the change: hours of grading saved, a count of students who mastered a concept, a student's work shown at a staff meeting. They are Kotter's short-term wins, made specific.

    Worked Example: A Pilot Plan on One Page¶

    Element Lincoln's plan for the afternoon-project pilot
    Teacher Ms. Rivera, a volunteer, and a champion
    Scope One seventh-grade section of 28 students
    Goal Test whether teams of four with defined roles complete a project better than a comparison section
    Duration Six weeks
    Baseline A teamwork rubric scored in week 0 for both sections
    Support A coach one hour a week; a half-day of planning time
    Measure The same rubric at week 6, scored by a second teacher who does not know which section is which
    Early visible result A student exhibition at week 6
    Sharing A fifteen-minute talk at a staff meeting, with a student presenter

    The plan fits on a page and could begin next Monday.

    Shrinking the First Step and Compounding Small Changes¶

    Shrinking the first step is the practice of making the first action so small that doing it is easy and even a little silly to refuse. Instead of "adopt the new model," a leader asks for "try this ten-minute activity once." It works on the elephant: a small step costs little effort, produces a success, and shifts how the person sees themselves ("I'm someone who tries this"). It also supplies information for the rider, since the person now knows something real about the change.

    Compounding small changes is the way a series of small improvements, each modest, adds up to a large one, because each builds on the one before. If a teacher improves some skill by one percent a week, the improvements multiply. Over 52 weeks they compound to \( 1.01^{52} \approx 1.68 \), about 68 percent better than the start, which is more than 52 percent, the simple sum. The illustration is deliberately simple, and real skills do not improve at a constant rate, but the principle holds: small, repeated steps beat large, rare ones, because they are more likely to be done at all.

    Worked Example: A Ladder of First Steps¶

    For a teacher with a personal concern ("Will I be able to do this?"), the leader offers steps that grow slowly.

    Week Step Time required
    1 Open the textbook page and try one simulation yourself 10 minutes
    2 Use one simulation with one class for the warm-up 10 minutes
    3 Add one self-check question and read the results 15 minutes
    4 Look at the dashboard together with the coach 20 minutes
    6 Run one full lesson in the new format 50 minutes

    The teacher has done a full lesson by week 6 without ever being asked to "adopt the new model." Each step was small enough to finish.

    Ask for the smallest step that counts

    When someone hesitates, don't argue for the big change. Ask for the smallest step that would still tell them something real, such as one ten-minute activity. A step small enough to say yes to is worth more than a bold request that gets "let me think about it."

    Coaching, Observation, and Learning Communities¶

    Professional development is the ongoing learning of teachers and other staff to improve their practice. Research on what works, summarized by Laura Desimone, finds five features of effective professional development: a content focus tied to what teachers teach, active learning in place of listening to lectures, coherence with other goals and with teachers' needs, sufficient duration over weeks or months, and collective participation by groups of teachers from the same school. A single day of training with a binder is the weakest form and, unfortunately, the most common.

    Staff AI literacy is the knowledge and skill staff need to understand what AI tools are, judge when to use them and when not to, evaluate their output, and protect student data (Chapters 5, 10, and 12). It is a part of professional development, and an early one, because teachers who do not understand the tools cannot guide students in using them well, and teachers who do not understand the risks cannot answer families' questions.

    Instructional coaching is a form of professional development in which a trained colleague works with a teacher one-on-one or in small groups over time: observing, modeling, planning, giving feedback, and helping the teacher try new practices. It is the best-supported form of professional development. A 2018 review of 60 studies by Matthew Kraft, David Blazar, and Dylan Hogan found that coaching improved teachers' instruction by about half a standard deviation and student achievement by about 0.18 standard deviations, with larger effects for coaching that was in person and not too widely spread. It addresses the task concerns of CBAM (Chapter 18) and the ability element of ADKAR, because it supplies practice and feedback in the teacher's own classroom.

    Peer observation is the practice in which teachers watch each other teach, for learning and not for evaluation, usually with a short conversation before and after. Its power is observability, one of Rogers' five attributes (Chapter 15): teachers see the change working in a real room with real students. It must be safe, so it works best with a protocol, such as the observer looking for one thing the host asked about, and with a firm rule that what is seen is not used to evaluate.

    A professional learning community (PLC) is a group of teachers who meet regularly to improve student learning together by examining evidence and sharing practice. The educators Richard DuFour and Robert Eaker built the model around a focus on learning, a collaborative culture, and attention to results. A PLC works through four questions: What do we want students to learn? How will we know when they have learned it? What will we do if they have not? What will we do if they already have? These four questions are mastery learning (Chapter 11) in a team routine, and the dashboards of Chapters 8 and 9 supply the evidence for the second question.

    Worked Example: A Fifteen-Minute PLC Routine¶

    Lincoln's seventh-grade math team meets weekly for fifteen minutes, using the dashboard.

    Minutes Step Question
    0–3 Look at the class mastery heatmap (Chapter 8) Which concept has a dark column?
    3–8 Share what is working for students who got it What did the bright spot do?
    8–12 Plan for students who haven't Who will we pull aside, and with what?
    12–15 Plan for students who already know it What will they do with the extra time?

    The routine takes no extra time that the team does not already spend, and it turns the dashboard into a decision.

    Matching the Strategy to the Person¶

    Most of the chapter so far lists tools. This section is about choosing among them.

    Matching strategy to concern is selecting a microstrategy that answers the CBAM stage of concern (Chapters 17 and 18) that a person actually holds. Matching strategy to adopter is selecting a strategy that fits the person's category on the adoption curve (Chapter 15). Together they say that the right move depends on who and where.

    Person's concern Typical statement Microstrategy that fits
    Informational (stage 1) "What does it actually look like?" Peer observation; a pilot-classroom visit
    Personal (stage 2) "Will I be able to do this?" Shrink the first step; a private demonstration
    Task (stage 3) "How do I fit it in? The logins fail." Instructional coaching; fix the obstacle
    Consequence (stage 4) "Are students better off?" Share early visible results; examine data in a PLC
    Collaboration (stage 5) "How can we improve it together?" A professional learning community; peer observation
    Adopter category What they respond to Microstrategy that fits
    Innovators Permission and resources to try Give them a pilot classroom and a small budget
    Early adopters Strategic vision and a chance to lead Make them champions; ask for their input on the plan
    Early majority Proof from peers and a smooth path Peer observation; a champion's story; remove obstacles
    Late majority Reassurance and pressure from norms Make the practice visible and routine; mandatory basics with support
    Laggards Respect and no surprises Listen to their concerns; give time; do not shame

    Resistance, Concerns, and Job Fears¶

    Resistance to change is opposition, active or passive, to a proposed change. It is usually treated as a problem, and the research suggests a different view: resistance is often information. In a 2009 article, Jeffrey Ford and Laurie Ford argued that resistance can be a resource, since people who push back often see real problems that the leaders do not.

    Sources of resistance are the reasons people resist, and they are worth sorting, because each calls for a different response:

    • Loss of identity, competence, routines, or status (Chapter 17).
    • Uncertainty: not knowing what the change means.
    • Lack of trust in the leaders or the process, often from past failed reforms.
    • Disagreement on substance: a belief that the proposal is wrong.
    • Workload and capacity: no time or support to do it.
    • Poor communication: not having been told, or having been told too late.

    A legitimate concern is a worry about a change that has a real basis in evidence or experience, and which the leader should take seriously and address. Many of the concerns teachers and families raise about AI, such as student privacy (Chapter 10), over-reliance (Chapter 12), screen time (Chapter 2), thin evidence (Chapter 11), and unequal access (Chapter 3), are legitimate. Treating them as resistance to be overcome throws away the people who have noticed the real risks.

    Fear of job loss is the worry that the change will make a teacher's job unnecessary. It is perhaps the strongest concern, and it deserves a straight answer, not a slogan. The honest answer, based on the design of this book, is that the model changes teachers' work and does not remove the need for it: the afternoon needs more adults who can coach, facilitate, and debrief, and the morning needs a coach who knows when a student needs a human explanation. The Stanford study of Tutor CoPilot, described in the AI-First Stories appendix, found that AI worked best when it helped human tutors do better, and not when it replaced them. A leader should not promise what she cannot guarantee, but she can say what the plan assumes, and put it in writing.

    Responding with empathy is meeting a concern by first understanding and acknowledging it, and only then responding with information or options. It has four moves: listen without interrupting, acknowledge the feeling and the reason ("That's a real worry, and it makes sense given what you've seen before"), ask a question to understand more, and respond with facts, options, or a commitment. It is active listening (Chapter 13) applied to change.

    Worked Example: Four Replies to a Worried Teacher¶

    A teacher says, "I'm afraid this means my job won't exist in five years."

    Reply Kind Effect
    "You're overreacting; nobody is losing their job." Dismissive Closes the conversation; trust falls
    "That's not what the plan says." Defensive Argues with the feeling; no acknowledgment
    "Don't worry, everything will be fine." Reassurance without substance Sounds kind and answers nothing
    "That's a real worry, and it makes sense. Here is what the plan assumes about teachers' roles, and what we can and can't promise. What would you want in writing?" Empathic and substantive Acknowledges, informs, and asks

    Don't promise what you can't guarantee

    A common trap is answering job fears with "nobody will lose their job." It feels kind, and if the promise cannot be kept it destroys trust. Say what the plan assumes, what you can commit to in writing, and what you don't know yet.

    Stakeholders: Families, Unions, the Board, and Students¶

    A stakeholder is any person or group with an interest in, or influence over, a change (Chapter 2). Stakeholder engagement is the practice of involving stakeholders in planning and carrying out a change, so that they shape it, understand it, and support it. A stakeholder adoption map is a simple chart that lists each stakeholder group and records where it stands on the adoption curve and what its main concern is, so that the leader can plan a move for each.

    Worked Example: A Stakeholder Adoption Map¶

    Stakeholder Where they stand Main concern First move
    Teachers in the pilot Innovators and early adopters Support and recognition Make them champions
    Other teachers Early and late majority Workload; "will it work for my students?" Peer observation; a coach
    Families Mixed; many pragmatists Screen time, privacy, "will my child still learn to think?" A parent night; a handout (Chapter 8)
    The teacher union Cautious Workload, evaluation, job security, data use Early meeting and written assurances
    The school board Pragmatist buyers Cost, evidence, risk A short, honest board presentation
    Students Often early adopters Fairness; voice Ask them; use their feedback

    Families and the Community¶

    Family communication is the regular, two-way exchange of information between the school and students' families. For a change like this one it needs to be early, specific, and honest, and it needs to go both ways. Communicating before rumors is the discipline of telling families and staff about a change before they hear about it elsewhere, because rumors, once started, are far harder to correct than a clear statement is to make. A leader who waits for the plan to be perfect lets the rumor mill write it. Silence is read as secrecy. Community communication extends the same practice to the wider community: local newspapers, community groups, employers, and neighbors without children in the school, whose taxes and goodwill the school depends on.

    Unions, the Board, and Students¶

    A teacher union is an organization that represents teachers in negotiating pay, working conditions, and policies with their employer, and in many districts it holds contractual rights that bear directly on a change: the length of the work day, class size, evaluation, and what teachers can be required to do. In the United States the main unions are the National Education Association and the American Federation of Teachers, and both have engaged with AI: the NEA has adopted a policy statement on AI in education, and in 2025 the AFT launched a national academy to train teachers in AI with support from several technology companies. Union engagement is the practice of involving the union early and as a partner: sharing plans before they are final, asking for its concerns, and agreeing on how workload, evaluation, and data will be handled, often in a written memorandum. A union that has been consulted can be a champion. One that learns of a change from the newspaper will oppose it, with good reason.

    A board presentation is the report to the school board, whose approval and budget a change depends on. It works best when it is short, honest, and organized around the board's questions: what are we trying to do, what is the evidence, what will it cost, what are the risks and how will we manage them, and how will we know if it is working? A board hears from every stakeholder, so a presentation that names the concerns and the responses earns more trust than one that sells.

    Student voice is the participation of students in decisions that affect them: giving opinions, shaping choices, and sometimes sharing in the decisions themselves. Roger Hart's 1992 "ladder of participation" describes eight rungs, from tokenism, where students are decoration, to projects in which young people initiate and share decisions with adults. Students are the people most affected by an AI-native school, and often the quickest to notice what is not working, such as a confusing interface, or a tutor that hands over answers. A leader who asks them gets information no one else has.

    Everyone with a stake sees a different school

    Notice how the same plan looks different from each seat: the teacher sees workload, the parent sees screens and privacy, the union sees contracts, the board sees cost, and the student sees whether it's fair. A leader's job is to hold all five views at once, which is why a stakeholder map is worth drawing.

    Back to Monday¶

    Mr. Santos's question now has an answer, and it is a list of five things: pick one teacher and ask for one small step; walk one skeptic into a colleague's classroom; fix the one obstacle that has been blocking the logins; write the first honest letter to families, including the dip; and call the union representative before the board meeting. None of it needs a budget, and all of it is matched to a person.

    The story has both sides. The benefit: moves small enough to start tomorrow and matched to what each person needs. The risks: treating microstrategies as a replacement for the plan, wearing out the champion, calling legitimate concerns resistance, promising what can't be kept, and leaving stakeholders to find out from rumors. The response is the combination in this chapter: the large plan with the small moves, a diagnosis before each move, empathy before information, and the families, the union, and the students at the table from the start.

    Small moves are real leadership

    If a hallway conversation or a ten-minute classroom visit feels too small to count as leading a change, remember the compounding arithmetic: small steps taken often beat big ones taken rarely. Pick one person and one move this week.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A microstrategy is a small action that moves one person; holistic plus micro change combines the whole-organization plan with a stream of microstrategies.
    • The Switch framework: direct the rider (bright spots, script the critical moves, point to the destination), motivate the elephant (find the feeling, shrink the change, grow identity), and shape the path (tweak the environment, build habits, rally the herd). Diagnose which one is stalled.
    • Bright spots and positive deviance find success already in the system; a teacher champion and a pilot classroom produce early visible results.
    • Shrinking the first step and compounding small changes make change doable; 1 percent a week for 52 weeks compounds to about 68 percent.
    • Professional development works with content focus, active learning, coherence, duration, and collective participation; staff AI literacy comes early; instructional coaching, peer observation, and a professional learning community have the strongest support.
    • Matching strategy to concern (CBAM stage) and to adopter (adoption category) chooses the move.
    • Resistance to change has sources; a legitimate concern deserves a response; fear of job loss needs an honest answer, not a slogan; responding with empathy comes before information.
    • Stakeholder engagement with a stakeholder adoption map covers family communication (communicating before rumors, community communication), union engagement with the teacher union, a board presentation, and student voice.

    You just learned the small, concrete moves that carry a change, how to match each move to the person in front of you, and how to treat resistance as information.
