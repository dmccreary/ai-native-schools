    Quiz: The Five-Level Model of Intelligent Textbooks¶

    Test your understanding of the five-level model, the SAE ideas it borrows, and how to classify products and match safeguards to each level with these review questions.

    1. Which level of the five-level model is defined by changing what each student sees based on a stored record of that student's goals and progress?¶

    1. Level 2, interactive textbook
    2. Level 4, chatbot textbook
    3. Level 5, autonomous textbook
    4. Level 3, adaptive textbook
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. A Level 3 adaptive textbook keeps a record of what each student has done and mastered and uses it to choose what comes next. The defining step up from Level 2 is memory about an individual: a Level 2 textbook responds to actions on the page but stores nothing between visits. Level 4 adds conversation and Level 5 adds real-time content generation, but stored-record adaptation is what first appears at Level 3.

    Concept Tested: Level 3 Adaptive Textbook

    See: Level 3: Adaptive Textbooks

    2. The book borrows three lasting effects of the SAE J3016 standard for self-driving cars. What are they?¶

    1. Lower prices, faster adoption, and fewer accidents
    2. A shared vocabulary, graduated regulation, and realistic expectations
    3. Federal funding, vendor certification, and independent testing
    4. Full automation, liability insurance, and a single national rule
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. J3016 gave the auto industry a shared vocabulary for "self-driving" claims, supported graduated regulation in which rules get stricter as the system takes on more responsibility, and set realistic expectations by making clear that Level 5 remained a goal while Level 2 systems were on the road. The textbook model borrows all three. A single national rule for all levels is the opposite of graduated regulation.

    Concept Tested: SAE J3016 Standard

    See: The SAE Levels: A Model from Self-Driving Cars

    3. Why does the chapter call Level 2 the sweet spot of the book's low-cost design?¶

    1. It is the only level that uses a large language model to explain concepts
    2. It tracks each student's mastery without needing any student accounts
    3. It responds to learners' actions but stores nothing, so it has almost no privacy burden
    4. It is the level most commercial vendors already sell to large school districts
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A Level 2 interactive textbook responds through embedded simulations, self-check quizzes, a learning graph, and path recommendations, but it does not need to know who the learner is and stores nothing between visits. That means essentially none of the privacy burden of higher levels, and ordinary web pages cost little to host and nothing to copy. Tracking mastery requires a stored record, which is Level 3, and Level 2 needs no language model.

    Concept Tested: Level 2 Interactive Textbook

    See: Level 2: Interactive Textbooks

    4. A demo shows web pages with simulations and quizzes, plus a chat box that answers students' questions about the fixed chapters. Everyone gets the same chapter order, and no student records are kept. At what level should the principal classify it?¶

    1. Level 4, because students can hold an open conversation about the material
    2. Level 2, because no student records are stored between visits
    3. Level 5, because the AI writes new answers in real time for each student
    4. Level 3, because the chat box adapts its replies to each question
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The checklist asks whether a student can hold an open conversation with the product about the material; if so, it is at least Level 4. Like the "autonomous" product in Ms. Okafor's emails, a chat box beside fixed chapters is a Level 4 chatbot textbook. Level 5 requires generating the textbook content itself for each student, not answering questions about prepared chapters. Responding to a question is not the stored-record adaptation that defines Level 3.

    Concept Tested: Level Classification

    See: Using the Levels to Evaluate Products and Claims

    5. The chapter says Level 3 is the "privacy inflection point." What makes it parallel to Level 3 in the SAE car levels?¶

    1. At both, the product first becomes affordable for most ordinary buyers
    2. At both, a human must supervise the system at every moment
    3. At both, the system stops working outside a defined area
    4. At both, the system takes on a new responsibility that changes the rules
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. For cars, Level 3 is where the system takes primary control, so liability questions arise. For textbooks, Level 3 is where the system begins to track individual learning, so privacy laws, governance duties, and bias checks come in. The chapter calls this the reason the framework is more than a metaphor: the level at which the system takes on a new kind of responsibility is the level at which the rules must change. Constant human supervision describes SAE Level 2.

    6. A district's level-specific policy follows the chapter's recommended safeguards. A school wants to adopt a Level 4 chatbot textbook. Which safeguards apply?¶

    1. Every safeguard from Levels 1 through 4, from accessibility to chat guardrails
    2. Only the Level 4 safeguards: hint-first rules, logging, and age limits for chat
    3. Only the privacy review and consent process, since chat products store records
    4. Only human review of AI-generated lessons, since chat output is generated by AI
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter says each safeguard keeps applying at higher levels, so a Level 4 product needs the Level 1, 2, and 3 safeguards as well: accessibility checks, simulation accuracy checks, records review, parent consent, and a bias audit, plus hint-first rules, logging, and age limits for chat. Applying only the Level 4 rules is the tempting mistake. Human review of generated lessons is first required at Level 5, where the system writes the content itself.

    7. How does full personalization at Level 5 differ from adaptation at Level 3?¶

    1. Level 3 changes the content itself; Level 5 changes only the order of lessons
    2. Level 3 changes the order of prepared content; Level 5 changes the content itself
    3. Level 3 needs no student data; Level 5 needs only the student's reading level
    4. Level 3 requires a chatbot; Level 5 works without any language model at all
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A Level 3 textbook selects and orders prepared content from a library based on a student's record. A Level 5 system generates explanations, examples, exercises, and simulations on the spot, adapting along every dimension at once: goals, prior knowledge, pace, interests, language, reading level, and accessibility needs. The first option reverses the two. Level 3 does require stored student data, and it needs no chatbot; adaptive math platforms using simple rules existed long before ChatGPT.

    8. A board member argues that only products labeled "AI-powered" should be considered intelligent textbooks. Why does the chapter's framework reject this reasoning?¶

    1. Because AI-powered products are banned from classrooms until they reach Level 5
    2. Because intelligent textbooks are defined by being printed with interactive features
    3. Because levels depend on capability delivered, and some need no AI model
    4. Because the label "AI-powered" applies only to Level 1 and Level 2 products
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The level depends on what the product actually does for students, not on whether an AI model is inside, so "AI-powered" is neither necessary nor sufficient for any level. A Level 2 textbook can do its work with careful design and simple software, and a Level 3 platform can adapt with simple rules. Meanwhile "AI-powered" can describe a PDF with a chat box. The chapter treats such marketing claims as uninformative and tells buyers to ask what the product does, using what data.

    9. According to the chapter, what determines a product's level?¶

    1. The most advanced feature listed in the vendor's brochure or website
    2. The highest defining capability the product delivers to students in use
    3. The price per student, since higher levels cost more to build
    4. Whether the product contains a large language model or a simpler AI model
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The rule that makes classification workable is that a product's level is the highest defining capability it actually delivers to students in use, not the highest one in its brochure. A product level assessment tests what the product does in a demonstration. Brochure features are vendor self-reports to be tested, and price and the presence of an AI model do not set the level; the chapter warns that "AI-powered" is not a level.

    See: The Five Levels at a Glance

    10. A vendor describes its product as "just a simple Level 2 interactive book." During a demo, a teacher reloads a page and the product remembers which problems the student got wrong last week. What should the school conclude?¶

    1. The product is Level 2 as claimed, since remembering errors is a navigation aid
    2. The product is Level 1, since it shows the same problems to every student
    3. The vendor has overclaimed, so the school should reject the product outright
    4. The product keeps student records, so it is Level 3 and needs a privacy review
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter's reset test says that if a page remembers where you were or what you got wrong after a reload, it is keeping a record about you, which is Level 3. This is an underclaim, and the chapter notes it matters because a vendor that does not realize it stores student records may also not be protecting them. The product therefore needs the Level 3 safeguards, including a review of storage, access, and deletion. The vendor understated the level rather than overclaiming it.
