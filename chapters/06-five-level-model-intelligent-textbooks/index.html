    The Five-Level Model of Intelligent Textbooks¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter presents the book's central organizing idea: the five-level model of intelligent textbooks, adapted from the SAE J3016 levels for self-driving cars. It walks through static, interactive, adaptive, chatbot, and autonomous AI textbooks, and teaches readers to classify an existing textbook or AI product by its level. Readers finish able to place any learning product on the five-level scale and explain what distinguishes each level from its neighbors.

    Three Emails, Three Promises¶

    On a single Monday, Ms. Okafor receives three emails from three vendors. The first offers an "AI-powered interactive textbook." The second sells an "adaptive, personalized learning platform." The third advertises an "autonomous AI textbook." Each comes with a demo link and a request for a meeting. The board wants to know which one to pilot and what to ask each vendor.

    The difficulty is that all three phrases sound alike and may mean very different things. One product might be a PDF with a chat box. Another might be a carefully built set of simulations with no AI in it at all. A third might store detailed records of every student's mistakes. Without a precise vocabulary, a school cannot compare them, price them, or decide what privacy protection each one needs.

    This chapter supplies that vocabulary. It presents the book's central framework, the five-level model of intelligent textbooks, and shows how to use it to cut through marketing language. Chapters 7 through 12 depend on it, since the cost, the privacy burden, and the guardrails all change from level to level.

    One vocabulary for every vendor email

    By the end of this chapter you'll be able to read any product brochure and say which of five levels it really sits at, what it will cost you in privacy, and what questions to ask. It's the single most useful tool in the book for a buyer. Let's find the balance between excitement about what's possible and precision about what's actually on offer.

    From Print Textbooks to PDFs¶

    Every level in the model is defined against the ordinary textbook, so we begin there.

    A textbook is a structured book of instructional content written to support a course, usually organizing a subject into chapters with explanations, examples, and exercises, and aligned to a curriculum (Chapter 1). Textbooks are the main instructional material in most classrooms, and for many students the textbook is the curriculum as they experience it.

    A print textbook is a textbook published on paper. It is durable and needs no power or network, which is a real advantage. Its content is fixed at the time of printing, so errors persist until the next edition, and every reader gets exactly the same pages in the same order. K-12 districts typically adopt a print textbook for several years at a time.

    A digital textbook is a textbook delivered electronically, to be read on a screen. The term covers a wide range, from a scan of a printed page to a rich web application. What makes a textbook "digital" is the delivery medium, not any new capability.

    A PDF textbook is a digital textbook delivered as a PDF file, which reproduces the fixed layout of the printed page. It can be searched, bookmarked, and carried on a laptop, and it costs little to distribute. But it does not respond to the reader: it shows the same content in the same way to every student, whatever they do. A PDF is a print textbook on a screen.

    An intelligent textbook is a textbook that uses software to go beyond fixed content: it responds to the learner through interactive elements, tracks and adapts to what the learner knows, converses about the material, or generates content. The word intelligent here does not mean that every intelligent textbook contains AI. Some of the most useful ones do their work with careful design and simple software. How much the textbook can do, and what it needs to know about the student to do it, is exactly what the five levels measure.

    Worked Example: What a PDF Cannot Do¶

    Maya opens her science textbook as a PDF. The PDF lets her search for the word "mitochondria," jump to a chapter from a bookmark, and zoom in on a diagram. It cannot change the diagram when she asks "what if the cell has no mitochondria?" It cannot notice that she read the chapter in four minutes and skipped the practice. It cannot give her a harder problem when the easy ones are done. All of these gaps arise because the PDF has no way to receive her actions and respond. Each of the higher levels adds one such capability.

    Classification Frameworks and Shared Vocabulary¶

    A classification framework is a system for sorting things into a small number of named categories according to stated criteria, so that people can describe and compare them consistently. You have already met one: Bloom's Taxonomy of Chapter 1 sorts learning outcomes into six levels by the kind of thinking they demand. Others include the periodic table for chemical elements and the system that biologists use to classify living things.

    A good classification framework creates a shared vocabulary: a common set of terms with agreed meanings that lets people from different groups talk about the same thing without misunderstanding. When a teacher, a principal, a vendor, and a board member all say "Level 3," they should mean the same thing. Without a shared vocabulary, a conversation about AI textbooks becomes a series of different people using the same words for different things.

    The framework in this book is borrowed from a field that faced the same problem: self-driving cars.

    The SAE Levels: A Model from Self-Driving Cars¶

    The SAE J3016 standard, published by SAE International (a professional association of engineers) beginning in 2014, is the document that defines the six levels of driving automation used throughout the automotive industry. It was written to solve a communication problem. Companies were describing their cars as "self-driving," "autopilot," or "autonomous" for systems with very different abilities, and drivers, regulators, and insurers could not tell them apart.

    The levels of driving automation in J3016 run from Level 0 to Level 5, and they are defined by who is responsible for driving in each case.

    SAE level Name Who drives Example
    0 No driving automation The human does everything A car with only warning sounds
    1 Driver assistance The human drives; the system helps with steering or speed Adaptive cruise control
    2 Partial automation The system steers and manages speed; the human must watch at all times Lane-centering plus cruise control
    3 Conditional automation The system drives in some conditions; the human must take over when asked A traffic-jam pilot that hands back control
    4 High automation The system drives itself in a defined area; no human needed there A driverless taxi in a mapped city
    5 Full automation The system drives anywhere a human could Not yet available

    The standard has had three lasting effects, and they are the reasons this book borrows it. First, it gave the industry a shared vocabulary. Second, it supported graduated regulation: rules that become stricter as a system takes on more responsibility, such as a human supervision requirement at Level 2 and liability and safety-case requirements at higher levels, rather than one rule for all "self-driving" cars. Third, it set realistic expectations: it made clear that Level 5, full automation everywhere, remained a goal while Level 2 systems were already on the road, so that buyers could ask "which level?" before trusting a car.

    The textbook model follows the same logic. It has five levels instead of six, and starts at static content instead of a human doing everything, but it keeps the key properties: levels are defined by what the system can do, higher levels shift responsibility from the human to the system, and an important threshold in the middle changes what rules apply. For cars that threshold is Level 3, where the system takes primary control and liability questions arise. For textbooks, it is also Level 3, where the system begins to track individual learning and privacy questions arise.

    Levels describe capability, not quality

    Notice what the car levels measure: who is responsible for the driving, not how good the car is. A well-built Level 2 system can be safer than a sloppy Level 3 one. The same holds for textbooks: a level tells you what the product does and what it needs from the student, never whether it is any good.

    The Five Levels at a Glance¶

    The five-level textbook model classifies intelligent textbooks into five levels by what the textbook can do and how much it needs to know about each student:

    1. Level 1, static textbooks: fixed content with no interactivity, like traditional print and PDF textbooks.
    2. Level 2, interactive textbooks: content that responds to the learner's actions, through simulations, quizzes, learning graphs, and recommended learning paths.
    3. Level 3, adaptive textbooks: content driven by each student's goals and progress, which requires storing student records.
    4. Level 4, chatbot textbooks: conversational tutoring through chatbots and large language models.
    5. Level 5, autonomous AI textbooks: real-time content generation and fully personalized instruction.

    The model was proposed by Dan McCreary in a December 2025 paper, A Five-Level Classification Framework for Intelligent Textbooks: Lessons from Autonomous Vehicle Standards. As a preprint (Chapter 3), the paper had not yet been peer reviewed when this book was written, so treat the exact boundaries between levels as a proposal that may be refined, though the overall structure is easy to check against real products.

    One rule makes classification workable: a product's level is the highest defining capability it actually delivers to students in use, not the highest one in its brochure. The sim below, reused from this book's tooling library, lets you explore the five levels before we look at each in turn.

    Level 1: Static Textbooks¶

    A Level 1 static textbook is a textbook with fixed content and no response to the learner. Print and PDF textbooks are the standard examples. At most they add navigation aids, such as a table of contents, bookmarks, and text search, which help the reader move around but do not respond to what the reader does.

    Level 1 is by far the most common level in use today. Its strengths are real: it is cheap to produce and copy, works everywhere, needs no student data, and raises no privacy questions. Its limits are the ones listed above: one pace, one path, no feedback.

    Level 2: Interactive Textbooks¶

    A Level 2 interactive textbook is a textbook whose content responds to the learner's actions: the learner changes something and the page changes in return. It does not need to know who the learner is, and it stores nothing about them between visits. Because it needs no student records, it carries essentially none of the privacy burden of the higher levels, which is what makes Level 2 the sweet spot of the book's low-cost design.

    Four features characterize Level 2.

    An embedded simulation, which this book calls a MicroSim, is a small interactive model inside the page, running in the browser with no server, in which the learner changes inputs and watches the result. The sims in this book, such as the effect-size visualizer of Chapter 2 and the task-horizon explorer of Chapter 4, are embedded simulations.

    Self-check quizzes give immediate feedback on answers, using the idea from Chapter 2 that quick feedback prevents practicing mistakes.

    A learning graph, covered in Chapter 9, maps each concept and the concepts it depends on. At Level 2 it is a fixed map the textbook author made, shared by every reader.

    Path recommendation is a suggestion, based on the learning graph, of what concept to read or practice next, such as "you opened Ratios; first review Equivalent Fractions." At Level 2 the recommendation depends on what the learner says they know or what they clicked, within a single visit. It does not require a saved record of the learner.

    Worked Example: A Level 2 Page in Action¶

    A student opens a page on unit rates in a Level 2 textbook. She drags a slider for the price of a pack of juice and the bottle count, and the page recomputes the price per bottle. She answers a self-check question and is told immediately that she divided in the wrong order, with a hint. At the bottom the page says, "If equivalent fractions are shaky, try this review." Every other student who opens the page sees the same content. When she closes the browser, the page remembers nothing. The textbook responded to her, and it never needed to know her name.

    The Intelligent Textbook Movement and the Growth of Level 2¶

    The intelligent textbook movement is the growing effort, from researchers, open-education authors, and developers, to build textbooks that go beyond static pages. Research workshops on intelligent textbooks have met alongside the major conferences on AI in education for several years, and the movement began in earnest in 2025, when AI tools made it practical for a single author to produce a complete interactive textbook.

    The growth of Level 2 textbooks is the most visible result. Because a Level 2 textbook is made of ordinary web pages and small simulations, it costs little to host and nothing to copy, and AI authoring tools lower the cost of writing it. Dozens of open Level 2 textbooks, and hundreds of reusable MicroSims, now exist in the catalog that supports this book's own tooling, and the number is rising quickly. As Chapter 7 explains, this is the economic force that makes Level 2 content cheap and often free.

    Check for the reset test

    Here's a quick way to tell Level 2 from Level 3: change something on the page, then reload it. If the page forgets you, it's Level 2. If it remembers where you were, what you got wrong, or what you should do next, it's keeping a record about you, and that's Level 3.

    Level 3: Adaptive Textbooks¶

    A Level 3 adaptive textbook is a textbook that changes what it shows each student based on a stored record of that student's goals and progress. The defining step from Level 2 is memory about an individual: the system keeps a record of what each student has done and mastered and uses it to choose what comes next.

    Two features define Level 3.

    Progress tracking is the recording of what a student has read, attempted, and mastered over time, kept in a store tied to that student. It is the foundation of adaptation: without a record, nothing can adapt. Chapter 8 explains how a standard called xAPI and a Learning Record Store make this possible while keeping the data under the student's and family's control.

    Student goal setting is the student's stating of what they want to achieve, such as passing a particular exam, mastering a unit by a date, or following an interest, which the system uses to shape the path. It builds on the student agency of Chapter 1 by giving the student a voice in the plan.

    An adaptive textbook might use simple rules, such as "if the student scored below 80 percent on the last three questions, review the prerequisite," or a statistical model that estimates mastery. Neither requires a large language model. Adaptive math platforms existed long before ChatGPT, and by this model's definition they are Level 3.

    Level 3 is the privacy inflection point. Below it, a textbook needs almost no student data. At Level 3 and above, it needs detailed learning histories, which brings in the privacy laws, the governance duties, and the checks for bias that Chapter 10 covers. This is the same step as the jump from Level 2 to Level 3 in the car model, where the system takes control and liability questions begin.

    Personalization has a price in privacy

    Here's the tradeoff at the heart of this book: the more a textbook adapts to a child, the more it has to know about that child. That is not a reason to avoid Level 3. It is a reason to decide, before adopting one, exactly what is kept, by whom, and for how long.

    Worked Example: What the Record Contains¶

    After a month, the record for Maya in a Level 3 math textbook holds the following. The values are illustrative.

    Item in the record Example value What the system does with it
    Goal "Be ready for the unit test on March 12" Plans backwards from the date
    Mastery of each concept Equivalent fractions: 40%; Ratios: 25% Sends her back to equivalent fractions
    Time on each page 4 minutes on unit rates Flags that she may have skipped the practice
    Errors made Divided in the wrong order, 6 times Selects practice on the specific mistake
    Last visit Yesterday, 7:40 p.m. Schedules a review of older material

    Each row is useful, and each row is also personal data about a child. Ms. Okafor should ask for every item in this kind of table: What is stored? Who sees it? When is it deleted?

    Level 4: Chatbot Textbooks¶

    A Level 4 chatbot textbook is a textbook that adds a conversational AI, usually built on a large language model, which students can talk to about the material. The chatbot, defined in Chapter 5, can answer questions, rephrase an explanation, and respond to a student's own words, ideally by drawing on the textbook's content so that its answers match what the course teaches.

    Conversational tutoring is instruction carried out through dialogue: the tutor asks questions, listens to the student's answers, gives hints, and adjusts its next question to the response. It imitates what a good human tutor does and is the reason one-to-one tutoring has been so effective in the research (Chapter 11). A well-designed conversational tutor does not hand over answers. It asks "what is the problem asking you to find?" and lets the student do the thinking, which is the hint ladder of Chapter 2.

    A common example is Khan Academy's Khanmigo, launched in 2023 as a tutoring assistant layered on a platform that already tracked student progress. By this model's definitions it is a Level 4 chat tutor on top of a Level 3 platform. This classification is the book's reading of the product, and the vendor may describe it differently.

    Worked Example: Two Chat Tutors, Same Question¶

    Maya types, "What's the price per bottle if 6 bottles cost $4.50?" Compare two Level 4 designs.

    Answer-first tutor Hint-first tutor
    Reply "Divide $4.50 by 6. The answer is $0.75." "What is the problem asking you to find? Which number is the total, and which is the count?"
    Maya's reply "ok" "The total is $4.50 and the count is 6, so... divide?"
    Next move Moves to the next problem "Good. Which goes first, 4.50 or 6? Try it and tell me what you get."
    What Maya practiced Copying an answer Identifying the quantities and choosing the operation
    Risk She cannot repeat this on the test The tutor takes longer, and a frustrated student may give up

    Both tutors are Level 4. The second is the one the evidence of Chapter 2 favors, and the Turkish high-school study the course description mentions found that this design difference mattered more than the power of the model. The remedy for the second design's risk is not to drop the hints but to add support: after two honest tries the tutor offers a worked example of a different problem.

    Level 4 adds the new risks of Chapter 5: hallucination, and students asking for answers instead of thinking. It also adds a per-conversation computing cost. It needs guardrails: hint-first behavior, limits on age and topic, and logs that teachers can review. Chapter 12 develops these.

    Level 5: Autonomous AI Textbooks¶

    A Level 5 autonomous textbook is a textbook that the AI generates and adapts for each student on its own, in real time, without a human author creating the content in advance. Where a Level 4 chatbot answers questions about a textbook someone wrote, a Level 5 system writes the textbook itself for the student in front of it.

    Two features define Level 5.

    Real-time content generation is the creation of explanations, examples, exercises, simulations, and assessments at the moment a student needs them, rather than their selection from a library prepared in advance.

    Full personalization is adaptation of the content along every relevant dimension at once: the student's goals, prior knowledge, pace, interests, language, reading level, and accessibility needs. A Level 3 textbook changes the order of prepared content; a fully personalized Level 5 textbook changes the content itself.

    Worked Example: A Level 5 Lesson for Maya¶

    Suppose Maya's record says she is weak on equivalent fractions, loves drawing, and reads two grades above level. A Level 3 textbook would select the prepared equivalent-fractions lesson from a library. A Level 5 system would instead write one: an explanation that uses the area of drawn tiles, since she likes drawing, at her reading level, with six new practice problems built around her recent errors, and a short simulation made on the spot to let her slide a fraction bar. No human wrote that lesson, and no human has read it before she does. The benefit is a lesson no library could contain. The risk is that nobody has checked it. If the explanation has a small error, Maya learns the error. That is why the safeguard table below puts human review of generated lessons first at Level 5.

    Level 5 is where the arithmetic of Chapter 5 matters. Writing and checking a chapter-sized lesson with simulations takes tens of hours of human work, so a system that could do it reliably needs a task horizon well beyond one day at high reliability. On the book's central trend that point is about three years away, and the doubts of Chapter 5 apply fully. Level 5 also has the largest verification problem: when no one has reviewed the content in advance, someone has to ensure it is accurate, appropriate, and free of bias.

    The table summarizes the five levels. Each row restates what you have just read; use it as a reference.

    Level Name What it adds What it needs to know about the student Main new risk
    1 Static Nothing beyond fixed content Nothing One pace for everyone
    2 Interactive Responds to actions: simulations, quizzes, graph, paths Nothing stored Poorly built or inaccurate simulations
    3 Adaptive Adapts to goals and progress A stored record of each student Privacy, bias, data breaches
    4 Chatbot Conversation about the material The record plus the conversation Hallucination; answers instead of thinking
    5 Autonomous Generates the textbook itself per student Everything above, in real time Unreviewed content; loss of human oversight

    Levels 3 to 5 on the Horizon and the Adoption Timeline¶

    A level adoption timeline is an estimate of when products at each level become available and widely used. The book's prediction (Chapter 5) is that within three to four years almost every student could have access to a free, high-quality interactive textbook. Level 2 textbooks are already being published in growing numbers, and Levels 3 to 5 on the horizon describes the rest: levels that exist in early form or in the lab and are not yet routine.

    Level Status as this book is written What would have to be true for wide adoption
    1 Dominant Already widespread
    2 Growing quickly; often free Authors and schools adopt open Level 2 textbooks
    3 Available in adaptive platforms, mostly commercial Privacy governance and standards such as xAPI become routine
    4 Early products; fast-moving Guardrails and independent evidence of benefit
    5 Not yet reliable Long-task reliability, content verification, and public trust

    On the central trend of Chapter 5, the horizon needed to write and check a lesson at 80 percent reliability is reached around mid-2028, which would make Level 5 technically plausible about three years out, with trust and regulation likely to arrive later. This is a projection with all the caveats of the last chapter, not a schedule.

    Using the Levels to Evaluate Products and Claims¶

    The levels become a working tool when you apply them to real products and real marketing. This section defines the terms used in that work.

    Level classification is the act of assigning a product to one of the five levels according to the capabilities it actually delivers. Product level assessment is the fuller process a school follows to do so, and it has four steps: gather the product's documentation and a demonstration; test what the product does for students, not what the brochure says; identify the highest defining capability delivered; and record what student data it needs at that level. The checklist for a product level assessment asks:

    1. Does the product do anything but show fixed content? If not, it is Level 1.
    2. Does it respond to the learner's actions on the page? If so, it is at least Level 2.
    3. Does it keep a record of each student and change what it shows from that record? If so, it is at least Level 3.
    4. Can a student hold an open conversation with it about the material? If so, it is at least Level 4.
    5. Does it generate the textbook content itself, for each student, in real time? If so, it is Level 5.

    Level terminology precision is the discipline of using the level names exactly as defined, so that a claim of "Level 3" means one thing wherever it is made. Precision protects buyers, and it matters because the common marketing words do not map to levels.

    AI-powered marketing claims are product descriptions that use "AI-powered," "intelligent," "personalized," or "adaptive" without saying what the system does or what data it uses. They are common and uninformative. "AI-powered" can describe a PDF with a chat box, a simple adaptive scheduler with no AI model, or a fully generative system. Because the level depends on capability and not on whether an AI model is inside, "AI-powered" is neither necessary nor sufficient for any level.

    A vendor level claim is a vendor's statement of the level of its own product. It is a self-report in the sense of Chapter 3: the vendor has an interest in the claim. The right response is not to reject it but to test it using the product level assessment, and to ask for a demonstration of each defining capability. The usual error is an overclaim, in which a vendor states a level above what the product delivers, and the sim above gave you practice spotting them. An underclaim, in which a product delivers more than it admits, matters too, because a vendor that does not realize it stores student records may also not be protecting them.

    'AI-powered' is not a level

    A common trap is accepting "AI-powered" or "personalized" as if it described capability. Instead, ask four things: what does it do for the student, using what data, stored where, and for how long? The answers place the product on the ladder and tell you what protections it needs.

    Graduated Regulation and Level-Specific Policy¶

    The SAE standard supported graduated regulation: rules that get stricter as a system takes on more responsibility. The same approach suits textbooks, because the risks change from level to level. A rule strict enough for Level 5 is wasteful at Level 1, and a rule written for Level 1 is dangerous at Level 5.

    A level-specific policy is a school or district policy that sets different requirements for products at different levels. It is the practical result of the model. For example, a policy might say that any product at Level 3 or above must pass a privacy and data-governance review before use. This book recommends the following minimum safeguards. They are recommended practice, not legal requirements, and the real legal duties, such as FERPA and COPPA, are the subject of Chapter 10.

    Safeguard First required at Reason
    Accessibility check of every page (screen reader, keyboard) Level 1 Every student must be able to use the product
    Independent accuracy check of simulations by a subject expert Level 2 Interactive models can mislead if they are wrong
    Review of how student records are stored, who sees them, and when they are deleted Level 3 The product now holds personal data
    Parent notice and consent process for collecting student learning data Level 3 Families have a right to know and to decide
    Bias audit of how the system chooses next steps for different groups Level 3 Adaptive choices can treat groups unequally
    Hint-first rules and logging for the chat tutor, reviewed weekly Level 4 Chat can give answers and can hallucinate
    Age limit and supervision rules for open-ended chat Level 4 Open conversation with minors needs boundaries
    Human review of AI-generated lessons before students see them Level 5 No author has checked the content in advance

    Each safeguard keeps applying at higher levels, so a Level 4 product needs the Level 1, 2, and 3 safeguards as well.

    Setting Realistic Expectations¶

    The last idea borrowed from the SAE standard is realistic expectations: making clear what each level can and cannot do today, so that buyers neither expect too much nor dismiss what works. A Level 2 textbook is not a tutor, and a Level 4 chatbot is not a trained teacher. Equally, a school that dismisses Level 2 as "just web pages" misses the best value for money in the model.

    A useful summary for a school leader is to match the level to the job. Level 2 is excellent for explaining ideas, practicing skills with feedback, and letting students explore simulations, at very low cost and with no privacy burden. Level 3 earns its added burden when a school needs to track mastery and personalize pacing at scale. Level 4 adds the most when a student is stuck and needs a patient conversation, provided guardrails are in place. Level 5 is a promising direction that should be watched, tested in small pilots, and independently evaluated before anyone relies on it.

    Five levels, one question at a time

    If five levels and thirty-odd terms feel like a lot, start with one habit: for any product, ask "what does it do, using what data?" You already used a simpler version of that question in Chapter 2 when you asked "compared with whom, chosen how?" Everything else in this chapter hangs off that one question.

    Back to the Three Emails¶

    Ms. Okafor now has a plan. She replied to each vendor with the same four questions: what does your product do for the student, using what data, stored where, and for how long? She asked for a demonstration of each defining capability, and she told the vendors she would classify the product herself. The Level 2 vendor needed nothing further except an accessibility check. The Level 3 vendor was invited to a privacy review. The "autonomous" vendor, whose product turned out to be a Level 4 chat tutor, was asked about its hallucination controls and given a pilot, not a district contract.

    The story shows both sides. The benefit is clear: the levels let a school buy the right thing, at the right price, with the right protections, and avoid paying a Level 4 price for a Level 1 product. The risk is that a framework can create false confidence: a level is a statement about capability, not quality, and a product can sit at any level and still be a poor one. The response is to treat the level as the first question and the independent evidence of Chapter 2 as the second.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A textbook can be print, digital, or PDF; a PDF textbook is a print textbook on a screen, with fixed content for every reader.
    • An intelligent textbook uses software to respond, adapt, converse, or generate; it does not have to contain AI.
    • A classification framework creates a shared vocabulary; the SAE J3016 standard and its levels of driving automation gave cars a shared vocabulary, graduated regulation, and realistic expectations, and the textbook model borrows all three.
    • The five-level textbook model: Level 1 static, Level 2 interactive, Level 3 adaptive, Level 4 chatbot, Level 5 autonomous. A product's level is the highest defining capability it actually delivers.
    • Level 2 adds embedded simulations, self-check quizzes, learning graphs, and path recommendation, with nothing stored about the student; the intelligent textbook movement and the growth of Level 2 textbooks make it cheap and often free.
    • Level 3 adds progress tracking and student goal setting, and is the privacy inflection point because it keeps records about individuals.
    • Level 4 adds conversational tutoring through a chatbot; Level 5 adds real-time content generation and full personalization.
    • The level adoption timeline has Levels 3 to 5 on the horizon, with Level 5 plausible technically around three years out on the central trend but dependent on reliability and trust.
    • Apply the four steps of product level assessment; level terminology precision matters because AI-powered marketing claims say nothing about level, and a vendor level claim is a self-report to be tested.
    • Level-specific policy matches safeguards to the level where each risk first appears: accessibility at Level 1, simulation accuracy at Level 2, records and bias audits at Level 3, chat guardrails at Level 4, human review of generated lessons at Level 5.
