    Sustaining Change: Systems Thinking and National Adoption¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter completes the change models with their later, sustaining stages: Senge's systems thinking and mental models, the later stages of ADKAR and CBAM, Bridges' new beginnings, the "grammar of schooling," and Estonia's national AI Leap as a whole-system example. Readers finish able to choose a change model suited to a school's size, culture, and history, and to read a simple causal loop diagram.

    A Year Later¶

    A year after the district-wide launch, Ms. Okafor visits a classroom at Eastside Elementary. The software is on every device, the dashboard is open, and the lesson looks, to a casual visitor, like the new model. Then she watches for ten minutes. The students are all on the same page at the same pace. The teacher is lecturing from the front while the software runs, unattended, behind her. The reform is in the room. The change is not.

    It is the oldest failure in school reform and the reason this chapter exists. Chapters 16 and 17 covered getting a change started. This chapter covers the stretch where most changes quietly fail: the middle, when the excitement has faded, the first wins are old news, the confusing in-between is hard, and the pressure has moved on to the next initiative. It completes each model with its later stages and then steps back to the view that helps most at this point: seeing the school as a system.

    Where changes quietly stall

    This chapter is about the middle and the long run of a change: the awkward in-between, the practice that has to become real, and the system that pushes back. You'll learn to tell a reform that's merely in the room from one that has changed the work, and to read a simple diagram of how a school's forces interact. Let's find the balance between starting strong and finishing for real.

    Bridges, Completed: Neutral Zone and New Beginnings¶

    Chapter 17 introduced Bridges' distinction and his first phase. Change versus transition is the distinction between the external event, a new schedule or tool, which can happen on a date, and the internal psychological process of adjusting to it, which takes as long as it takes. Change is a situation, and transition is a process. A leader who treats them as one issues a date and expects people to be ready on it.

    The neutral zone is the middle phase of a transition: the old way has ended and the new way is not yet comfortable. People are confused, anxious, and less productive, and morale often dips. Bridges called it a time of both danger and opportunity: people may withdraw or leave, and they may also be unusually creative, because the old rules no longer bind them. Leaders cannot skip it, and they help by providing temporary structure, communicating often, setting short-term goals, and treating the confusion as normal.

    New beginnings are the third phase, in which people take on a new identity and purpose and the new way becomes their own. Bridges named four things leaders must supply to start a new beginning, the four P's: the purpose behind the change, a picture of what the outcome looks and feels like, a plan for getting there, and a part for each person to play. The last is the most neglected. A teacher who knows the plan but not her own role cannot begin.

    Worked Example: Three Phases for a Veteran Teacher¶

    Phase What Mr. Hale experiences What helps
    Ending He misses the whole-class explanations he built over twenty years Acknowledge the loss; ask which part of his craft to keep
    Neutral zone He is unsure of his role; he is sometimes lecturing, sometimes coaching Temporary routines, a weekly check-in, no pressure to be fluent
    New beginning He leads small-group sessions and is proud of a student breakthrough Purpose, picture, plan, and a named part to play: "reading lead for the small groups"

    ADKAR, Completed: Ability and Reinforcement¶

    The first three ADKAR elements, awareness, desire, and knowledge, are about readiness. The last two are about sustaining.

    ADKAR Ability is the demonstrated capacity to put the change into practice. Knowing how and being able to do it are different, as anyone who has read about swimming and then entered the water knows. Ability comes from practice, coaching, time, and permission to be imperfect. It is the element where the implementation dip of Chapter 16 appears, and the gap between knowledge and ability is where many well-trained teachers stall. ADKAR Reinforcement is what keeps the change in place after it begins: recognition, feedback, accountability, and the removal of the old way. Without reinforcement, people drift back to the familiar. It corresponds to Lewin's refreeze and Kotter's anchoring.

    Element The person's question What builds it A sign it is missing
    Ability "Can I actually do it?" Practice, coaching, time, tolerance for mistakes "I know how, but I can't get it to work in my class"
    Reinforcement "Will this last, and is it noticed?" Recognition, feedback, accountability, removing the old way "It went well for a month, then everything went back"

    CBAM, Completed: Task and Impact Concerns, and Levels of Use¶

    Chapter 17 introduced the first three of CBAM's stages of concern, the self concerns. The remaining four are the ones that appear once a teacher starts using the innovation.

    Task concerns are the concerns of stage 3, management: how to organize time, materials, and logistics in order to use the innovation. "How do I fit this into my fifty minutes? What do I do when the logins fail?" They are practical and they dominate the first months of use. Impact concerns are the concerns of stages 4 to 6: consequence (stage 4), whether students are benefiting; collaboration (stage 5), how to work with colleagues to increase the benefit; and refocusing (stage 6), ideas for improving or replacing the innovation with something better. A teacher with impact concerns is the leader's best asset, and a leader who answers task concerns well lets teachers move on to them.

    Levels of use is CBAM's second dimension: it describes what a person does with the innovation, in eight levels. They run from nonuse (0), through orientation (I, learning about it) and preparation (II, getting ready), to mechanical use (III, short-term, disjointed, focused on getting each step right), routine (IVA, stable use with little change), refinement (IVB, varying the use to increase impact), integration (V, combining efforts with colleagues), and renewal (VI, re-evaluating and seeking major modifications). Where the stages of concern describe what a person feels, the levels of use describe what a person does, and the two can be different: a teacher may be at mechanical use while holding impact concerns.

    Stage of concern Kind Typical statement
    0 Awareness, 1 Informational, 2 Personal Self "What is it? How will it affect me?"
    3 Management Task "How do I fit this in? Why does the login fail?"
    4 Consequence, 5 Collaboration, 6 Refocusing Impact "Are students better off? How can we do this better together?"

    Worked Example: Reading Three Teachers¶

    Teacher says Stage of concern Level of use
    "I use it every day but I'm always behind and scrambling to keep up." Task (3) Mechanical (III)
    "My students who were stuck are moving, and I want to see whether the others are too." Impact: consequence (4) Routine (IVA)
    "Three of us are comparing dashboards to decide how to change the morning." Impact: collaboration (5) Integration (V)

    A coach who answers the first teacher with a lecture on student outcomes has answered a question she has not yet asked. What she needs is help with the logistics.

    Culture, Assessed¶

    Chapter 17 introduced Schein's three levels. They deserve a closer look now, since culture is what pulls a change back.

    Cultural artifacts are the visible and audible products of a culture: the building, the layout of rooms, the schedule, the dress code, the posters, the language people use, the rituals they perform. They are easy to observe and hard to interpret, since the same artifact can mean different things in different places. Espoused values are the goals, philosophies, and ideals that an organization says it holds, found in mission statements, speeches, and how people explain themselves. Basic assumptions are the deepest layer: the unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs about how the world works that shape perception and behavior and are rarely discussed. They are what people would be shocked to hear questioned. Schein's central claim is that the assumptions drive behavior, so that when stated values and assumptions disagree, the assumptions win.

    Culture assessment is the process of finding out what a school's culture actually is, using observation, documents, interviews, and surveys, and especially by looking for gaps between the layers. A simple four-step version: gather artifacts by walking through and watching; compare them with the espoused values in the mission and in conversation; infer the assumptions where the two disagree by asking "why do we do it this way?" repeatedly; and decide which assumptions will help and which will hinder the change.

    Team learning, one of Senge's five disciplines, is the capacity of a group to think together and to produce results beyond what any member could alone. It uses dialogue, a free flow of meaning in which people explore ideas without trying to win, as distinct from discussion, in which positions are defended. Culture assessment works best as a team-learning exercise, since people see their own assumptions only when others show them.

    Mental models, another of Senge's disciplines, are the deeply held pictures and assumptions about how the world works that shape what we see and do. They are the individual form of what Schein calls basic assumptions. A teacher's mental model that "students learn when I explain" will shape how she uses an adaptive textbook whatever the training says. Surfacing and testing mental models is how a school finds the beliefs that stand between it and a new practice.

    Why Reforms Stall¶

    The grammar of schooling, the phrase of David Tyack and William Tobin, popularized with Larry Cuban in Chapter 1, names the persistent regularities in how schools divide time and space, sort students into age-graded classes, assign them to separate classrooms, and split knowledge into subjects. Tyack and Tobin asked in 1994 why such a structure had been so hard to change, and they showed that it is deeply embedded in the expectations of teachers, parents, and policymakers, so that reforms that disturb it are absorbed or rejected.

    Reform without real change is the pattern in which an innovation is adopted in name but leaves the actual work of teaching unchanged. It is the Eastside classroom: the software is running and the lecture goes on. The historian Larry Cuban documented it across a century of technology in schools, summed up in his phrase "oversold and underused," and in an earlier book as "tinkering toward utopia." The reasons are the ones in this chapter: reforms that do not touch the grammar of schooling, the basic assumptions of the culture, or the daily work of teachers get absorbed. The test of a real change is whether the practice is different, which is why the innovation configuration of Chapter 17 matters.

    Change fatigue is the weariness and cynicism that people feel when one change initiative follows another with no time to absorb any. The organizational researcher Eric Abrahamson called the pattern "repetitive change syndrome." In schools it takes the form of teachers who say, "This too shall pass," because past initiatives did. Change fatigue is an argument for focus: fewer changes, kept longer, and a leader who finishes what she starts.

    Whole-organization change is change that affects an entire organization at once, its structure, culture, and routines, as distinct from a change that affects one team. The compressed morning with a pro-social afternoon is whole-organization change, since it alters the schedule, the roles of every adult, and the daily experience of every student. Whole-organization change is slower and riskier than a pilot and cannot be completed by decree. It requires every tool in Chapters 16 to 18, and usually a combination of the large-scale plan and the small-scale moves of Chapter 19.

    In the room isn't the same as in the work

    A common trap is counting logins and device use as proof that a change has happened. Instead, watch what teachers and students actually do, and compare it with the innovation configuration. A school can have 100 percent adoption on the dashboard and no change in practice.

    Seeing the System¶

    Systems thinking, the fifth of Senge's disciplines, is the practice of seeing a situation as a whole, with its connected parts, feedback, and delays, and not as a series of separate events. It asks, "What structure produces this behavior?" instead of "Who is to blame?" The environmental scientist Donella Meadows made the approach accessible in her book Thinking in Systems. Its value in schools is that many problems are produced by the way the parts interact: a policy that helps one group harms another, a fix works briefly and then fails, an effort in one place causes a problem somewhere else.

    A feedback loop is a chain of cause and effect that circles back, so that a change in one element eventually influences itself. There are two kinds. A reinforcing loop amplifies: more of something leads to still more of it, whether a virtuous spiral or a vicious one. A balancing loop resists: it pushes the system toward a goal or limit and counteracts change. Success breeding confidence breeding more success is reinforcing. A thermostat is balancing, and so is an organization's resistance to being pushed too fast.

    A causal loop diagram is a picture of a system's feedback loops: the variables are named, arrows show causal links, and each arrow carries a sign. A plus (+) means the two variables move in the same direction (more of one leads to more of the other), and a minus (−) means they move in opposite directions. Reading a loop is easy: count the minus signs around it. An even number (including zero) makes the loop reinforcing (R), and an odd number makes it balancing (B). Delays, where the effect takes time to appear, are marked with two short lines across the arrow.

    Worked Example: Two Loops in a Rollout¶

    Lincoln's rollout contains two loops. The first is reinforcing.

    Link Sign Meaning
    Student results → Teacher confidence + Better results raise confidence
    Teacher confidence → Quality of use + Confident teachers use it better
    Quality of use → Student results + Better use improves results

    There are zero minus signs, so the loop is reinforcing: success feeds itself, and so does failure, in the other direction. The second loop is balancing.

    Link Sign Meaning
    Rollout speed → Teacher overload + Faster rollout means more work
    Teacher overload → Resistance + More overload means more resistance
    Resistance → Rollout speed − More resistance slows the rollout

    There is one minus sign, an odd number, so the loop is balancing: pushing the rollout faster raises resistance that pushes back. A leader who sees only the first loop will accelerate and be surprised by the second. The two together explain why the Chapter 17 advice to remove obstacles works better than adding pressure.

    Convention for reading the table: the sign in parentheses after a variable is the sign of the link from that variable to the next one in the ring, and the last variable links back to the first. In loop 4, more breach risk means less family trust, so that link is a minus.

    Provenance: The rule follows the standard convention for causal loop diagrams, as described in the chapter. The loops are illustrative, written for this chapter, and the sim must label them "Illustrative loops."

    Rules: Type = Reinforcing if the number of minus signs around the loop is even (0, 2, ...) and Balancing if it is odd. Each loop has exactly one correct type, given in Content. A loop is scored on its first submitted answer only. The score counts first-attempt correct answers out of 6. Mastery is a score >= 5. Zoom and reset are by buttons; mouse-wheel zoom is disabled.

    Learner Activity:

    1. The learner sees one loop drawn as a ring with signed arrows and the two types.
    2. The learner counts the minus signs, selects a type, and submits it. The sim marks it correct or incorrect and shows the feedback text.
    3. If incorrect, the learner may choose again; the second attempt does not change the score. After the second attempt, the correct type and reason are shown.
    4. After all 6 loops, the sim shows the score and the count of minus signs in each loop.
    5. The learner should notice that three loops are reinforcing and three are balancing, and that loop 5 is reinforcing even though it is harmful.

    Feedback: 6 loops, shuffled once per session, two attempts each. Correct: "Correct: [type]. [Why text]." Incorrect: "Not quite. Count the minus signs around the whole loop. [Why text for the correct answer, without naming the type]." After the second attempt the correct type is revealed and the loop counts as missed. A running score "First-try correct: n of 6" is shown. Final message for a score >= 5: "You can read a loop." For a score < 5: "Review the counting rule and try again; the loops will be reshuffled."

    Starting State: Loop 1 of the shuffled order is shown with the two types, the score "First-try correct: 0 of 6," and the prompt "Is this loop reinforcing or balancing?"

    Chapter Anchors: The chapter's worked example uses loop 1 (student results, teacher confidence, quality of use) as reinforcing and loop 2 (rollout speed, teacher overload, resistance) as balancing. The counting rule is even for reinforcing and odd for balancing. The mastery bar is 5 of 6.

    Coherence and Capacity¶

    Two ideas from Michael Fullan, with Joanne Quinn, address how a whole system sustains change.

    The coherence framework is a model, from Fullan and Quinn's 2016 book, of the four elements that, together, make the parts of a school or district pull in one direction: focusing direction (a small number of clear priorities), cultivating collaborative cultures (teams that learn together), deepening learning (improving what happens in classrooms), and securing accountability (internal responsibility first, external second). It addresses the change-fatigue problem directly: a system asked to do twelve unconnected things will do none well, and coherence is the discipline of fewer, connected priorities.

    Capacity building is the development of the knowledge, skills, resources, and motivation of people, individually and collectively, so that they can carry out and sustain a change. Fullan distinguishes it from simply demanding results. A district that tells teachers to adopt a new model, and measures them on it, without investing in their ability, has set a target and built no capacity. Capacity building is the long-run form of ADKAR Ability and Knowledge, applied to the whole system.

    Choosing a Change Model and a National Example¶

    Choosing a change model is the work of selecting the model, or the combination, that fits a particular school's size, culture, history, and the type of change. No model is best for everything. A short guide follows.

    If the school... Lean on Because
    Is small, with a strong collegial culture CBAM, ADKAR, and peer routines Individual concerns and conversations can steer the change
    Is large or a district Kotter's steps, with CBAM for monitoring A sequence and a structure for many people
    Has a closed-door culture Schein, Senge The culture is the main obstacle and must be surfaced
    Has a history of failed reforms Bridges, change fatigue, capacity building People need their losses and the past honored
    Is under time pressure Kotter for the plan, ADKAR for individuals Speed needs a sequence and fast diagnosis

    National AI adoption is the adoption of AI in education by an entire country's school system, led by national policy. It tests every idea in this chapter at the largest scale, and two recent cases show different outcomes. In Estonia, the AI Leap program (Chapter 15) paired the tools with teacher training and built on the country's record of earlier success with school technology, and it is an example of a coherent, capacity-building approach whose results are still unfolding. South Korea offers a contrasting example: in 2025 the government introduced AI-powered digital textbooks in many schools, and, as reported, strong objections from teachers and parents about the pace, screen time, privacy, and workload led the legislature within months to reclassify them as supplementary materials rather than official textbooks. The details of both cases should be checked against current reporting. The contrast, a rollout built with training and trust against a rapid mandate that met resistance, is the lesson of the whole chapter.

    The system pushes back in proportion to the push

    Notice what the balancing loop of the rollout says: the harder you push, the harder the system pushes back. A national rollout that outruns teachers' capacity meets exactly that resistance. Going slower on the push and faster on the capacity is often the faster route.

    Answering the Eastside Classroom¶

    Ms. Okafor's visit to Eastside now has a diagnosis. The reform is in the room and not in the work: teachers are at orientation or mechanical use, still holding task concerns, in the neutral zone of a transition no one acknowledged. The culture assumes lecturing is teaching. The rollout was fast, which raised resistance. Capacity, not just training, was missing, and nothing reinforced the new practice.

    The story has both sides. The benefit: tools that tell a leader why a change has stalled and what to do in each case. The risks: declaring success at adoption, pushing speed that the system pushes back against, changing too many things, and imposing a national or district plan without building capacity. The response is to measure practice and not logins, to expect and honor the neutral zone, to read the culture, to build capacity, and to focus on fewer priorities for longer.

    Slow is allowed

    If a year of work seems to have produced a reform that's only in the room, don't despair; that's the ordinary middle of a real change, and you now have the tools to read it. Pick one classroom, one concern, one loop, and work on that.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Change versus transition: change is an event and transition is an inner process; Bridges' neutral zone is a confusing, necessary middle, and new beginnings need purpose, picture, plan, and part.
    • ADKAR Ability needs practice and coaching, and ADKAR Reinforcement needs recognition and feedback to prevent sliding back.
    • CBAM's task concerns (stage 3) and impact concerns (stages 4 to 6) follow the self concerns; levels of use describe what a teacher does, from nonuse to renewal.
    • Cultural artifacts, espoused values, and basic assumptions are assessed together; culture assessment looks for gaps, mental models are the individual form, and team learning surfaces them.
    • The grammar of schooling explains reform without real change; change fatigue follows a pile of initiatives; whole-organization change needs the whole toolbox.
    • Systems thinking uses feedback loops and the causal loop diagram: an even number of minus signs makes a loop reinforcing, an odd number makes it balancing.
    • The coherence framework and capacity building aim a system at fewer, connected priorities and the people to carry them out.
    • Choosing a change model depends on size, culture, history, and time; national AI adoption shows both a coherent approach (Estonia) and a rapid mandate that met resistance (South Korea, as reported).

    You can tell a real change from a reform in the room

    You just learned how to carry a change through the neutral zone and the practice gap, to read a culture and a feedback loop, and to tell real change from adoption in name only. Chapter 19 gets practical: concrete moves a leader can make next week. One step at a time, together.
