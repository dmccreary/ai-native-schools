    Mastery Learning and the Case for a Compressed Morning¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter makes the case for a shorter academic morning: adaptive and mastery learning, personalized pacing, Bloom's two-sigma problem, one-on-one tutoring, and what AI tutors do well, alongside the critical- and computational-thinking skills a compressed morning is meant to protect time for. Readers finish able to summarize the evidence that two to three hours of adaptive learning can match a traditional day's results.

    The Claim That Needs Evidence¶

    At the board meeting where Ms. Okafor presents the pilot results, Mr. Dang, the skeptical teacher from Chapter 5, asks the question the whole book depends on. "You're telling us students could learn today's material in two or three hours a day instead of the five or six we spend now. I've been teaching for twenty years. Where's the proof?"

    It is the strongest objection to the book's central idea, and it deserves a direct answer. The idea is that if interactive, adaptive textbooks let each student move at their own pace and fill their own gaps, then the hours spent on whole-class explanation and waiting are no longer needed, and a shorter academic morning could free the rest of the day for something else. The "something else" is the subject of Part 3, the pro-social afternoon. This chapter examines whether the compression itself is plausible.

    It does so honestly. The evidence is promising and mixed: some carefully designed studies show large gains in less time, and some real programs have produced disappointing or unclear results. By the end you will be able to say what the evidence supports, what it does not, and which design choices decide the difference.

    Does the evidence hold?

    This chapter takes Mr. Dang's challenge seriously and walks through the best evidence for and against a shorter morning, including the studies that cut the wrong way. By the end you'll be able to summarize what we know and what we don't, in your own words. Let's find the balance between excitement about a shorter day and respect for the data.

    Adaptive Learning and Mastery Learning¶

    Adaptive learning is an approach in which a system adjusts what a student sees, the order, difficulty, and pace, based on data about that student's performance. It is what Level 3 textbooks do (Chapter 6), using the mastery data and learning graph of Chapters 8 and 9. Adaptation can be simple, such as rules that choose the next problem from a mastery record, or statistical, such as a model that estimates the probability the student knows each concept. Well-known examples include the ALEKS mathematics system, based on a theory of "knowledge spaces," and Carnegie Learning's Cognitive Tutor, a mathematics program that tracks each student's skills as they work.

    The evidence for adaptive learning is real and modest. A large randomized study of Cognitive Tutor Algebra I by the RAND Corporation found no effect in the first year of use and an effect of about 0.2 standard deviations in the second year, which, by the benchmarks of Chapter 2, is a large effect for a school intervention but one that took time and teacher training to appear. Adaptive learning is not a miracle; it is a tool whose results depend on how it is implemented.

    Mastery learning is an instructional approach in which students must demonstrate mastery of a concept, typically 80 to 90 percent on a check, before moving on to the next one, and get extra time and different instruction when they do not. The educational psychologist Benjamin Bloom described it in 1968, building on John Carroll's 1963 observation that learning is a function of the time a student actually spends relative to the time they need. If the time allowed is fixed, achievement varies; if achievement is fixed, the time varies. Mastery learning fixes achievement and lets time vary, the reverse of the seat-time model of Chapter 3.

    A 1990 meta-analysis of mastery-learning studies by Chen-Lin Kulik, James Kulik, and Robert Bangert-Drowns found that mastery learning raised exam performance by about half a standard deviation on average, which in the terms of Chapter 2 moves the average student from the 50th to roughly the 70th percentile. Most of those studies were in college and high school courses, many were short, and the quality varied, so the number is a guide and not a promise.

    Worked Example: An Adaptive Session, Item by Item¶

    Adaptive learning follows a rule. Here is a simple one, with five difficulty levels. Start at level 3. Two correct answers in a row at the same level move the student up one level, and one wrong answer moves the student down one. Maya's ten-question session goes as follows. The results are illustrative.

    Question Level Result Next level
    1 3 Correct 3 (one correct so far)
    2 3 Correct 4 (two in a row)
    3 4 Wrong 3
    4 3 Correct 3
    5 3 Correct 4
    6 4 Correct 4
    7 4 Correct 5
    8 5 Wrong 4
    9 4 Correct 4
    10 4 Correct 5

    Six of the ten questions were at levels 4 or 5, where Maya was stretched, and none was wasted on material far below or far above her. A fixed ten-question worksheet at level 3 would have told her teacher only that she can do level 3. The rule also leaves a clear record, since the sequence of levels is itself data about what she can do. It uses no language model: only a rule and a counter.

    Worked Example: A Mastery Learning Cycle¶

    Bloom's mastery learning is a cycle, not a single lesson. Imagine a class of 28 learning equivalent fractions.

    Stage What happens Result
    Teach A short lesson with practice n/a
    First check A formative quiz, with a mastery threshold of 80 percent 17 of 28 students (61 percent) meet it
    Correct and enrich The 11 below the threshold get different instruction (a new explanation, a manipulative, a peer tutor); the 17 who met it do an extension project n/a
    Second check A parallel quiz of equal difficulty 8 of the 11 now meet the threshold
    Result 25 of 28 students (89 percent) have mastered the concept 3 students need more help and are flagged

    Two points matter. The cycle needs different instruction for the second try, not a repeat, since the first explanation already failed these students. And the students who met the threshold were not made to wait, because they did something worthwhile. This is the pattern an adaptive textbook automates: the check, the branch, and the second check happen continuously for every student, and the teacher's time goes to the three who still need a human.

    Worked Example: Fixed Time Versus Fixed Mastery¶

    Recall Chapter 3's class of 20 students who needed between 70 and 260 hours to master a course. The table contrasts what happens in the two models. The values repeat the illustrative numbers from that example.

    Fixed time (150 hours) Fixed mastery (time varies)
    Students who master the course 14 of 20 20 of 20
    Students who do not 6 0
    Idle hours among those who finish early 535 0 (they move on when ready)
    Total hours used 3,000 2,770
    Average hours per student 150 138.5

    Mastery learning gets every student to the goal with fewer total hours in this example. But notice the average: 138.5 hours is only about eight percent below 150. Mastery learning is mainly a way of getting everyone to the goal, not of making the course dramatically shorter. The case for a compressed morning needs more than mastery: it needs the time that students spend on explanation and waiting to shrink as well, which is the subject of the next sections.

    Personalized Pacing and Gap Filling¶

    Personalized pacing is letting each student move through material at the speed that suits them: faster through what they already know, slower through what is new or difficult. It is the self-paced learning of Chapter 1, made practical by software that tracks progress and offers the next step. Gap filling is the diagnosis and repair of missing prerequisite knowledge: finding the root gap, in the sense of Chapter 2, and teaching the missing concept before returning to the grade-level work. It is the remedy for the "missing stair" of Maya's fractions, and, according to the learning-gap analysis of Chapter 1, it is the main reason students who are behind stay behind: they sit through lessons built on foundations they do not have.

    Together they address the main weakness of whole-class instruction. Recall Chapter 1's class of 28, in which only 13 students were "in reach" of any single lesson. Adaptive software can serve all 28 at their own readiness. The question is whether it does so well enough to replace the time the whole class spends together.

    Worked Example: Pacing Does Not Create Time¶

    Personalized pacing lets each student move at their own speed, and it raises a question that is easy to overlook: what happens to the student who needs more time than the year contains? Take the 20 students of Chapter 3, who need between 70 and 260 hours to master a 150-hour course, and suppose each gets 50 minutes a day for 180 days.

    Hours needed Days needed at 50 minutes a day Finishes within the 180-day year?
    70 (fastest) 84 Yes, with 96 days to spare
    100 120 Yes
    150 180 Exactly
    200 240 No: 60 days short
    260 (slowest) 312 No: 132 days short

    Pacing alone lets fast students move on and lets slow students keep working, and it does nothing to make the year longer. The slowest students would need about 1.7 times the daily time to finish in one year. The design has to answer this honestly: protect extra morning time and coaching for the students who are behind (the gap filling of this section), accept that some will finish the course the following year, or both. A model that advertises personalized pacing without a plan for the slower students has moved the problem and not solved it, and the equity commitments of Chapter 20 apply directly.

    Worked Example: Gap Filling Is an Investment¶

    Gap filling costs time up front and saves it later. Return to Maya, who fails unit-rate problems because of a root gap in equivalent fractions (Chapter 2). Compare two responses over a month. The hour estimates are illustrative.

    Reteach unit rates Fill the gap first
    Hours in the first week 3 on unit rates, no progress 3 on equivalent fractions, with mastery reached
    Hours in the second week 3 more on unit rates, still stuck 2 on ratios, now moving
    Hours in the third week 3 more, stuck, frustration grows 2 on unit rates, mastered
    Total after three weeks 9 hours, nothing mastered 7 hours, three concepts mastered

    The gap-filling path is not only cheaper in hours; it ends with three concepts mastered and a student whose confidence has risen. The reteaching path spends more and teaches nothing, because the lesson being repeated rests on a missing stair. This is the main reason the learning graph of Chapter 9 matters: it is how the software finds the stair.

    Pace is a variable, not a constant

    Notice what mastery and pacing change: they treat time as the thing that varies and learning as the thing that's fixed. Our schools do the opposite, so some students finish early and wait and some are moved on before they're ready. Almost everything in this chapter follows from flipping that one assumption.

    Bloom's Two-Sigma Problem and Tutoring¶

    The most famous number in this part of educational research comes from a 1984 article by Benjamin Bloom, titled "The 2 Sigma Problem: The Search for Methods of Group Instruction as Effective as One-to-One Tutoring."

    One-on-one tutoring is instruction in which a single tutor works with a single student, responding moment by moment to what the student says and does. A tutor can ask a question, hear the answer, notice hesitation, give a hint, and adjust. This is the practice that a teacher with 28 students cannot provide, as the arithmetic of Chapter 1 showed: under two minutes per student in a 50-minute period.

    Bloom's two-sigma problem is the finding, reported by Bloom and his graduate students, that students tutored one-on-one using mastery learning performed about two standard deviations better than students in a conventional classroom: the average tutored student scored above about 98 percent of the conventional class. ("Sigma" is the Greek letter used for standard deviation.) The problem in the name is a practical one: tutoring that effective is far too expensive to give to every child, so Bloom asked whether group methods could be found that come close to it. Mastery learning in a group, by comparison, produced about one standard deviation, the middle of three conditions.

    Condition in Bloom's studies Effect compared with a conventional class The average student in this condition scores above
    Conventional class 0 (baseline) 50% of the conventional class
    Mastery learning in a class about 1 standard deviation about 84%
    One-on-one tutoring with mastery learning about 2 standard deviations about 98%

    The two-sigma number has been quoted for forty years, often without the cautions it needs. The original studies were small, ran for a few weeks on a single topic, and tested students on the material that was taught. When researchers later pooled many randomized trials of tutoring in real schools, they found much smaller average effects. A 2020 review of randomized trials of tutoring by Andre Nickow, Philip Oreopoulos, and Vincent Quan found an average effect of about 0.37 standard deviations (a later published version of the review reports a smaller figure, about 0.29). Other work, such as VanLehn's 2011 review of human tutoring and intelligent tutoring systems, found effects of about 0.76 to 0.79 compared with no tutoring for human tutors and for step-by-step computer tutors, with simpler systems doing worse. The economist Paul von Hippel argued in 2024 that part of Bloom's result came from the tutored students taking far more tests with feedback, and that this may explain about half of it. Tutoring is among the most effective interventions known, with effects in the range of a half to a whole standard deviation, and Bloom's two sigma is the upper end of what has been seen in small, careful studies.

    Cost is the other half of the problem. High-dosage tutoring programs for schools, with a tutor working with a small group several times a week, often cost on the order of $1,000 to $2,500 per student per year, which is why they reach only a fraction of the students who need them.

    Worked Example: The Tutoring Arithmetic¶

    Convert the effect sizes into percentiles with the table of Chapter 2, and compare what each would cost at scale for a 12,000-student district. The cost figure for human tutoring is an illustrative midpoint, and the AI figure is the Chapter 7 Level 4 cost.

    Approach Effect size (approximate) Average student's percentile Cost per student per year District of 12,000
    Bloom's small studies 2.0 98th not scalable not scalable
    Pooled randomized tutoring trials 0.37 64th about $1,500 about $18 million
    Well-designed tutoring system about 0.7 about 76th about $23 (Level 4, Chapter 7) about $276,000

    The last row is the hope behind the AI tutor: an effect in the range of half a standard deviation or more, at a cost of tens of dollars rather than thousands. It is a hope and not a result. The effect size in that row comes from studies of earlier tutoring systems, and whether current AI tutors reach it in real classrooms is what the next section examines.

    Worked Example: Why Bloom's Two Sigma May Overstate¶

    The two-sigma figure is an upper bound, and several features of the original studies push it up. Reading them side by side with a typical school trial shows why the pooled average is so much lower.

    Feature Bloom's small studies A typical real-school trial
    Duration A few weeks on one unit A semester or a year
    Group size A few dozen students per condition Hundreds or thousands
    What was tested The material that had been taught A broad state-aligned test
    Tutors Trained and closely managed Varied, with turnover
    Dosage Intensive, with mastery learning built in Often a few sessions a week
    Setting University lab schools and local classrooms Many different schools

    None of this makes the studies wrong. It means that a result obtained under tight control and a narrow test should not be expected to carry over unchanged. The practical conclusion is to plan for effects of a third to three-quarters of a standard deviation from well-run tutoring, and not for two.

    Worked Example: What Human Tutoring Costs by Group Size¶

    Tutoring cost depends mostly on how many students share a tutor. Suppose a tutor costs $40 an hour fully loaded and works with each student for 30 minutes, three times a week, for 30 weeks, or 45 hours.

    Format Students per tutor Tutor cost per student for 45 hours Cost for 1,000 students
    One-on-one 1 $1,800 $1,800,000
    Small group 3 $600 $600,000
    Larger group 6 $300 $300,000
    AI tutor (Chapter 7, Level 4) many about $23 about $23,000

    One-on-one tutoring for a thousand students costs about eighty times as much as the Level 4 figure. Small-group tutoring, which keeps much of the human contact at a third of the cost, is a sensible middle option for students who are furthest behind, which is why the book treats AI tutoring as the base for everyone and human tutoring as a targeted addition. These costs are illustrative, and a district should price its own.

    AI Tutors¶

    An AI tutor is a system, usually built on a large language model, that tutors a student through conversation: it asks questions, gives hints, explains in several ways, and adjusts to what the student says. It is a Level 4 feature in the five-level model, and it is the attempt to deliver something like the one-on-one tutoring of Bloom's experiments at the cost of software.

    What an AI tutor does well is real. It is available at any hour, never loses patience, can try many explanations, and can respond to a student's own words. What it does badly is also real: it can state wrong answers confidently (the hallucination of Chapter 5), it can slide into simply giving answers, and it lacks the human sense of when a student is discouraged. The evidence below, drawn from the book's AI-First Stories appendix, shows both sides.

    • A Harvard physics course ran a randomized study in which students learned from a carefully designed AI tutor and from an instructor-led active-learning class. The AI tutor group learned more, in less time. The tutor was built on proven teaching practices, not left as an open chatbot.
    • An after-school program in Edo State, Nigeria, used a GPT-4-based tutor to practice English for six weeks with teachers in the room, and the World Bank team reported large learning gains, around 0.3 standard deviations, at very small cost per student. By Chapter 3's benchmark for typical annual growth, that is about a year of ordinary progress in six weeks, though a result from a short pilot in one setting should be treated with care.
    • A study at a high school in Turkey gave students open access to GPT-4 while practicing math. They did much better on the practice problems, and on later exams taken without AI they did about 17 percent worse than students who had never had access. A second version of the tutor that gave hints instead of answers removed most of the harm.
    • Khanmigo, Khan Academy's AI tutor, is designed to guide students with questions, not answers. Reports describe early versions making math mistakes and students in some districts using it less than expected.

    The pattern across these cases is the book's thesis in miniature: how the AI is designed matters more than how powerful it is. The Turkish study used the same model in both conditions, and the difference between harm and help was whether it gave answers or hints. Chapter 12 develops the design principles.

    Worked Example: Judging the Evidence for a Compressed Morning¶

    Return to Mr. Dang's question and sort the evidence using the levels of Chapter 2. This is the exercise the sim below repeats.

    Evidence Design What it supports Limits
    Harvard physics AI tutor Randomized comparison AI tutoring can teach more in less time One course at one university; college students
    Nigeria after-school pilot Randomized pilot, six weeks Low-cost AI tutoring can help where resources are scarce Short; one setting; English practice
    Turkish high-school study Randomized field experiment Design decides help versus harm One school; short-term math
    Meta-analyses of mastery learning Many classroom studies pooled Mastery learning raises achievement by about half a standard deviation Mostly older, many short; quality varies
    Alpha School's reported growth School-reported Nothing yet that can be checked independently Self-reported; no outside evaluation known
    Unbound Academy's early results Board slides, no baseline Nothing about the effect of the program Reported proficiency below state averages; unknown starting points

    Notice what the table says. The strongest evidence, the randomized studies, supports the possibility that a well-designed AI tutor can teach more in less time. The evidence for the full package, two hours of software replacing a long academic morning, comes from a school whose results are self-reported and a second whose early results look weaker than the state average. The honest summary is that compression is plausible and unproven. A school should pilot it, measure it independently, and be prepared for it not to work.

    Diagram: Judging the Evidence for a Shorter Morning¶

    Judging the Evidence for a Shorter Morning

    Type: microsim sim-id: judging-evidence-for-shorter-morning
    Library: p5.js
    Status: Specified
    Bloom Level: Evaluate
    Bloom Verb: judge
    Learning Objective: The learner will judge the strength of the evidence behind eight claims about AI tutoring and the compressed morning by assigning each to one of the five evidence levels from Chapter 2, correctly placing at least seven of eight on the first attempt.

    Prerequisites: the five evidence levels (Strong, Moderate, Promising, Demonstrates a rationale, No evidence of effect), randomized controlled trial, self-reported results (all defined in Chapters 2 and 3), and the studies described in the section "AI Tutors" above.

    Evidence of Mastery: The learner places each of 8 claims in one of five evidence-level bins and submits it. A placement is correct when it matches the "Correct level" column in Content. Mastery is 7 of 8 correct on the first attempt. Reading the level definitions is exploration, not evidence.

    Misconceptions: (1) A famous or often-quoted result is strong evidence. (2) A school's own reported gains are evidence of effect. (3) A published meta-analysis is the same as a randomized trial.

    Instructional Rationale: Judging requires applying the same design criteria to real, unevenly supported claims about the very idea the book proposes. Including claims that support the book's thesis but have weak evidence prevents the learner from rating by agreement.

    Content:

    The five levels, as defined in Chapter 2: Strong (random assignment, well designed and carried out); Moderate (a comparison group, no random assignment); Promising (a correlational study with statistical controls); Demonstrates a rationale (a research-based reason, results not yet in); No evidence of effect (testimonials, surveys, self-reported or uncontrolled results).

    Eight claims:

    # Claim shown to the learner Correct level Why (shown as feedback)
    1 In an introductory Harvard physics course, students randomly assigned to a carefully designed AI tutor learned more, in less time, than in an instructor-led active-learning class. Strong Random assignment and a direct comparison, though in a single course.
    2 In a six-week Nigerian after-school pilot, randomly assigned students who used a GPT-4 tutor with teachers present showed large gains in English. Strong Random assignment and a comparison group, though short and in one setting.
    3 In a Turkish high-school field experiment with random assignment, open GPT-4 access improved practice scores but lowered later exam scores by about 17 percent, while a hint-first version removed most of the harm. Strong Random assignment; the finding concerns design, not just power.
    4 A pooled analysis of many classroom studies found that mastery learning raised exam scores by about half a standard deviation. Moderate It combines many studies with comparison groups, but most were not randomized and quality varies.
    5 A school reports that its students gain more than double the expected growth after two hours a day on adaptive software. No evidence of effect The result is self-reported and has not been independently evaluated.
    6 A new online school's early reports show 10 percent proficiency in math, with no information about where students started. No evidence of effect Without a starting point or a comparison, the result says nothing about the program's effect.
    7 A district reports that students in some schools used an AI tutor less than expected and that early versions made math errors. No evidence of effect These are implementation reports, with no comparison group measuring learning.
    8 A new AI tutor is built on research about hint-first questioning and spaced practice, and an independent evaluation is planned. Demonstrates a rationale It has a research-based reason to expect benefit, with results still to come.

    Provenance: The studies are those described in the chapter section "AI Tutors" and in the book's AI-First Stories appendix, which notes that several accounts still need source checking. The sim must label the claims "Summaries; check the sources before citing." Level definitions follow Chapter 2.

    Rules: Each claim has exactly one correct level, given in Content. A claim is scored on its first submitted placement only. The score counts first-attempt correct placements out of 8. Mastery is a score >= 7.

    Learner Activity:

    1. The learner reads one claim at a time and sees the five levels.
    2. The learner places the claim in a level and submits it. The sim marks it correct or incorrect and shows the feedback text.
    3. If incorrect, the learner may place it again; the second attempt does not change the score. After the second attempt, the correct level and reason are shown.
    4. After all 8 claims, the sim shows the score and the misplaced claims with their correct levels.
    5. The learner should notice that the three strongest claims are all about tutoring design, and that none of the claims about whole-school results reaches the Strong level.

    Feedback: 8 claims, shuffled once per session, two attempts each. Correct: "Correct: [level]. [Why text]." Incorrect: "Not quite. [Why text for the correct answer, without naming the level]." After the second attempt the correct level is revealed and the claim counts as missed. A running score "First-try correct: n of 8" is shown. Final message for a score >= 7: "You can weigh the evidence on both sides." For a score < 7: "Review the five levels and try again; the claims will be reshuffled."

    Starting State: Claim 1 of the shuffled order is shown with five empty levels, the score "First-try correct: 0 of 8," and the prompt "How strong is the evidence behind this claim?"

    Chapter Anchors: The chapter reports a 17 percent drop on later exams in the Turkish study, a Harvard randomized physics study, a six-week Nigerian pilot, and an effect of about half a standard deviation for mastery learning. The chapter states that Alpha School's results are self-reported and Unbound Academy's early results lack a baseline. The mastery bar is 7 of 8.

    Test the tutor's first reply

    When evaluating any AI tutor, ask it a homework question and read only its first reply. If it hands over the answer, the design fails the Turkish-study test, whatever the brochure says. A good first reply asks the student something.

    Cognitive Offloading¶

    Cognitive offloading is the use of an external tool or resource to reduce the mental effort a task demands: writing a phone number down instead of remembering it, or using a calculator for arithmetic. Offloading is ordinary and often wise. It becomes a problem in learning when the thing offloaded is the very thing the student is supposed to be learning, since the effort is the learning (the productive struggle of Chapter 2).

    The research on AI and offloading is early. The MIT Media Lab study summarized in the appendix, a small preprint, found that students writing essays with ChatGPT showed lower brain engagement and recalled less of their own writing than students who wrote unaided. Survey studies have linked heavy AI use with lower critical thinking scores, though correlation cannot show which way the effect runs. The Turkish study is stronger evidence, because it was randomized: students with open access learned less. A working rule is to offload the routine and keep the target: let a tool do the arithmetic once the concept of division is mastered, but not before, and let AI check a draft after the student has written it, not write it.

    Worked Example: What to Offload¶

    Skill being learned Tool use that offloads the target (avoid) Tool use that offloads the routine (fine)
    Solving unit-rate problems Asking the chatbot to solve each problem Using a calculator for the division after choosing which numbers to divide
    Writing a paragraph argument Having AI draft the paragraph Having AI point out unclear sentences in the student's own draft
    Understanding a historical cause Asking AI "what caused the war?" and copying Asking AI for sources to evaluate, then judging them
    Debugging a program Pasting the error and accepting the fix Asking for a hint about where to look

    The calculator lesson doesn't transfer automatically

    A common trap is reasoning "we let students use calculators, so we can let them use chatbots." A calculator does arithmetic and leaves the thinking; a chatbot can do the thinking too. Before allowing a tool, ask what part of the task it does and whether that part is the skill being taught.

    The Compressed Academic Morning¶

    Academic morning is the part of the school day devoted to the core academic subjects, usually reading, writing, mathematics, science, and social studies. In a traditional school these subjects are spread through the whole day. The compressed academic morning is the proposal that, with adaptive learning, students could reach today's academic results in about two to three hours a day, concentrated in the morning, which frees the rest of the day. Alpha School, as the AI-First Stories appendix reports, runs about two hours of adaptive academic software a day. Whether the compression works is the question the evidence above leaves open.

    Two mechanisms can make it work. Reduced direct instruction is the shift from long, whole-class, teacher-led explanation (the direct instruction and lecture of Chapters 1 and 2) to explanation that is short, interactive, and paced by the student, with the teacher moving from lecturer to coach. And the removal of waiting: the idle hours of fixed-time classes, the time students spend on material they already know, and the transitions between periods.

    Freed-up school time is the time the compression releases, available for other uses. It is the reason compression matters at all: not that students spend less time learning, but that the time can be spent on things that AI cannot do and that students need, the subject of Part 3.

    Worked Example: How Much Time Is Freed?¶

    Use the illustrative school day of Chapter 2. Its five academic periods total 250 minutes a day: each period had 5 minutes of settling and attendance, 20 minutes of whole-class direct instruction, 20 minutes of guided and independent practice, and 5 minutes of wrap-up. In a compressed morning, suppose settling and wrap-up fall to 10 minutes in all, explanation becomes 50 minutes of short interactive segments paced by the student, and practice with immediate feedback takes 90 minutes. These values are an illustrative scenario, not data.

    Part of the academic morning Traditional (minutes) Compressed (minutes)
    Settling, attendance, wrap-up 50 10
    Explanation 100 50
    Practice 100 90
    Total 250 150

    The compressed morning is 150 minutes, 2.5 hours, and frees \( 250 - 150 = 100 \) minutes a day. Over a 180-day year that is \( 100 \times 180 = 18{,}000 \) minutes, or 300 hours, about 46 school days of 6.5 hours. This is a substantial amount of time, enough for a half-day of afternoon activity every day.

    The scenario also shows what must be true. Students would need to cover in 150 minutes what the traditional morning covers in 250, which means learning about \( 250 / 150 \approx 1.67 \) times as much per minute. The compression works only if the adaptive morning delivers that gain, and that is what the evidence, so far, does not show. If the morning must be 190 minutes (explanation 70 and practice 110), the freed time falls to 60 minutes a day, or about 28 school days a year, still worth having but less transformative.

    Worked Example: Cutting a Twenty-Minute Lecture Into Pieces¶

    Reduced direct instruction does not mean no explanation. It means explaining in short pieces, with the student doing something between them. Research on online video found that short segments hold attention best: in an analysis of 6.9 million viewing sessions on edX courses (Guo, Kim, and Rubin, 2014), shorter videos were much more engaging, and the median time students spent on a video was at most about six minutes however long the video was. The authors advised segmenting videos into chunks shorter than six minutes. The study looked at four university-level online courses, so treat six minutes as a useful guide for chunking and not a law. Here is one 20-minute lecture on unit rates, rebuilt.

    Minute Traditional lecture Rebuilt as a student-paced sequence
    0–5 Teacher explains unit rates A 5-minute interactive explanation with a slider the student moves
    5–6 (continues) The student answers a self-check question and sees feedback
    6–11 (continues) A 5-minute second piece: unit price from a table
    11–12 (continues) A self-check; the dashboard notes who missed it
    12–17 (continues) A 5-minute third piece with a real example
    17–20 Practice problems, for those who followed Students needing help meet the teacher; the rest move to practice

    The explanation has not disappeared, and it has been cut into three pieces with checks. A student who understood the first piece moves on in five minutes instead of waiting twenty. One who did not meets the teacher sooner and is not carried along through two more pieces built on the first.

    Worked Example: Where Might 300 Freed Hours Go?¶

    The compressed morning could free about 300 hours a year, but the hours only help if they are planned. An illustrative allocation:

    Use Hours a year Share
    Project block with public exhibitions 120 40%
    Arts and ensembles 60 20%
    Sports and clubs 60 20%
    Life-skills workshops and service learning 40 13%
    Advisory and reflection 20 7%
    Total 300 100%

    The largest share goes to the project block, because the evidence in Chapters 13 and 14 favors extended, team-based work. A school can change the split, but it should write the allocation down, since time that is not assigned drifts, which is the risk Chapter 14 names.

    Worked Example: Does the Afternoon Count as Instructional Time?¶

    State rules require a minimum amount of instructional time, and a redesigned day has to meet them. The numbers are illustrative, and each state's definition of instructional time differs, so a district should check its own and ask about waivers. Suppose a state requires 990 hours a year and the school provides 6 instructional hours a day for 180 days.

    Hours
    Instructional time available: 6 hours × 180 days 1,080
    Compressed morning: 150 minutes × 180 days 450
    Remaining time in the day 630
    Needed from the afternoon to reach 990 540
    Share of the afternoon that must count as instruction 540 ÷ 630, about 86%

    If the state counts only teacher-led academic instruction, most of a project-and-arts afternoon may not qualify, and the school would fall short. If the state counts any supervised, standards-aligned learning, it may. This is a real design constraint, and it is one reason Chapter 21 lists policy embedding, including waivers, among the steps of making the model last.

    Critical Thinking and Computational Thinking¶

    The reason to free time is not to stop learning academic content. It is to spend the time on abilities that AI cannot replace and that students need to practice with other people. Two of them, critical and computational thinking, are things AI can teach well, as the course description says, and students still have to practice.

    Critical thinking is the disciplined ability to analyze information, evaluate claims and arguments, and decide what to believe or do on the basis of reasons and evidence. It is the higher-order thinking of Chapter 1, the Analyze and Evaluate levels of Bloom's taxonomy, made into a habit. It is the skill this book has used throughout: asking "compared with whom, chosen how, measured by whom?" is critical thinking applied to evidence.

    Two parts of critical thinking are worth naming. Evidence evaluation is judging how much weight a piece of evidence deserves, by asking about its source, its method, its sample, and who benefits. Argument analysis is the breaking of an argument into its parts: the conclusion it wants you to accept and the premises offered as reasons, and then judging whether the premises are true and whether they actually support the conclusion. A vendor's claim, "94 percent of teachers love it, so it improves learning," is an argument whose conclusion does not follow from its premise.

    Worked Example: Analyzing an Argument¶

    Take a statement from a brochure: "Our students spend only two hours a day on software and score above the state average. Therefore the software, not the teachers, produces the results."

    Part Text Question to ask
    Premise 1 Students spend two hours a day on software Is it true? How is it measured?
    Premise 2 They score above the state average Which test, and compared with whom? Did they start higher?
    Conclusion The software, not the teachers, produces the results Does it follow from the premises?

    The argument skips a step. Even if both premises are true, students might score high because of who enrolls, what the adults do in the rest of the day, or families' resources. The conclusion requires evidence that rules those out, which is why the randomized designs of Chapter 2 matter. A student who can lay out an argument this way is practicing critical thinking, and this is also exactly what the pro-social afternoon needs to exercise, in debate and in joint projects.

    Computational thinking is the set of thinking habits used to formulate problems so that a computer, or a person following precise steps, can solve them. It is usually described with four parts: decomposition, breaking a problem into smaller pieces; pattern recognition, noticing what repeats; abstraction, ignoring detail that does not matter; and algorithm design, writing precise steps to solve the problem. Jeannette Wing's 2006 essay popularized the term and argued that it is a basic skill for everyone, and not only for programmers.

    Worked Example: Computational Thinking in Maya's Textbook¶

    The ready-concepts rule of Chapters 8 and 9 is computational thinking in action. The problem, "what should Maya study next?", is decomposed into one question per concept: is it mastered, and are its prerequisites mastered? The pattern is that every concept is checked the same way. The abstraction ignores everything about a concept except its name, its status, and its prerequisites. The algorithm is the rule itself: for each unmastered concept, mark it ready if all its direct prerequisites are mastered. A student who can run, and then write, a rule like this has learned something that applies to scheduling a tournament, sorting a list, or planning a project.

    Worked Example: Taking a Claim Apart¶

    Critical thinking is a habit of taking a claim apart. Apply it to a sentence from a brochure: "Students who use our tutor score 20 percent higher, so our tutor works."

    Part Text Question to ask
    Premise Students who use the tutor score 20 percent higher Higher than whom? On what test? By how many points?
    Hidden assumption The tutor caused the higher scores Did motivated students choose the tutor? Did they start higher?
    Conclusion The tutor works Does a difference between two groups show a cause?
    Evidence needed Random assignment or a matched comparison with baselines Is there a randomized or independent study?

    The argument moves from a correlation to a cause. A student who can write this table has practiced evidence evaluation and argument analysis on a real example, and it is the same table Chapter 2 used to read the MathPath brochure.

    Worked Example: Debugging an Algorithm¶

    Computational thinking includes testing a rule on an example and fixing it when it fails. Here is a first version of the ready-concepts rule, written in plain steps: "For each concept, if all its prerequisites are mastered, it is ready." Test it on a small graph: concept A has no prerequisites, concept B depends on A, and the student has mastered A.

    Step What the rule does Result
    Check A A has no prerequisites, so all of them (none) are mastered A is "ready"
    Check B B's prerequisite A is mastered B is "ready"
    Output A and B A is wrong: the student already mastered it

    The test exposed a bug: the rule never asks whether the concept is already mastered. The fix adds a condition: "if it is not yet mastered and all its prerequisites are mastered, it is ready." Running the same example gives only B, which is right. This is the habit of Chapter 12's algorithmic thinking: write the steps, run them on a small case, look for a surprising answer, and repair the rule. It is also the reason a transparent rule is easier to trust than an opaque model: you can run it on a small case yourself.

    Thinking skills are learned by practice

    If critical and computational thinking sound like talents you either have or don't, they're not; they're habits built by doing. You already practiced both in this chapter, laying out an argument and running a rule. Try one on a real brochure this week.

    Practice: Reading a Tutor's Reply¶

    The sim below ties the thread of this chapter together. It asks you to read an AI tutor's reply and say how much of the solution it gives away, using the hint ladder from Chapter 2, because that is the property that decided help from harm in the Turkish study. The hint ladder had four levels after the student works alone: Level 1 asks what the problem is asking; Level 2 narrows the plan; Level 3 gives the step; Level 4 gives the answer.

    Answering Mr. Dang¶

    Mr. Dang asked for proof. The honest answer has three layers. What is solid: mastery learning and adaptive practice improve achievement, with effects of about half a standard deviation or less in real settings; one-on-one tutoring is among the most effective interventions known; and a well-designed AI tutor, giving hints instead of answers, can teach well in careful studies. What is not yet shown: that two hours of software can replace a traditional academic morning for most students at scale; the one school with a long track record reports its own results, and an online version has produced early results below the state average. What decides the difference: design, with hint-first tutors, mastery checks, human adults in the room, and the rest of the day spent with other people.

    The story has both sides. The benefit: a morning that fills each student's gaps and could free around 300 hours a year, many weeks of school time. The risks: a compression that does not deliver and shortchanges students, tutors that give answers and erode learning, and screen time that grows. The response: pilot the compression, measure it independently, keep a person in the loop, and keep the morning short and the afternoon social. The next chapters supply the rest: guardrails in Chapter 12, and the afternoon in Chapters 13 and 14.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Adaptive learning adjusts content to each student's data; mastery learning fixes achievement and lets time vary, with effects of about half a standard deviation in pooled studies. Fixed mastery saved only about eight percent of total hours in our example, so mastery alone does not compress the day.
    • Personalized pacing and gap filling address the one-pace problem: in a class of 28, a single lesson reaches 13.
    • One-on-one tutoring is highly effective; Bloom's two-sigma problem (two standard deviations in small studies) is the upper end, pooled randomized trials average about 0.37, and cost keeps human tutoring out of reach for most students.
    • An AI tutor can approach tutoring at the cost of software, but design decides the outcome: in the Turkish study, answer-giving harmed later exams by about 17 percent and hint-first removed most of the harm.
    • Cognitive offloading is fine for the routine and harmful for the target skill; offload the routine and keep the target.
    • The compressed academic morning and reduced direct instruction could free freed-up school time: in our illustration 100 minutes a day, 300 hours a year, but it requires learning about 1.67 times as much per minute, which the evidence has not yet shown.
    • Critical thinking, including evidence evaluation and argument analysis, and computational thinking, are what the freed time is for, and AI can teach them while students still need to practice them with other people.
    • The strongest evidence supports well-designed AI tutoring; the evidence for the full compressed-morning package is self-reported or early, so pilot it and measure it independently.

    You can weigh the case for a shorter morning

    You just weighed the evidence on mastery learning, tutoring, and AI tutors, and worked out how many hours a compressed morning could free and what it must deliver. Chapter 12 turns to the guardrails that keep an AI tutor helping. One step at a time, together.
