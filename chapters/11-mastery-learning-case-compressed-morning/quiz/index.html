    Quiz: Mastery Learning and the Case for a Compressed Morning¶

    Test your understanding of mastery learning, tutoring evidence, AI tutors, cognitive offloading, and what a compressed academic morning would require with these review questions.

    1. What is the defining principle of mastery learning?¶

    1. Students move on when the class calendar says the unit is finished
    2. Students are grouped by age so the whole class reaches mastery together
    3. Students earn credit for each course by attending a fixed number of hours
    4. Achievement is held fixed and time is allowed to vary from student to student
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. In mastery learning, students must demonstrate mastery of a concept, typically 80 to 90 percent on a check, before moving on, and they get extra time and different instruction when they do not. Building on John Carroll's model, Bloom's approach fixes achievement and lets time vary, the reverse of the seat-time model. The other three options describe features of fixed-time, industrial-era schooling, where time is fixed and achievement varies.

    Concept Tested: Mastery Learning

    See: Adaptive Learning and Mastery Learning

    2. Bloom reported that one-on-one tutoring with mastery learning produced about two standard deviations of improvement. What did a 2020 review of randomized tutoring trials in real schools find?¶

    1. A much smaller average effect, about 0.37 standard deviations
    2. An average effect of about two standard deviations, confirming Bloom
    3. No measurable effect from tutoring in real school settings
    4. An average effect of about four standard deviations for online tutors
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The review by Nickow, Oreopoulos, and Quan found an average effect of about 0.37 standard deviations, and a later published version reported about 0.29. Bloom's studies were small, short, and tested the material taught, so two sigma is the upper end of what careful small studies have seen. The chapter still calls tutoring among the most effective interventions known, so "no measurable effect" is wrong, and it suggests planning for a third to three-quarters of a standard deviation.

    See: Bloom's Two-Sigma Problem and Tutoring

    3. In the Turkish high school study, students with open GPT-4 access did better on practice but about 17 percent worse on later exams, while a hint-giving version removed most of the harm. What lesson does the chapter draw?¶

    1. AI tutors should be banned from high school math classes
    2. More powerful models produce better long-term learning
    3. How the AI is designed matters more than how powerful it is
    4. Practice scores are the best measure of what students learned
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The study used the same model in both conditions; the difference between harm and help was whether it gave answers or hints. The chapter calls this the book's thesis in miniature. A ban ignores the hint-first version that worked, and model power was held constant. The gap between strong practice scores and weak exam scores shows practice scores can mislead, echoing Chapter 1's warning that performance is not the same as learning.

    Concept Tested: AI Tutor

    See: AI Tutors

    4. A school's traditional academic periods total 240 minutes a day. A redesigned compressed morning takes 160 minutes. Over a 180-day year, how much time is freed?¶

    1. About 80 hours
    2. About 240 hours
    3. About 480 hours
    4. About 720 hours
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The compressed morning frees 240 - 160 = 80 minutes a day. Over 180 days that is 80 x 180 = 14,400 minutes, or 240 hours. The 80-hour answer confuses minutes per day with hours per year, and the 480-hour answer counts the freed time twice. The chapter adds a caution: freeing this time requires students to learn about 1.5 times as much per minute in the shorter morning, which the evidence has not yet shown.

    5. In the chapter's fixed-mastery example, every student reached the goal, but total hours fell only about eight percent. What does the chapter conclude a compressed morning needs beyond mastery learning?¶

    1. Longer school days, so that slower students have enough time to finish
    2. Larger classes, so that each teacher can serve more students at once
    3. More summative tests, so that progress can be reported to the state
    4. Less time on whole-class explanation and waiting, not just mastery checks
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Mastery learning is mainly a way of getting everyone to the goal, not of making the course much shorter. The case for compression needs the time students spend on explanation and waiting to shrink: reduced direct instruction, with short student-paced explanations and the teacher as coach, plus removal of idle time, transitions, and work on material students already know. Longer days work against the goal of freeing time, and the chapter does not propose larger classes or more summative tests.

    Concept Tested: Reduced Direct Instruction

    See: The Compressed Academic Morning

    6. An adaptive session starts at level 3. Two correct answers in a row at the same level move the student up one level; one wrong answer moves the student down one. A student answers: correct, correct, correct, wrong, correct. At what level is the sixth question?¶

    1. Level 2
    2. Level 3
    3. Level 4
    4. Level 5
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The first two correct answers at level 3 move the student to level 4. The third answer, correct at level 4, is one in a row, so the student stays at 4. The fourth answer is wrong, so the student drops to level 3. The fifth answer, correct at level 3, is again one in a row, so the student stays at level 3 for the sixth question. The rule uses no language model, only a counter, and the sequence of levels is itself data.

    Concept Tested: Adaptive Learning

    See: Worked Example: An Adaptive Session, Item by Item

    7. A teacher is learning to write paragraph arguments with her class. Which use of AI offloads the routine and keeps the target skill?¶

    1. Having AI point out unclear sentences in each student's own draft
    2. Having AI draft each paragraph for students to revise
    3. Asking AI to write a model answer that students copy into their notes
    4. Asking AI to summarize the argument so students skip the reading
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter's working rule is to offload the routine and keep the target. When the target skill is writing an argument, AI drafting the paragraph or providing an answer to copy offloads the very thing being learned. Pointing out unclear sentences in a draft the student wrote leaves the thinking with the student. The chapter warns that a chatbot, unlike a calculator, can do the thinking as well as the routine, so each use must be checked against the skill being taught.

    8. A brochure says: "Our students spend only two hours a day on software and score above the state average. Therefore the software produces the results." What is the main flaw a critical thinker should identify?¶

    1. The premises must be false, since no software runs for only two hours a day
    2. The argument is sound, because both of its premises are measurable facts
    3. The conclusion does not follow; enrollment or the rest of the day could explain the scores
    4. The argument is flawed only because it compares students to a state average
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Argument analysis separates premises from the conclusion and asks whether the premises support it. Even if both premises are true, students might score high because of who enrolls, what adults do in the rest of the day, or families' resources. Ruling those out requires evidence such as a randomized comparison. Measurable premises do not make an argument sound, and the chapter does not claim the premises are false. Comparing to the state average is a question to ask, not the core flaw.

    Concept Tested: Argument Analysis

    See: Worked Example: Analyzing an Argument

    9. Why does the chapter say personalized pacing alone does not solve the problem of students who are furthest behind?¶

    1. Because some students need more time than the school year contains
    2. Because pacing software forces slower students to keep up with the class average
    3. Because pacing only works for students who are already ahead of their grade level
    4. Because pacing removes the teacher, leaving slower students with no human support
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Pacing lets fast students move on and slow students keep working, but it does not make the year longer. In the chapter's example, the slowest student would need 312 days at 50 minutes a day to finish a 180-day course. The design must protect extra time and coaching for students who are behind, accept that some finish the following year, or both. The chapter says a model that advertises pacing without such a plan has moved the problem, not solved it.

    10. A state requires 1,000 instructional hours a year. A school offers 6 hours a day for 180 days and plans a 2-hour compressed academic morning. What does this mean for the school's afternoon program?¶

    1. The afternoon does not matter, since the 1,080-hour day already exceeds the requirement
    2. The morning alone meets the requirement, since academic time counts double
    3. The school must lengthen the morning to six hours to meet the rule
    4. Most of the afternoon, about 89 percent, must count as instruction under state rules
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The morning provides 2 x 180 = 360 hours. The rest of the day provides 1,080 - 360 = 720 hours, and 1,000 - 360 = 640 must come from it, about 89 percent. If the state counts only teacher-led academic instruction, a project-and-arts afternoon may not qualify and the school would fall short; if it counts supervised, standards-aligned learning, it may. The chapter calls this a real design constraint and points to policy steps such as waivers.
