    Quiz: Leading Change: Vision, Culture, and Early-Stage Concerns¶

    Test your understanding of the early stages of leading change, from urgency and vision to losses, culture, and teachers' first concerns, with these review questions.

    1. In Kotter's eight steps, which step comes first?¶

    1. Form a guiding coalition
    2. Create a change vision
    3. Generate short-term wins
    4. Establish a sense of urgency
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Kotter's sequence begins with a sense of urgency, the shared belief that change is needed now and that staying the same is riskier. The steps then run through guiding coalition, change vision, communicating the vision, removing obstacles, short-term wins, building on change, and anchoring in culture. Kotter found organizations often skipped early steps like urgency in their hurry, and the skipped steps came back to cause failure. A vision announced without urgency is ignored.

    Concept Tested: Sense of Urgency

    See: Kotter: From Urgency to Anchoring

    2. In the Concerns-Based Adoption Model, which stages make up the "self concerns"?¶

    1. Management, consequence, and collaboration
    2. Awareness, informational, and personal
    3. Consequence, collaboration, and refocusing
    4. Informational, management, and consequence
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Self concerns are the first three of the seven stages of concern: awareness (0), informational (1), and personal (2). In these stages, the person's concern is about themselves in relation to the innovation, such as "What is it?" and "How will this affect me?" Management (3) is about the task, and consequence, collaboration, and refocusing (4 to 6) are about impact on students and colleagues. The chapter stresses that self concerns are not resistance and need answers, not persuasion.

    See: CBAM: Concerns at the Start

    3. A school's force field analysis scores the driving forces at a total of 13. Its restraining forces are students without devices (5), fear of job loss (4), privacy concerns (3), and closed-door culture (2). The school lends devices, cutting that force to 2, and completes a privacy review, cutting that force to 1. What is the net balance now?¶

    1. About +4 in favor of change
    2. About -1 against change
    3. About +9 in favor of change
    4. About 0, still evenly balanced
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The restraining forces start at 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 14, giving a net of 13 - 14 = -1 against change. After the changes they are 2 + 4 + 1 + 2 = 9, so the net is 13 - 9 = +4 in favor of change. The -1 answer is the starting balance, and +9 confuses the restraining total with the net. Lewin's point is that reducing restraining forces tips the balance without adding pressure on teachers.

    See: Worked Example: A Force Field for the Compressed Morning

    4. What is Lewin's most useful practical insight from force field analysis?¶

    1. Driving forces always outweigh restraining forces once a leader fully commits
    2. Restraining forces disappear on their own after the refreeze stage
    3. Pushing on driving forces raises resistance; reducing restraining forces works better
    4. A change effort should begin only when the driving forces total twice the restraining forces
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Force field analysis pictures a situation as a balance between driving forces toward change and restraining forces against it. Lewin's insight is that pushing harder on the driving forces tends to raise the restraining forces as people dig in, while reducing restraining forces lets change happen with less tension. The chapter echoes this under Kotter's step of removing obstacles: removing a barrier is more effective than pushing people over it. The other options are not claims Lewin made.

    Concept Tested: Unfreeze Stage

    See: Lewin: Unfreeze, Change, Refreeze

    5. A teacher says, "I understand the data and why we're doing this, but my job is to lecture, and I don't want to change." What is the best first move according to ADKAR?¶

    1. A listening conversation about what the teacher fears and values
    2. A short, direct explanation of the data behind the change
    3. A 20-minute demonstration of the dashboard and a one-page guide
    4. A formal evaluation of the teacher's lecture-based instruction
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The teacher has awareness of why the change is needed but lacks desire, the hardest element to create because it cannot be ordered. Desire grows from the person's own motives, trust in leadership, values, and a sense of being heard, so the first move is a listening conversation. Explaining the data again targets awareness, which is already present. A demonstration targets knowledge, which the chapter says is useless without awareness and desire.

    See: Worked Example: Reading Three Teachers

    6. According to Bridges, why are leaders who talk only about an exciting new beginning often puzzled when staff do not respond?¶

    1. Because staff have not yet received enough technical training
    2. Because the new beginning is usually announced too late in the school year
    3. Because staff respond only to urgency, not to positive messages
    4. Because people cannot begin anew until they have an ending for what they lose
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Bridges distinguishes the external change from the internal transition, which begins with endings and losses. Every change, even a welcome one, involves loss, and the losses are often not obvious: identity as an expert, hard-won competence, routines, relationships, control, and meaning. Leaders who skip the ending are puzzled by the lack of response. In the chapter's loss inventory, Ms. Okafor acknowledges each loss and builds what teachers most want to keep into their new role.

    Concept Tested: Endings and Losses

    See: Bridges: Endings and Losses

    7. At a staff meeting, a teacher asks, "Will I be evaluated on this? What if I can't do it?" Which stage of concern is this, and how should a leader respond?¶

    1. Stage 4, consequence; explain the expected effect on student learning
    2. Stage 2, personal; answer the question about the teacher directly and honestly
    3. Stage 0, awareness; send the teacher information about the plan
    4. Stage 5, collaboration; pair the teacher with a colleague to coordinate
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The teacher is asking how the change will affect them, which is stage 2, personal, one of the self concerns. The chapter warns that a person at stage 2 told "it will be great for the students" has not been answered, since the question was about themselves. The chapter's tip is to answer the question about them first, directly and honestly; people can hear about students only after their own concern has been heard. Responding with student impact addresses stage 4.

    See: CBAM: Concerns at the Start

    8. What is the difference between a vision that is announced and a shared vision, in Senge's terms?¶

    1. An announced vision is long and detailed; a shared vision fits on one line
    2. An announced vision comes from the board; a shared vision comes from the principal
    3. An announced vision yields compliance; a shared vision, built together, yields commitment
    4. An announced vision is written down; a shared vision is never put in writing
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A shared vision is a picture of the future that people throughout the organization truly hold, built through conversation rather than handed down. Compliance produces the minimum and fades when the leader leaves; commitment produces initiative. In the chapter's example, the principal who built the vision over six weeks with teachers, students, and parents got proposed improvements, while the one who emailed it got minimum use. Length and who writes it are not the distinction.

    See: Worked Example: Compliance or Commitment?

    9. A school's mission statement says it is a "collaborative learning community," but new teachers are told, "Close your door and do your own thing." A principal plans collaborative afternoon teams. Using Schein's model, what should she expect, and what fits as a response?¶

    1. The mission statement will carry the plan, so she can mandate teams right away
    2. Teachers will welcome teams, since the school already values collaboration
    3. Resistance will come only from new teachers, so she should focus on them
    4. The hidden assumption will win, so she should open doors through low-risk peer visits
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Schein's model separates artifacts, espoused values, and basic assumptions. The mission is an espoused value; the advice to new teachers reveals the basic assumption that autonomy outranks collaboration. When stated values and assumptions disagree, the assumptions win, so the plan will meet resistance at that point. The chapter's response is to open doors through low-risk peer visits rather than mandates. The first two options mistake the stated value for the real culture.

    See: Worked Example: Reading a School's Culture Quickly

    10. Using the chapter's innovation configuration map, a coach observes that a teacher lets students move at their own pace with little teacher contact but never opens the teacher dashboard. What should the coach conclude?¶

    1. Acceptable on pacing, unacceptable on the dashboard, so help should target the dashboard
    2. The teacher is fully using the innovation, since students work at their own pace
    3. The teacher is not using the innovation at all, so the coach should start over with training
    4. The teacher is ideal on every component, since the students appear engaged
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. An innovation configuration map lists an innovation's essential components and, for each, the range from ideal to unacceptable. In the chapter's map, self-pacing with little teacher contact is acceptable, while a dashboard that is never opened is unacceptable. "Using it" is no longer a yes or no, and the map tells the coach exactly where to help. The second and third options treat use as all-or-nothing, which is the problem the map was designed to solve.
