    Content Economics and Open Educational Resources¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers the economics behind falling content-creation costs: open educational resources, open licenses, the OpenBook QR Score, content commoditization, and how publisher business models are changing as AI makes level 1 and level 2 content cheap to produce. Readers finish able to use the OpenBook QR Score to compare two open textbooks and explain why AI is commoditizing basic textbook content.

    "What Will It Cost Per Student?"¶

    At the district's budget meeting, the finance director, Mr. Patel, asks the question every board member eventually asks: "Fine, it's a good idea. What will it cost per student, per year, all in?" Behind him sits a binder with a $900,000 proposal from a publisher for a district-wide digital math program, and beside it a librarian's printout of a free open textbook that covers the same grades.

    The two items on the table are not obviously comparable. One is a product with a price. The other is free, and nobody can say what "free" leaves out. To answer Mr. Patel, a school leader needs to understand three things: why content that once cost a publisher a great deal to produce is becoming cheap, what makes a free textbook good and reusable, and where the money goes at each of the five levels of Chapter 6.

    The answer is more surprising than "it's all free." Content is becoming nearly free, but devices, teacher training, and data protection are not, and the biggest costs in a digital classroom are often the ones that never appear on a software invoice.

    Follow the money, not the hype

    This chapter lets you answer a finance director with numbers: what content costs to make, what a free textbook really offers, and what each level of textbook costs per student. You'll also learn to score a free textbook for quality and reusability in a few minutes. Let's find the balance between the savings that are real and the costs that hide.

    Why Textbook Content Costs What It Does¶

    A textbook publisher is a company that commissions, edits, produces, markets, and distributes textbooks and related materials. The large publishers serving U.S. schools and colleges, such as Pearson, McGraw Hill, Cengage, and Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, grew over many decades by solving a hard problem: assembling expert authors, editors, designers, reviewers, and a sales force to put a reliable book in every classroom.

    Content creation cost is the total cost of producing a textbook's content before a single copy is sold: author and subject-expert time, editing, fact-checking and review, design and illustration, simulations and assessments, accessibility work, and production. For a traditional textbook this cost is large and mostly up-front, which is why publishers needed to sell many copies at a high price to recover it. After the book is finished, producing one more copy costs almost nothing in digital form, so the economics are those of fixed cost: the first copy is expensive, and every later copy is nearly free.

    A publisher business model is the way a publisher earns money from that content. The classic model is to sell a new edition every few years at a high price, rely on the fact that the person who chooses the book (a professor or a district) is not the person who pays (a student or a taxpayer), and sell updated editions so that used copies lose value. Later models added digital subscriptions, bundled "inclusive access" fees charged with tuition, and adaptive homework platforms sold alongside the book.

    Textbook price is what the buyer pays. The College Board estimates that the average undergraduate's annual budget for books and supplies is about $1,300, a figure that has made textbook cost a long-running political issue in higher education. In K-12, districts pay, usually through adoption cycles of five to seven years, and the cost is part of the per-pupil spending of Chapter 1.

    Worked Example: Anatomy of a Textbook's Cost¶

    The table gives an illustrative breakdown for one chapter of a traditional textbook with its simulations and assessments, using round numbers chosen to show where the effort goes. The hours and hourly cost are assumptions, not industry data.

    Task Hours of skilled work per chapter Cost at $50 per hour
    Drafting the text 30 $1,500
    Designing and building the simulations 25 $1,250
    Writing practice questions and assessments 10 $500
    Expert review and fact-checking 8 $400
    Editing, accessibility, and production 7 $350
    Total 80 $4,000

    For a 20-chapter book the content cost in this illustration is about $80,000, before marketing and sales. Notice that the largest items, drafting and building simulations, are exactly the tasks that AI is getting better at, which leads to the next section.

    AI and Falling Production Costs¶

    AI-generated content is text, images, simulations, quizzes, and other learning materials produced mainly by an AI system, typically a large language model (Chapter 4), from a prompt or an outline, and then reviewed and edited by a person. The people review it because of the hallucination problem of Chapter 5.

    Falling production cost is the decline in the cost of producing content as AI takes over parts of the work. It follows from the two trends of earlier chapters: the task horizon is growing, so AI can complete longer jobs such as a whole chapter, and the price per token is falling, so each job costs less to run. The human role shifts from writing every sentence to specifying, reviewing, and correcting.

    Worked Example: The Same Chapter, AI-Assisted¶

    Take the 80-hour chapter above and suppose AI generates the first draft of the text, the practice questions, and the first version of each simulation, while a skilled person specifies, reviews, tests, and fixes.

    Task Hours of skilled work, AI-assisted Cost at $50 per hour
    Specifying the chapter and prompts 3 $150
    Reviewing and editing the draft 6 $300
    Testing and fixing the simulations 5 $250
    Expert review and fact-checking 5 $250
    Accessibility check and production 3 $150
    Total skilled work 22 $1,100
    AI computing for generation n/a about $20

    The illustrative cost falls from about $4,000 to about $1,120 per chapter, a fall of about 72 percent, and for a 20-chapter book from about $80,000 to about $22,400. Two cautions apply. First, the saving depends on the review step: skipping it moves the cost into errors that students learn. Second, the assumptions are illustrative, and an author who measures their own hours will find different values.

    Cheap to write is not cheap to trust

    A common trap is counting the generation cost and forgetting the review cost. A draft that costs $20 to produce can still cost students dearly if no one checks it. Always budget reviewer time, and treat AI-generated content as a draft until a subject expert has read it.

    AI-generated content also raises a rights question. In the United States, courts and the Copyright Office have so far held that a work produced entirely by an AI system, without human authorship, is not eligible for copyright. Human choices in selecting, arranging, and editing can be protected. For open resources the point cuts both ways: material no one owns is easy to share, but a school cannot assume it can license purely AI-generated material, and it should check the terms of the AI tool that produced it.

    Open Educational Resources¶

    An open educational resource (OER) is a teaching, learning, or research material, in any medium, that is in the public domain or released under an open license that allows others to access, use, adapt, and share it at no cost or with minimal restrictions. The idea was formalized at a 2002 UNESCO forum and endorsed in UNESCO's 2012 Paris Declaration and 2019 Recommendation on OER. Well-known examples include OpenStax, which Rice University launched in 2012 to publish free peer-reviewed college textbooks; the CK-12 Foundation, which offers free K-12 materials; and K-12 curricula such as Illustrative Mathematics.

    The word open is the one that matters. A free textbook is one that students can use at no cost. Free is a statement about price, and open is a statement about permissions. A free PDF that you may read but not edit, translate, or share is free and not open. An open textbook is free and carries a license that grants permissions, so teachers can fix errors and adapt it to their own students. Many open textbooks are also free, and this chapter uses "free open textbook" for the combination.

    An open license is a copyright license by which the owner grants the public advance permission to use a work in specified ways, so that no one needs to ask. The most widely used family is the Creative Commons licenses.

    A Creative Commons license is one of a set of standard open licenses, published by the nonprofit Creative Commons, that combine four elements: BY (attribution: give credit), SA (share-alike: share adaptations under the same terms), NC (non-commercial: no commercial use), and ND (no derivatives: share only unchanged copies). The six main licenses are:

    License Elements What it allows
    CC BY Attribution Almost anything, if you credit the author
    CC BY-SA Attribution, share-alike Adaptations allowed if shared under the same license
    CC BY-NC Attribution, non-commercial Adaptations allowed for non-commercial use only
    CC BY-NC-SA Attribution, non-commercial, share-alike Non-commercial adaptations under the same license
    CC BY-ND Attribution, no derivatives Sharing unchanged copies only
    CC BY-NC-ND Attribution, non-commercial, no derivatives The most restrictive: unchanged, non-commercial sharing only

    The book you are reading uses CC BY-NC-SA, which is why it carries the non-commercial label in its footer.

    The 5R Permissions¶

    The 5R permissions, described by the education researcher David Wiley, are the five things an open license lets you do. They are the working test of whether something is truly open:

    1. Retain: make, own, and control copies of the work, such as downloading and archiving it.
    2. Reuse: use the work in its original form in many settings, such as a class or a website.
    3. Revise: adapt, edit, translate, or reformat the work.
    4. Remix: combine the work with other materials to create something new.
    5. Redistribute: share copies of the original or your changed version with others.

    The licenses in the table grant the 5Rs to different degrees. CC BY grants all five. A no-derivatives license withholds revise and remix. A non-commercial license limits what a school may do if it also sells a course. A school choosing a textbook should check which of the five it needs, and the sim below, reused from the companion book OER Evaluator, practices matching classroom actions to permissions.

    License check in thirty seconds

    Before a school adapts an open textbook, find the license in the footer and ask one question: "Will we need to edit it, combine it, or hand out our version?" If the answer is yes, a BY or BY-SA license is safest. NC and ND can block exactly the things teachers most want to do.

    The OpenBook QR Score: Judging an Open Textbook¶

    A free textbook is only useful if it is good and if others can reuse it. Textbook quality is the degree to which a textbook helps learners learn: whether it is accurate, aligned with the learning outcomes it claims, readable, accessible to all students, and gives learners practice with feedback. Quality is a matter of judgment, which is why different reviewers using different checklists reach different conclusions. A repeatable method was needed.

    The OpenBook QR Score, developed in the author's companion book OER Evaluator, is that method. It scores a resource on ten metrics worth ten points each, for a total from 0 to 100. Five metrics measure quality and five measure reusability, and the score is reported with both halves, as in "QR 65 (Q 32 / R 33)." The letters stand for Quality and Reusability.

    Quality (up to 50 points) Reusability (up to 50 points)
    Accuracy and currency Open license
    Alignment to learning outcomes Modularity
    Readability Metadata
    Accessibility Editable source
    Practice with feedback Portability

    The quality subscore is the sum of the five quality metrics, from 0 to 50, and shows how well the resource helps learners learn. (The OER Evaluator calls it the quality subtotal.) The reusability subscore is the sum of the five reusability metrics, from 0 to 50, and shows how easily others can find, change, and reuse the resource. The five reusability metrics in plain terms: an open license clearly grants permissions; modularity means the parts can be lifted out and used alone; metadata is structured descriptive information that lets people and software find the resource; an editable source means the original files, not just a finished copy, are available; and portability means the content works across devices and platforms.

    The total places a resource in a band: Excellent (90 to 100), Good (75 to 89), Fair (50 to 74), Weak (25 to 49), and Poor (0 to 24). A band is a quick label and not an adoption decision, and a bare total can hide a weak half, which is why the score is always reported with both subscores.

    Worked Example: Comparing Two Open Textbooks¶

    A school is choosing between two free open algebra textbooks. A reviewer scores each on the ten metrics. These scores are illustrative.

    Metric Open Algebra FreeMath Complete
    Accuracy and currency 9 6
    Alignment to learning outcomes 8 6
    Readability 8 7
    Accessibility 7 5
    Practice with feedback 8 6
    Quality subscore (Q) 40 30
    Open license 6 9
    Modularity 6 8
    Metadata 5 7
    Editable source 7 7
    Portability 8 7
    Reusability subscore (R) 32 38
    Total 72 68
    OpenBook QR Score QR 72 (Q 40 / R 32) QR 68 (Q 30 / R 38)

    Both books fall in the Fair band, and the totals differ by only four points. The halves tell the story: Open Algebra is the better book to hand students tomorrow, while FreeMath Complete is the better raw material for a district that wants to adapt, combine, and republish. Neither total alone would reveal that. The sim below, reused from the same companion book, lets you practice the calculation from metric scores to subscores, total, and band.

    Rising Quality Scores¶

    Rising quality scores is the trend of open textbooks improving on measures like the OpenBook QR Score over time. The reason follows from the structure of the score. Several of its ten metrics are mechanical improvements that AI does cheaply and well: rewriting for readability, adding text descriptions to images for accessibility, generating practice questions with feedback, writing metadata, and splitting content into modules. These are the tasks that a human editor skips because they are tedious.

    Worked Example: A Semester of Improvement¶

    An open textbook, Community Algebra, is scored before and after a semester of AI-assisted improvement, with each change reviewed by the author. The scores are illustrative.

    Metric Before After What changed
    Accuracy and currency 7 8 Out-of-date examples refreshed and checked
    Alignment to learning outcomes 6 7 Outcomes written for each section
    Readability 6 8 Long sentences shortened
    Accessibility 5 8 Alt text added to every image
    Practice with feedback 7 8 Self-check questions with explanations added
    Q subscore 31 39
    Open license 8 9 Clear license statement added
    Modularity 4 7 Chapters split into stand-alone sections
    Metadata 3 8 Structured metadata added to each page
    Editable source 6 8 Source files published
    Portability 6 8 Exported to open formats
    R subscore 27 40
    Total 58 (QR 58, Fair) 79 (QR 79, Good)

    The book moves from Fair to Good, and the reusability half rises most. A caution belongs with this example: a higher score is not proof that students learn more. The score describes properties of the book. Whether the improved book helps students is a question for the kind of controlled comparison that Chapter 2 described, and the OER Evaluator teaches how to run one.

    Content Commoditization and Publisher Disruption¶

    Content commoditization is the process by which a product becomes interchangeable with competitors and its price falls toward its cost of production, because buyers can no longer tell it apart on quality. When AI makes content cheap to produce, and open licenses make it free to copy, level 1 and level 2 content (Chapter 6), meaning fixed text and interactive pages, tends toward a commodity. A publisher that sells such content competes with a free open textbook of comparable quality.

    Publisher disruption is the change that results for publishers: pressure on revenue from the content itself, and a forced shift to other ways of earning. A company like Chegg, which sold homework help and textbook services to students, saw its stock price drop by about half in a single day in May 2023 after its chief executive said that ChatGPT was hurting its growth, which is an early and public example of how quickly AI can undercut a content-based business. Established textbook publishers face a slower version of the same pressure.

    The question for a school leader is not whether publishers will adapt but what they will sell. Value beyond content names what stays valuable when content is cheap:

    • Trust and quality assurance: someone accountable for accuracy, bias review, and alignment with standards.
    • Assessment and data: banks of validated questions, and analytics that show which students need help.
    • Integration: working inside the school's learning management system and roster, with single sign-on and accessibility support.
    • Teacher development: training and coaching that change classroom practice (Chapter 16).
    • Human services: tutoring, coaching, and support for students and teachers.
    • Privacy and safety: the legal responsibility for protecting student data at levels 3 and above (Chapter 10).

    This is good news and bad news for schools. The good news is that a school no longer needs to pay a premium for content it can get free. The bad news is that the pressure on publishers can push them to compensate with data collection, subscriptions, and lock-in. A purchasing decision should therefore ask what the school is paying for, and whether it is something content cannot supply.

    Worked Example: What Is the $900,000 Paying For?¶

    Mr. Patel asks the publisher to break down its $900,000 proposal for a 12,000-student district. A simple version of the answer might look like this.

    Item Annual cost Is it content?
    Digital textbook license $240,000 Yes
    Adaptive practice platform and student records (Level 3) $300,000 Partly
    Assessment bank and teacher dashboard $150,000 No
    Teacher training and coaching $120,000 No
    Support, integration, and data protection $90,000 No
    Total $900,000

    Only $240,000, about 27 percent, pays for content that has an open equivalent. The remaining $660,000 pays for platform, assessment, training, and support, which are the things Value beyond content names. The district can now ask a better question: which of those do we need, which can we get elsewhere or build ourselves, and which are worth the price?

    Putting a Cost on Each Level¶

    Mr. Patel's original question, "what will it cost per student, all in," needs a model. Cost per student is the total annual cost of a product or program divided by the number of students it serves, the same logic as the per-pupil spending of Chapter 1 applied to one line item. Cost modeling is the practice of building a simple, explicit model of the costs of an option, with stated assumptions, so that choices can be compared and tested.

    A total cost of ownership (TCO) is the full cost of using something over its life, including not only the purchase price but also the devices to run it, the training to use it, the support, the data protection, and the replacement. TCO is the reason a "free" product is not always cheap. Software may be free while the laptop to run it is not.

    The next sections build the model, one level at a time, using illustrative numbers that a school should replace with its own quotes.

    Level 2 Cost Per Student¶

    The Level 2 cost per student is the cost of an interactive textbook served as ordinary web pages. Hosting static pages is close to free, and the content is open or one-time. The book's penny-per-day vision sets a target: about one cent per student per school day. Over a 180-day year that is \( 180 \times \$0.01 = \$1.80 \) per student, the figure used here for content and hosting. Adding $4.00 per student per year as the share of teacher training gives a Level 2 cost of $5.80 per student per year, not counting the student's device.

    Level 3 Cost Per Student¶

    The Level 3 cost per student adds what adaptation needs: a place to store each student's records, such as a Learning Record Store (Chapter 8), at about $3.00 per student per year; privacy governance, meaning staff time for data-protection reviews and audits (Chapter 10), at about $2.00; and more training, $6.00 in all instead of $4.00. The Level 3 total is \( \$1.80 + \$3.00 + \$2.00 + \$6.00 = \$12.80 \) per student per year.

    Level 4 Cost Per Student¶

    The Level 4 cost per student adds the chatbot. Inference cost (Chapter 4) depends on tokens, sessions, and price. At 20,000 tokens per session, one session a day, 180 days, the year's usage is 3.6 million tokens. At a price of $2.00 per million tokens, a price chosen to represent a stronger model than the $0.07 GPT-3.5-level price of Chapter 4, inference costs \( 3.6 \times \$2.00 = \$7.20 \). Add $1.00 for reviewing logs and guardrail monitoring (Chapter 12), and training rises to $8.00. The Level 4 total is \( \$1.80 + \$3.00 + \$2.00 + \$1.00 + \$8.00 + \$7.20 = \$23.00 \).

    Worked Example: The Table Mr. Patel Asked For¶

    The table puts the levels side by side, with a print textbook as the baseline. The print baseline assumes an $80 book used for six years in four core subjects, which is \( \$80/6 \times 4 \approx \$53.33 \) per student per year. The device column assumes a school laptop of $300 used four years, or $75 a year. All values are illustrative.

    Print (Level 1) Level 2 Level 3 Level 4
    Content and hosting $53.33 $1.80 $1.80 $1.80
    Records platform n/a n/a $3.00 $3.00
    Privacy governance n/a n/a $2.00 $2.00
    AI inference n/a n/a n/a $7.20
    Guardrail log review n/a n/a n/a $1.00

    For a 12,000-student district, the Level 2 software and training cost \( 12{,}000 \times \$5.80 = \$69{,}600 \) a year, Level 3 costs $153,600, Level 4 costs $276,000, and print costs about $640,000. Even the Level 4 figure of $23.00 is about 0.15 percent of the $15,600 per-pupil spending of Chapter 1.

    The lesson of the bottom row is the surprising one. When devices must be bought, the device is the largest cost at every digital level, about 77 to 93 percent of the total. This is why Mr. Patel's question cannot be answered by comparing software prices. A school that already owns devices gets Level 2 and Level 3 at a small fraction of the price of print, and a school that has to buy them sees the gap close. It is also why equity (Chapter 3) turns on devices and connectivity as much as on content.

    The Penny-Per-Day Vision and Universal Textbook Access¶

    The penny-per-day vision is the book's name for a target: high-quality interactive textbooks, at Level 2 and increasingly Level 3, costing a school roughly one cent per student per day, about $1.80 a year. It is a target and not a price list. It is achievable for content and hosting because of the three forces of this chapter: AI lowers the cost of creating content, open licenses remove the cost of copying it, and static web pages are nearly free to serve.

    Universal textbook access is the goal that follows: every student, in every school, has a free, high-quality, interactive textbook. The course description's prediction of Chapter 5 is that this could happen within three to four years. The barriers that remain are not the price of content. They are devices and connectivity (the equity issues of Chapter 3), teacher training, accessibility for students with disabilities, translation into the languages students speak, and the privacy protections that levels 3 and above require.

    Worked Example: Pennies at Scale¶

    For the 12,000-student district, the penny-per-day content cost is \( 12{,}000 \times \$1.80 = \$21{,}600 \) a year. The district's total spending at the Chapter 1 figure is \( 12{,}000 \times \$15{,}600 = \$187.2 \) million. The content line is \( 21{,}600 / 187{,}200{,}000 \approx 0.012\% \), about one dollar in every 8,700. Compare this with the $240,000 content license in the publisher's $900,000 proposal, which is more than eleven times as much for content with an open equivalent.

    Answering Mr. Patel¶

    Mr. Patel's question now has an answer in three parts. Content is falling toward a penny a day, because AI cuts the cost of making it and open licenses cut the cost of copying it. The all-in cost per student depends on the level: in our illustrative model, about $6 at Level 2, $13 at Level 3, and $23 at Level 4 when devices are already owned, against about $53 for print, and about $75 more per student a year if devices must be bought. And the right way to compare a free open textbook with a paid product is to score both on quality and reusability, and to ask what the money pays for beyond content.

    The story has both sides. The benefit is large: a district that adopts an open Level 2 textbook might redirect most of a $900,000 content budget to teachers, devices, and support. The risks are also real. A free textbook can be a poor one, so check its QR score. An NC or ND license can block the changes a school needs. AI-generated content can carry errors, and purely AI-generated material may not be licensable. And the savings can be eaten by devices, training, and data protection that a software invoice never shows. The response is the set of habits from this chapter: check the license, score the quality, budget the review, and build the full cost model before the vote.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Content creation cost is mostly up-front, which is why a textbook publisher's publisher business model was built on selling many copies at a high textbook price.
    • AI-generated content drives falling production cost: in our illustration a chapter falls from about $4,000 to about $1,120, as long as the human review step stays.
    • An open educational resource carries an open license; a free textbook is free in price, and open adds the 5R permissions (retain, reuse, revise, remix, redistribute). A Creative Commons license grants them to different degrees.
    • The OpenBook QR Score rates ten metrics worth 100 points: a quality subscore (Q, out of 50) and a reusability subscore (R, out of 50). Textbook quality needs both halves reported, since totals hide which half is weak.
    • Rising quality scores reflect AI doing the tedious improvements, but a higher score does not prove students learn more.
    • Content commoditization pushes content prices toward cost, causing publisher disruption; value beyond content is trust, assessment, integration, training, services, and data protection.
    • Cost per student and cost modeling make options comparable: in the illustration, Level 2, Level 3, and Level 4 cost per student are about $5.80, $12.80, and $23.00 without devices.
    • Total cost of ownership includes devices, which at $75 a year dominate the software at every digital level.
    • The penny-per-day vision (about $1.80 per student per year) makes universal textbook access plausible for content, with devices, training, accessibility, and privacy as the remaining barriers.
