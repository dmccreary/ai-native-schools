    Social Learning and Human Skills¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter introduces social constructionism and constructivism as the theoretical basis for the pro-social afternoon, along with the specific human skills it targets: collaboration, teamwork, communication, empathy, creativity, and social-emotional learning. Readers finish able to explain why students need practice working with other people even when AI can teach thinking skills well on its own.

    What the Morning Cannot Teach¶

    By Friday afternoon, a team of four seventh graders at Lincoln Middle School, Maya, Jordan, Dev, and Priya, is stuck. They have two weeks to cut the cafeteria's food waste, and they have spent forty minutes arguing about whether to start with a poster or a survey. Jordan, who finished the week's adaptive math in half the time, has taken over the keyboard. Maya has not said a word. Dev has stopped listening. Priya is quietly drawing.

    No adaptive software, however good, would have helped this team, because nothing in the problem is a lack of knowledge. The problem is between people: who decides, who is heard, how a disagreement gets settled, and whether Maya feels that her idea would be welcome. Those are the skills of this chapter, and they are the reason the book's answer to "what will fill the rest of the day?" is people.

    Part 2 argued that AI can teach thinking skills well and that a compressed morning might free time. Part 3 begins here with the other half of the question. If a tutor can teach critical and computational thinking, why do students still need an afternoon? The answer has two parts: a body of learning theory that says students learn deeply by building things with others, and a set of human skills that are practiced only in the company of other people.

    The other half of the day

    This chapter explains why the afternoon isn't filler: the learning theories behind it, and the skills students can only build with each other. You'll leave able to say why a student who has mastered the morning's content still needs to practice listening, disagreeing, and building something with a team. Let's find the balance between what AI does well and what only people can teach each other.

    Pro-Social Behavior and Why Students Need People¶

    Pro-social behavior is voluntary behavior intended to benefit other people: helping, sharing, cooperating, comforting, and including. The psychologist Nancy Eisenberg's long research program found that it grows from a mix of empathy, practice, and example, and that it is learned in relationships. It is the opposite of a purely individual, screen-based experience, and it is the organizing idea of this book's afternoon.

    Skills that need people are the abilities that can be developed only through interaction with other people, because the other person is part of what is being learned. Chapter 12 distinguished them from the skills AI teaches well. They include collaborating on a task, negotiating, persuading a real audience, resolving a conflict, noticing how someone else feels, and caring about a problem that is not your own. An AI can explain what empathy is, but a student learns it from the experience of understanding and being understood by another person.

    There is a practical case as well. Employers consistently rank skills such as communication, teamwork, and problem solving among the most valued in surveys of hiring managers. And there is a human case. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness and isolation described them as an epidemic with serious effects on health, and research by Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues has linked strong social connection to longer life. A school that spends its mornings on screens carries a responsibility to build connection in the rest of the day.

    Worked Example: Sorting the Skills¶

    Skill Can an AI tutor teach it well? Why
    Solving a unit-rate problem Yes Clear right answers, small steps, instant feedback
    Debugging a program Yes Errors give objective feedback
    Persuading a school board Partly AI can critique a speech, but only a real audience pushes back
    Settling a disagreement within a team No It needs the real people involved
    Noticing that a teammate is upset No It requires reading another person in the moment

    Worked Example: Counting Pro-Social Acts¶

    Pro-social behavior can be observed, and a teacher who watches for it learns where it grows. Over three weeks of the food-waste project, Ms. Rivera tallies four kinds of acts she sees during the project block: helping (offering unasked-for assistance), sharing (giving up a turn or a resource), including (inviting a quiet teammate in), and comforting (responding to a teammate's frustration). The counts are illustrative.

    Kind of act Week 1 Week 2 Week 3
    Helping 6 9 12
    Sharing 3 4 6
    Including 2 5 7
    Comforting 1 2 2
    Total 12 20 27

    The total more than doubled, from 12 to 27, as the team learned its roles (the next section) and she began debriefing at the end of each block. Two cautions are worth making. The tally is a teacher's observation and not a measurement, and what she notices depends on where she stands. And Chapter 2's finding on rewards applies: handing out stickers for pro-social acts can turn kindness into a transaction, so the better reinforcement is to name the act and its effect: "When you asked Maya for her idea, she shared the survey plan we needed."

    How Students Learn: Four Theories¶

    Four related theories explain why building and doing things with others is such a powerful way to learn. They are easy to confuse, and the differences matter for designing an afternoon.

    Constructivism is the theory that learners actively build their own understanding by connecting new experiences to what they already know, rather than receiving knowledge ready-made. It draws on the work of the Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget, who described how children assimilate new information into existing mental structures and, when it does not fit, accommodate by changing the structures. It connects directly to the prior knowledge of Chapter 2: what a student already knows shapes what they can learn next.

    Hands-on learning is learning by doing, through direct experience with materials, tools, experiments, or projects. Its modern roots are in John Dewey's argument, in Experience and Education (1938), that students learn through experiences connected to their lives. Hands-on activity is effective when it is guided and tied to the concepts being learned, and it is much weaker when students simply do things without direction, a point on which the Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark paper of Chapter 2 was right. A popular chart called Dale's Cone of Experience, which claims that people remember 10 percent of what they read and 90 percent of what they do, is a myth: the percentages were added later and have no research behind them.

    Worked Example: What Piaget's Stages Suggest for Afternoon Design¶

    Piaget described four broad stages of cognitive development. The ages are approximate, and modern research finds children vary and that the stages overlap, so treat the table as a rough guide for design and not as a rule.

    Stage (approximate age) How children tend to think A design implication for the afternoon
    Sensorimotor (birth to 2) Through senses and actions Not a school-age stage
    Preoperational (2 to 7) Through symbols and play; difficulty seeing other viewpoints Play-based, hands-on activity; very short teams of two
    Concrete operational (7 to 11) Logical about concrete things they can handle Projects with real objects and clear roles; teams of three or four
    Formal operational (11 and up) Abstract and hypothetical reasoning Debate, design under constraints, and projects that weigh trade-offs

    A kindergarten afternoon built around a debate would fail for a reason that has nothing to do with the teacher, and a high school afternoon of only stacking blocks would waste students' abilities. The stages are the reason the grade-band models of Chapter 20 differ.

    Worked Example: Constructivism and Hands-On Learning in a Fraction Lesson¶

    Dev believes that "a bigger denominator means a bigger fraction," because 8 is bigger than 4, so \( 1/8 \) must beat \( 1/4 \). A constructivist teacher does not simply say "wrong." The teacher gives him two identical paper strips, has him fold one into four equal parts and the other into eight, and shade one part of each. Laid side by side, the one-eighth is visibly smaller. His old idea, that bigger numbers mean bigger parts, cannot account for what he sees, so he revises it: more parts means smaller parts.

    Step What happens Theory
    Dev states his belief His prior knowledge is made visible Constructivism: start from what the learner knows
    He folds and shades the strips He handles real materials Hands-on learning
    The result contradicts his belief He feels the conflict Piaget's accommodation: the old structure must change
    He explains the new rule in his own words He rebuilds the idea Learning is constructed, not received

    The same lesson without the strips, with only a rule to memorize, would leave the belief in place beneath the rule. The hands-on step works because it was guided: the teacher chose the strips and the question, so the activity targeted the misconception. A free-play session with the same paper strips would have been enjoyable and might not have touched it.

    Social constructivism is constructivism with a social foundation: the theory, associated with the Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky, that knowledge is built in interaction with other people, through conversation, shared activity, and the help of those who know more. For Vygotsky, language and culture are tools of thought, and a child first does something with others before doing it alone.

    The zone of proximal development (ZPD) is Vygotsky's term for the range of tasks a learner cannot yet do alone but can do with guidance or collaboration. Below the zone are tasks the learner can already do independently; above it are tasks too hard even with help. Teaching is most effective in the zone. The help given there is called scaffolding, a term from Wood, Bruner, and Ross in 1976: temporary support, such as a hint, a model, or a prompt, that is removed as the learner becomes capable. It is the hint ladder of Chapter 2, seen from the theory side.

    Constructionism, spelled with an n and a different idea from constructivism, is the theory of Seymour Papert at MIT that people learn especially well when they are building something tangible and shareable in the world, such as a program, a robot, a model, a story, or a machine. Papert was a student of Piaget and the inventor of the Logo programming language for children. In his book Mindstorms (1980) he argued that the act of making an artifact, which can be examined, shown, and fixed, makes thinking visible and gives the learner something to be proud of and to improve.

    Social constructionism, in the sense this book uses it, is constructionism done with other people: students build things together, so that the shared artifact becomes the focus of conversation, disagreement, and revision. (The same words name a different idea in sociology, the claim by Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann in 1966 that social reality is built by human agreement; that is not what is meant here.) Learning by building together is the practice that follows, and it is the heart of the afternoon: a team designs, builds, tests, and presents something real, and learns the content, the skills, and each other along the way.

    Theory Key thinker Core claim A classroom example
    Constructivism Piaget Learners build understanding from what they already know A student reworks a wrong idea about fractions after seeing a model that contradicts it
    Social constructivism Vygotsky Understanding is built through interaction, with help from others A student solves a problem with a peer's hint that she could not solve alone
    Constructionism Papert Learning is strongest when making a shareable artifact A student builds a working robot and debugs it
    Social constructionism (this book's sense) Papert's ideas applied in groups Learning is strongest when a team builds something together A team builds a food-waste dashboard and argues over its design

    Worked Example: Fading the Scaffold¶

    Scaffolding is temporary. It is offered in the zone of proximal development and then withdrawn, so that the learner does the task alone. Here is a six-session fade for a student learning to set up a ratio table, using the support levels from the hint ladder of Chapter 2.

    Session Support offered Who does the thinking
    1 A fully worked example, with every step explained The tutor, with the student watching
    2 A worked example with the last two steps left blank Mostly the tutor
    3 A partly filled table and a prompt for each step Shared
    4 A blank table and one question: "What is the unit?" Mostly the student
    5 A blank table and no prompts; hints on request The student
    6 A new problem type, no help, and a check afterward The student alone

    The skill of scaffolding is knowing when to step back. A tutor who stays at session 3 forever creates dependence, and one who jumps from session 1 to session 6 leaves the student stranded outside the zone. The observation that the student keeps asking for the next hint is the signal that the scaffold is still needed, and the observation that she no longer reads the hints is the signal to remove it. Adaptive software can read both signals from the log.

    Worked Example: What Making Teaches¶

    A seventh grader builds a small computer game that teaches a younger child to compare fractions. Constructionism predicts that the building teaches more than the topic.

    What the student did What it required her to understand or practice
    Chose which fraction pairs to show Which comparisons are easy and which are hard
    Wrote the rule that decides "bigger" An exact, step-by-step procedure (algorithmic thinking)
    Tested it on a friend, who found a bug with 2/4 and 1/2 That equivalent fractions break a naive rule
    Fixed the bug and added a hint Her own explanation of equivalence, in her own words
    Showed the game at the exhibition Explaining her thinking to an audience

    The assignment "do fractions worksheet 4" would give her one of these. The artifact gave her five, and it left something she could show and be proud of, which is the point of Papert's argument.

    Making makes thinking visible

    Notice what Papert noticed: when a student builds something, their thinking becomes an object everyone can see, test, and improve. A wrong idea in a student's head is hard to find; a bug in a student's robot is right there. That's why this book treats the project, not the worksheet, as the afternoon's unit of learning.

    Collaboration, Teamwork, and Team Problem Solving¶

    Collaboration is working together with others toward a shared outcome, in which the contribution of each person shapes what the group produces. It differs from cooperation in a small but important way: in cooperation people may divide a job into pieces and put the pieces together, while in collaboration the thinking itself is shared. Teamwork is the coordinated effort of a group of people with different roles toward a common aim, including the skills of planning, dividing the work, communicating, and supporting each other.

    Research on cooperative learning, led by David and Roger Johnson and by Robert Slavin, finds that students working in groups generally learn more than students working alone or in competition, when the groups are structured well. The Johnsons name five elements of a well-structured group: positive interdependence (members need one another to succeed), individual accountability (each person is responsible for their own part), face-to-face promotive interaction, the use of social skills, and group processing, in which the group reflects on how it is working. Slavin's reviews identify the two that matter most: a shared group goal and individual accountability. A group with no shared goal turns into a collection of individuals, and a group with no individual accountability lets one person do the work.

    Shared goals are the outcomes a team agrees it is working toward and each member understands and accepts. A good shared goal is specific, visible, and important to all members, such as "reduce the cafeteria's food waste by 20 percent in two weeks" and not "work on food waste." Team roles are the defined responsibilities of members, such as facilitator (keeps the group on task and ensures everyone is heard), recorder (writes down decisions and next steps), timekeeper (tracks the schedule), and reporter (presents the team's work). Roles prevent two common failures: the member who does everything and the member who does nothing. Good roles rotate, so that every student practices each.

    Team problem solving is a team's process for working through a problem it has not seen before. A simple version has six steps: define the problem, break it into parts, assign the parts to roles, gather and judge the evidence, decide, and reflect on how the team and the result went.

    Applying thinking on teams is using the thinking skills of Chapters 11 and 12 inside that process. Decomposition splits the problem into tasks, pattern recognition spots what the data shows, abstraction keeps the team focused on what matters, and algorithmic thinking turns a plan into clear steps. The evidence evaluation and argument analysis of Chapter 11 are what a team uses to decide, instead of simply following the loudest voice.

    Worked Example: Cooperation Versus Collaboration¶

    The two words are used loosely, and the difference matters for how a task is designed. Take the same project, a poster about the water cycle, done two ways.

    Cooperation Collaboration
    How the work is divided Each student does a panel alone and the panels are stuck together The team decides together what the poster must say, and parts are drawn from that
    Where the thinking happens Within each student Between the students, in conversation
    What if one student misunderstands Only that panel is wrong The team's discussion is likely to catch it
    What each student learns Their own panel's topic The topic, and how to reason with others

    Cooperation is efficient, and it is sometimes the right choice. Collaboration is what the afternoon is for, because it exercises the skills of Chapter 13 and not just the product. A simple test of a design is to ask whether the task requires students to talk with each other to succeed. If each could finish alone, it is cooperation at best.

    Worked Example: Tuckman's Stages in a Team's Life¶

    Teams rarely work smoothly from the start. In 1965, the psychologist Bruce Tuckman described four stages, with a fifth added later, that groups pass through. The food-waste team shows each.

    Stage What it looks like The food-waste team
    Forming Polite, uncertain, looking to the leader Week 1: students wait for the teacher to say what to do
    Storming Disagreement about goals and roles; frustration The forty-minute argument about a poster versus a survey
    Norming Agreeing on roles and ways of working After the rescue: roles assigned, a rotation agreed
    Performing Working fluently toward the goal Weeks 3 to 5: data collected, a plan made
    Adjourning Wrapping up, reflecting, saying goodbye The exhibition and a debrief

    The useful message is that the storming stage is normal, not a sign of failure. A teacher who knows this does not step in to end the argument at once, and does not let it harden into a grudge. The structure questions of the earlier worked example are the tools for moving a team from storming to norming.

    Worked Example: Writing a Shared Goal¶

    A shared goal is specific, visible, and accepted by every member. Compare drafts of the food-waste team's goal.

    Draft Problem Revision
    "Work on food waste." Not measurable; any activity counts "Reduce the cafeteria's food waste."
    "Reduce the cafeteria's food waste." No size and no date "Cut food waste by 20 percent in two weeks."
    "Cut food waste by 20 percent in two weeks." Not clear how it will be measured, or by whom "Cut weekly cafeteria food waste, weighed by the kitchen staff, from 120 kg to 96 kg or less within two weeks."

    The last version has a number, a date, a method, and a witness. It can also be tested, so the team will know whether it succeeded, which is the property that makes a goal shared and not merely stated.

    Worked Example: Rotating the Roles¶

    Roles are most useful when they rotate, so that every student practices each. With four roles and four students, a simple rotation gives each student every role over four weeks.

    Week Facilitator Recorder Timekeeper Reporter
    1 Priya Maya Dev Jordan
    2 Maya Dev Jordan Priya
    3 Dev Jordan Priya Maya
    4 Jordan Priya Maya Dev

    Each column and each row contains every student exactly once. Maya, who rarely spoke, is the reporter in week 3 and the facilitator in week 2, which is how she is given the practice that her quietness would otherwise avoid. A teacher may adjust the order so a student takes on a harder role after a success, and not on the first day.

    Milk 18 15%
    Entrees 18 15%
    Fruit 14 12%

    The team brainstorms three fixes and estimates what each would save. Their estimates are guesses to be tested.

    Idea Estimated effect Saving
    A: smaller vegetable portions, with seconds available Cuts vegetable waste by 30% \( 0.30 \times 46 = 13.8 \) kg
    B: bread served on request Cuts bread waste by 40% \( 0.40 \times 24 = 9.6 \) kg
    C: milk taken only on request Cuts milk waste by 50% \( 0.50 \times 18 = 9.0 \) kg

    Their goal is a 20 percent cut, or 24 kg. Idea A alone saves 13.8 kg, 11.5 percent. A and B together save 23.4 kg, 19.5 percent, just short. All three save 32.4 kg, 27 percent, which meets the goal. The team chooses all three, with A first because it saves the most, and plans to re-weigh after a week to check the estimates. Notice that the decision came from decomposition (the table), pattern recognition (vegetables dominate), and evidence evaluation (estimates to be tested), which is the "applying thinking on teams" of this section.

    Worked Example: Rescuing the Food-Waste Team¶

    Return to Maya, Jordan, Dev, and Priya, who are arguing about a poster versus a survey. A teacher intervenes with five questions, one for each structural element.

    Question What the team finds Fix
    What is our shared goal? They had two goals: Jordan's "finish fast" and Maya's "find out why food is thrown away" Agree on one measurable goal: cut the waste measured in kilograms
    Who has which role? Jordan is doing everything; nobody is facilitator Assign facilitator (Priya), recorder (Maya), timekeeper (Dev), reporter (Jordan); rotate next week
    How do we break the problem down? The poster and survey are both parts of a bigger plan Decompose: collect data, find the main cause, choose a fix, present
    Who is accountable for what? One person was holding the keyboard Each member owns one part and shows their piece
    How will we decide? The loudest voice wins Decide using the data on which food is wasted most

    Within ten minutes the team has a plan. Nothing the team lacked was knowledge. The intervention was structure, and the structure is what a good afternoon program teaches.

    Ask the structural question first

    When a student team stalls, don't say "work harder." Ask: Do we share one goal, does everyone have a role, does each person own a piece, are we listening, and is there an argument nobody is dealing with?

    Communication, Listening, and Conflict¶

    Communication skills are the abilities to express ideas clearly, in speech and writing, and to understand what others express. They include speaking so an audience follows, writing for a reader, asking good questions, and reading nonverbal signals such as tone and posture. A team's ability to share a goal, divide roles, and settle disagreements depends on them.

    Active listening is listening with full attention to understand the speaker, and showing that you have understood. It goes beyond waiting for your turn to talk. The psychologist Carl Rogers popularized it in counseling, and its parts are simple: give your full attention; paraphrase what you heard ("So you're saying the poster should come after the survey"); ask a clarifying question; reflect feelings ("It sounds like you're frustrated"); and hold back judgment and advice until the speaker has finished. It is also one of the clearest places where an AI cannot stand in for a person: a student learns to listen by being listened to.

    Conflict resolution is the process of working through a disagreement so that the people involved reach an outcome they can accept, while keeping the relationship intact. A widely taught approach, from Roger Fisher and William Ury's Getting to Yes (1981), is to focus on interests instead of positions: Jordan's position is "the poster comes first," but his interest may be "I want us to finish on time," and a plan that meets the interest may suit everyone.

    Worked Example: Saying It Clearly¶

    Communication skills include writing a message so that it can be understood and acted on. Jordan sends the team a message after a bad meeting. Compare two versions.

    Version Text Problem or strength
    1 "You guys never listen, and I'm tired of doing everything." Accuses, generalizes, and gives no request; invites defensiveness
    2 "In today's meeting I felt unheard when my idea about the survey was skipped. I'd like us to give each person two minutes to propose before we vote. Can we try that tomorrow?" Names a specific moment and a feeling, makes a concrete request, and asks for agreement

    The second version is an I-statement: it describes what happened, how the speaker felt, and what they want, without judging the others. It does not guarantee a good reply, but it makes one possible.

    Worked Example: Positions and Interests¶

    Conflict resolution, in the approach of Fisher and Ury, separates what people say they want (a position) from why they want it (an interest). In the food-waste team's argument, Jordan says "the poster comes first" and Maya says "the survey comes first."

    Jordan Maya
    Position Poster first Survey first
    Interest "I want us to finish on time, and a poster is quick" "I want to find out why food is thrown away before we design anything"
    What both need A plan that finishes on time and rests on real data The same

    Once the interests are on the table, a plan that satisfies both is easy to find: spend two days on a short survey, then make the poster from what it shows. Neither position wins, and both interests are met. The skill is to ask "why do you want that?" before arguing about "what," and active listening is how the question gets an honest answer.

    Worked Example: Responses to a Teammate¶

    Dev says to the team, "I think this whole project is pointless; nobody cares about food waste." Consider five replies.

    Reply Kind Effect
    "That's not true, lots of people care." Judgment or argument Closes the conversation; Dev feels dismissed
    "You should be more positive." Advice Ignores Dev's feeling
    "So you feel like this won't change anything?" Paraphrase Shows Dev was heard; invites more
    "What made you think nobody cares?" Clarifying question Opens up the reason behind the comment
    "It sounds like you're discouraged." Reflecting feeling Names the feeling; builds trust

    The last three are active listening. The first two are common and well-meant, and they end the conversation. Dev's real concern, probably that earlier student projects led nowhere, only surfaces after he has been heard.

    Social-Emotional Learning¶

    Social-emotional learning (SEL) is the process through which people develop the skills to understand and manage their emotions, show care for others, form positive relationships, and make responsible decisions. The leading organization in the field, the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL), organizes it into five competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making. A 2011 meta-analysis by Joseph Durlak and colleagues of 213 school-based SEL programs, covering more than 270,000 students, found improvements in social and emotional skills, attitudes, and behavior, and an average gain in academic performance of about 11 percentile points. As with all pooled results, programs vary in quality, and the benefits depend on careful implementation.

    Several of the concepts of this chapter are parts of SEL.

    Self-awareness is the ability to recognize one's own emotions, strengths, limits, and values, and how they influence behavior. A student with self-awareness can say, "I get frustrated when I'm not understood, and I tend to take over." It is the foundation of the others, since a person who cannot name their feelings cannot manage them.

    Empathy is the ability to understand and share another person's feelings. Psychologists distinguish cognitive empathy, understanding what someone feels, from affective empathy, feeling something of what they feel. Both can be developed, and both are the root of pro-social behavior.

    Perspective taking is the ability to see a situation from another person's point of view, understanding what they think and why. It is the cognitive side of empathy, and it grows with practice: debating a position you disagree with, reading a story from a different character's view, or hearing a teammate's reason. Without it, conflict resolution is just negotiation between positions.

    Relationship skills are the abilities to form and keep healthy relationships: communicating clearly, listening, cooperating, resolving conflict, and asking for and offering help. They are active listening, communication, and conflict resolution assembled into a habit.

    Belonging is the feeling of being a valued member of a group, accepted and able to be oneself. Research by Gregory Walton, Geoffrey Cohen, and colleagues suggests that students who feel they belong in a school or subject engage more and do better, and that brief efforts to build belonging can help students from groups who are at risk of feeling out of place, though the size of the effect varies by setting. Human connection is the experience of meaningful, caring relationships with other people, of which belonging is one form. It is what a student with a screen-heavy morning most needs from the afternoon.

    Worked Example: Three Kinds of Empathy¶

    Psychologists often distinguish kinds of empathy, which can be developed separately. Suppose a teammate, Dev, learns that his project idea was not chosen and goes quiet.

    Kind What it is What it looks like for a teammate
    Cognitive empathy (perspective taking) Understanding what the other person thinks and feels "He probably feels his idea wasn't valued."
    Affective empathy Feeling something of what the other feels A pang of disappointment on his behalf
    Compassionate empathy Being moved to help "Dev, I liked your idea about taste tests; can we use part of it?"

    Cognitive empathy can be taught directly, through activities such as retelling an event from another person's point of view. Affective empathy grows through relationships. Compassionate empathy is the one that produces pro-social behavior, and it needs the other two plus a chance to act, which a team project provides.

    Worked Example: A Belonging Check-In¶

    Belonging can be checked with a few questions asked regularly. A teacher gives each student a five-question check at the start and end of the project, answered from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). The numbers are illustrative.

    Question Class average, start Class average, end
    I feel I belong in this team 3.4 4.1
    My ideas are listened to 3.1 3.9
    I can disagree without worrying 2.8 3.7
    I know my teammates care about me 3.5 4.2
    I look forward to this class 3.3 4.0

    The biggest gain was on disagreeing without worry, which is what the structure and the listening practice were meant to build. The teacher also looks at the lowest individual scores, since an average can hide the student for whom nothing changed, and follows up with a conversation. The check is an input for the teacher's attention, not a grade.

    Worked Example: SEL in One Project¶

    Over the two weeks of the food-waste project, a teacher watches for SEL competencies and records one example of each.

    Competency What the teacher saw How it was supported
    Self-awareness Jordan said, "I take over when I'm worried we'll be late" A weekly reflection: "How did I act in my team?"
    Empathy and perspective taking Dev, after being heard, listened to Maya's reason for the survey Active-listening practice before each meeting
    Relationship skills Priya and Maya resolved a disagreement over the design A short conflict-resolution routine
    Belonging Maya, who rarely spoke, was asked to present her survey The reporter role rotated

    The teacher is not adding SEL as an extra lesson. The project is the lesson: the competencies appear because the structure creates the need for them.

    Don't turn feelings into a worksheet

    A common trap is teaching SEL as a fill-in-the-blank lesson about feelings, separate from real work. Students learn these skills in the moments a team actually needs them, with an adult who notices and names what happened. A project with good structure and a teacher who debriefs beats a stand-alone lesson.

    Creativity¶

    Creativity is the ability to produce ideas or products that are both novel and useful or valuable. It is not only for artists: a student who finds a new way to sort the cafeteria's waste is being creative. Creativity depends on knowledge, as the earlier chapters stress, since one has to know what has been done to do something new, and it is nourished by freedom, challenge, and an audience.

    Divergent thinking is the generation of many different ideas or solutions to an open-ended question, in contrast to convergent thinking, which narrows to the single best answer. The psychologist J. P. Guilford drew attention to it in the 1950s, and E. Paul Torrance developed tests of it. Typical tasks ask, "How many uses can you think of for a paperclip?" Fluency (the number of ideas), flexibility (the variety), and originality (how unusual) are the usual measures, and Guilford's list also included elaboration (the amount of detail). Creative work alternates the two: diverge to generate, converge to choose.

    Creative confidence is the belief that one can come up with new ideas and act on them, an application of the psychologist Albert Bandura's idea of self-efficacy. Tom and David Kelley of the design firm IDEO use the term for the confidence that many people lose when they decide they are "not creative." It matters because creative work involves being wrong in public, and students who fear that will not try.

    Worked Example: Scoring Divergent Thinking¶

    Divergent thinking is often scored on three measures from Guilford's work, which Torrance's tests also used (Torrance later dropped flexibility from the figural test because it tracked fluency so closely). A student has two minutes to list uses for a paperclip and writes 12 ideas.

    Idea Category Given by fewer than 5% of the class?
    Hold papers together Fastening No
    Bookmark Marking No
    Reset a phone Tools No
    Zipper pull Fastening No
    Pick a lock Tools Yes
    Earring Jewelry No
    Chain for decoration Jewelry No
    Cleaning tool Tools No
    Antenna for a toy Building Yes
    Sculpture wire Art No
    Hang a poster Fastening No
    Tiny hook for a plant Fastening No

    Her fluency, the number of ideas, is 12. Her flexibility, the number of different categories, is 6 (fastening, marking, tools, jewelry, building, art). Her originality, the number of ideas that few others gave, is 2. A teacher can use the three scores to coach: a student with high fluency and low flexibility is stuck in one kind of idea and can be asked "what is a completely different kind of use?" The scores are a conversation starter, not a verdict on a child's creativity.

    Worked Example: A Divergent Round¶

    The food-waste team has ten minutes to produce ideas to reduce waste, with one rule: no judging until the clock stops.

    Round Rule Result
    Diverge (5 minutes) Say any idea; no criticism; build on others' ideas 23 ideas, including "waste art," "tray sizes," "taste-test Tuesday," "compost garden"
    Group (2 minutes) Cluster similar ideas Four clusters: portion size, menu, awareness, composting
    Converge (3 minutes) Vote, then check each top idea against the data Portion size and awareness chosen, since the data show most waste is large servings of vegetables

    The round shows both modes of thinking in order, and each has a rule: no criticism while diverging, and evidence while converging. It also builds creative confidence, because every student contributed ideas without being told they were wrong.

    'I'm not creative' is a habit, not a fact

    If a student, or you, say "I'm not the creative type," that's usually a lesson learned from being judged too soon. Try the ten-minute divergent round with no judging for five of them; people surprise themselves.

    Why the Afternoon Still Matters¶

    Return to the question this chapter began with: if AI can teach the thinking, why do students need people? Three reasons run through the chapter. First, the learning theories say that understanding is built through experience, with other people, by making things; a worksheet or an adaptive screen is only part of that. Second, the skills of collaboration, communication, empathy, and creative confidence are learned in the company of others and need practice, structure, and an adult who notices. Third, belonging and connection are needs in themselves, and a school day of screens would starve them.

    The story has both sides, as always. The benefit: an afternoon in which students build things together, learn to listen, and are known by name. The risks: an afternoon that drifts into unstructured free time (Chapter 14 treats this directly), teams in which one student does everything, SEL reduced to a worksheet, and creativity crushed by early judgment. The response is the structure this chapter described: shared goals, roles, accountability, listening routines, conflict routines, and an adult who debriefs.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Pro-social behavior and skills that need people are learned through interaction; AI teaches procedures and thinking well, and people teach each other collaboration and care.
    • Constructivism (Piaget): learners build understanding from what they know. Social constructivism (Vygotsky): through interaction, within the zone of proximal development, with scaffolding. Constructionism (Papert): by making tangible artifacts. Social constructionism, in this book's sense, and learning by building together: by building a shared artifact as a team.
    • Hands-on learning works when guided; Dale's Cone and its percentages are a myth.
    • Collaboration and teamwork work when there are shared goals, team roles, and individual accountability; team problem solving uses decomposition, evidence, and argument, which is applying thinking on teams.
    • Communication skills include active listening (paraphrase, clarifying question, reflecting feeling) and conflict resolution (interests, not positions).
    • Social-emotional learning covers self-awareness, empathy, perspective taking, relationship skills, belonging, and human connection, and is best taught inside real projects.
    • Creativity needs divergent thinking followed by convergent choice, and creative confidence is built by not judging too soon.
    • When a team stalls, ask the structure questions first.

    You can say why the afternoon matters

    You just learned four learning theories, what makes a team work, how active listening differs from advice, and how social-emotional skills grow inside real projects. That's the case for the afternoon, grounded in theory. Chapter 14 turns it into practice: what actually fills those hours. One step at a time, together.
