    Technology Adoption and the Diffusion of Innovation¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter introduces the technology adoption curve: Rogers' diffusion of innovations, the five innovation attributes, adopter categories from innovators to laggards, the chasm between early adopters and the early majority, and the hype cycle. Readers finish able to place a new technology or practice on the adoption curve and explain why it might stall at the chasm.

    "Why Hasn't Anyone Else Done This?"¶

    Eight weeks after the pilot at Lincoln Middle School, the results are good. Maya's math scores are up, six teachers are using the Level 2 textbook, and the principal is pleased. At the next district meeting, Ms. Okafor presents the results and expects enthusiasm. What she gets is a long silence, followed by a polite question from a principal at another school: "That's great for your six teachers. Why would I expect it to work for my forty?"

    It is a fair question, and it has a body of research behind it. A technology that works for a few enthusiasts often fails to spread. Some innovations sweep through a field in a few years and others stall for decades, whatever their merits. A school leader who wants the model of this book to reach beyond one pilot needs to understand why, because a good idea that does not spread changes nothing.

    This chapter begins Part 4 of the book, on the culture and change that a new school day requires. It covers the classic theory of how innovations spread, the five properties that predict whether they will, the five kinds of adopters, the gap where many innovations fail, and the cycle of hype and disappointment that educational technology has repeatedly followed. Chapter 16 then turns to how whole organizations change.

    Good ideas don't spread on their own

    This chapter explains why a pilot that works for six teachers can still stall at forty, and what research says about getting past that point. You'll learn to place any new tool or practice on the adoption curve and to name what is holding it back. Let's find the balance between enthusiasm for a good idea and respect for how slowly trust spreads.

    Innovation and the Diffusion of Innovations¶

    An innovation is an idea, practice, or object that is perceived as new by an individual or a group, whether or not it is new to the world. A school that has never used adaptive software treats it as an innovation even if other schools have used it for years. The "perceived as new" part matters because what drives adoption is how people see the innovation, not how new it objectively is.

    Diffusion of innovations is the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels, over time, among the members of a social system. The sociologist Everett Rogers developed the theory in his book Diffusion of Innovations, first published in 1962 and revised through a fifth edition in 2003. He identified four elements in every case: the innovation itself; the communication channels by which people hear about it; time, including how long it takes individuals to decide and how fast it spreads; and the social system, the community whose norms and relationships shape the decision.

    Rogers built on earlier studies, most famously a 1943 study by Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross of how Iowa farmers adopted hybrid seed corn. The corn was clearly better, producing a higher yield, and yet adoption took about a decade to become nearly universal. Farmers learned from neighbors, not from agricultural bulletins, and they waited to see results in the fields of people they trusted. The same pattern holds in schools: teachers learn about new practices from other teachers, and they wait to see that it works with students like theirs.

    Worked Example: The Four Elements at Lincoln Middle¶

    Element At Lincoln Middle's Level 2 pilot
    Innovation An open interactive math textbook with simulations and self-check quizzes
    Communication channels Ms. Rivera's conversations in the staff room, a demonstration at a staff meeting, a district newsletter article
    Time Eight weeks for six teachers to try it; the rest of the district has not yet decided
    Social system A middle school with a strong collegial culture, inside a district with 400 teachers and many schools

    Notice which element Ms. Okafor controlled least: the social system, the other principal's staff, who trust their own colleagues more than a presentation from another school. The rest of this chapter is about working with that fact.

    The Five Innovation Attributes¶

    Rogers found that five properties of an innovation, as potential adopters perceive them, explain much of the difference in how fast it spreads. The five innovation attributes are relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability. Together they account for roughly half or more of the variation in adoption rates in his review of studies.

    Relative advantage is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as better than the idea or practice it replaces. It can be economic, as in saving money, or social, as in raising status, or practical, as in saving time. For a teacher, "it saves me three hours of grading" is a relative advantage. "It is more innovative" is not.

    Compatibility is the degree to which an innovation is consistent with potential adopters' existing values, past experiences, and needs. A tool that requires a teacher to give up a practice she believes in, or that conflicts with her school's schedule, is incompatible however good it is. Compatibility is why the afternoon of Chapter 14 must fit into the bell schedule of Chapter 3.

    Complexity is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as difficult to understand and use. Complexity works against adoption, so a simple tool spreads faster than a powerful but confusing one. A teacher who must attend a two-day training before opening the software has met high complexity.

    Trialability is the degree to which an innovation can be tried on a limited basis before committing. A free Level 2 textbook that a teacher can try with one class for one unit has high trialability. A district-wide purchase with a three-year contract has low trialability. Observability is the degree to which the results of an innovation are visible to others. If a colleague can see a classroom using the new tool and the students' work, observability is high. If the benefit is invisible, such as a slightly better data record, it is low.

    Attribute Question a teacher asks Pushes adoption when...
    Relative advantage "Is this better than what I do now?" The benefit is large and clear
    Compatibility "Does it fit how I teach and my school's schedule?" It matches existing practice and values
    Complexity "Can I figure it out without a lot of trouble?" It is simple to learn and use
    Trialability "Can I try it without committing?" A small, reversible trial is possible
    Observability "Can I see it working for someone else?" Results are visible to peers

    Adopter Categories and the Adoption Curve¶

    Rogers observed that people differ in how soon they adopt a new idea, and he divided them into five categories based on the time of adoption. The technology adoption curve is the bell-shaped distribution of adopters over time, with the five categories as segments. He set the boundaries by standard deviations from the mean adoption time, which gives the percentages below.

    Category Share of adopters Character
    Innovators 2.5% Venturesome; enjoy risk and novelty; have the resources to absorb failure
    Early adopters 13.5% Respected; look for strategic advantage; often opinion leaders
    Early majority 34% Deliberate; adopt just before the average; follow evidence and peers
    Late majority 34% Skeptical; adopt after the average, often from pressure or necessity
    Laggards 16% Traditional; suspicious of change; adopt last, if at all

    Innovators are the first to try something new, drawn by the novelty itself. They are essential, since they bring the idea in from outside, but they are not always well regarded by their peers, who may see them as too eager. Early adopters adopt early out of a sense of vision and are typically respected figures whose judgment others follow. The early majority are deliberate: they will adopt, but only after seeing that it works for people like them. The late majority adopt after most others have, often skeptically and because the norm has shifted. Laggards are the last, and the word is not a criticism. They often have good reasons, such as limited resources, experience of past failed reforms, or loyalty to practices that work, and Chapter 16 treats their concerns with respect.

    Plot the cumulative share and the bell curve becomes an S-shape. The S-curve of adoption is the cumulative form of the same pattern: slow at first, then rapid through the middle, then slow again as the last holdouts adopt. The cumulative shares at the end of each category are 2.5, 16, 50, 84, and 100 percent. The steepest part of the S is the early majority, and the turning point at 50 percent is where the typical adopter, the average person, adopts.

    Worked Example: A District of 400 Teachers¶

    Apply the percentages to the district's 400 teachers.

    Category Share Teachers Cumulative adopters
    Innovators 2.5% 10 10
    Early adopters 13.5% 54 64
    Early majority 34% 136 200
    Late majority 34% 136 336
    Laggards 16% 64 400

    Ms. Okafor's six teachers are innovators and early adopters. The district has about 64 people in those two groups, and it will not reach a majority until the early majority, 136 more teachers, are persuaded. That group is a different audience from the first 64. What impressed the innovators, a novel tool with big promise, will not impress the early majority, who want proof, simplicity, and colleagues they trust to have done it first.

    Opinion Leaders and Change Agents¶

    Two roles in Rogers' theory explain how an idea moves from one category to the next. An opinion leader is a member of a social system whom others look to for advice and information about innovations. Opinion leaders influence through informal respect, not authority, and they are often people who are close to the norm of the group and well connected, and not necessarily the most innovative. In a school, the teacher whom colleagues ask "have you tried this?" is an opinion leader. A change agent is a person who works, on behalf of an organization, to influence the adoption of an innovation in a desired direction. Instructional coaches, technology integrators, and principals are change agents. The most effective change agents work with and through opinion leaders, since an outsider's recommendation counts for less than a trusted colleague's.

    Worked Example: Choosing a Messenger¶

    Ms. Okafor wants to bring the early majority along. She considers two teachers to demonstrate the Level 2 textbook at the district meeting.

    Candidate Profile How colleagues see them Verdict
    Mr. Park An innovator who tries every new tool and presents at conferences "He'll try anything; it won't work for the rest of us" Poor messenger for the early majority
    Ms. Rivera A respected veteran, cautious, who adopted only after she saw it work and changed her mind publicly "If she says it helps, it helps" An opinion leader; the right messenger

    The tempting choice is the enthusiast. The right choice is the person whose endorsement the early majority will believe, which is a decision about trust and not about technical skill.

    Pick the respected skeptic

    When you need peers to believe a pilot worked, put the most respected cautious colleague on stage, not the biggest enthusiast. A skeptic who changed their mind is worth ten champions.

    The Chasm¶

    The author and consultant Geoffrey Moore, in his 1991 book Crossing the Chasm, argued that Rogers' curve hides a gap. The two groups on either side of the first boundary want different things, and the usual ways of winning the first group do not work on the second.

    The chasm is the gap, between the early adopters and the early majority, that an innovation must cross to become widely used. Early adopters, whom Moore calls visionaries, are willing to accept a new product that is incomplete and risky because they see a chance for a big payoff. Pragmatist buyers make up the early majority. They want a solid, proven product that fits into how they already work, and they take their cue from references, from other pragmatists like themselves. They do not want to be the first, and they will not take a risk for a vision. Because early adopters cannot give pragmatists what they need, and pragmatists will not buy until others like them do, many innovations that thrive with the first 16 percent of adopters stall completely at the gap.

    Crossing the chasm is the work of moving an innovation from the early adopters to the early majority. Two ideas from Moore's strategy matter most for schools.

    A beachhead strategy is the choice to concentrate all effort on winning one narrow, well-defined group first, in the way an army lands on one beach before advancing, instead of spreading effort thinly across everyone. Moore's image is the D-Day landing in Normandy. In a school system, a beachhead might be "seventh-grade math at one or two schools," where the new approach is made to work completely, visibly, and with reference stories, before expanding to every subject.

    A whole product is the complete solution a pragmatist needs, including everything around the core product that makes it work in practice: the training, the integration with existing systems, the support, the instructions, and the answers to the worries about privacy and safety. Pragmatists do not buy a "technology"; they buy something that works on Monday morning. For a school, the whole product of a Level 2 textbook includes login and learning-management-system integration (Chapter 9), accessibility, teacher training, a plan for students without devices, and a family communication plan.

    Worked Example: Lincoln's Chasm¶

    The district has about 64 teachers in the innovator and early-adopter groups, and Ms. Okafor's pilot reached six of them. Here is what the chasm looks like in her situation, and what crossing it means.

    Early adopters (the pilot) Early majority (the other 136 teachers)
    What they want A chance to try something new and make it work Proof it works, ease, and support
    Who they listen to Their own vision and conference talks Colleagues like them, in schools like theirs
    What the pilot offered An unfinished tool and a lot of help from Ms. Okafor Not yet: no training package, no integration, no references
    Beachhead to choose Seventh-grade math in two more schools, with the full package
    Whole product to build Single sign-on, a one-hour training, a parent letter, an accessibility check, a plan for students without home devices

    The pilot was not wasted. It produced the reference stories of Ms. Rivera and a set of lessons about what the whole product must include. But expanding it unchanged to every school would be crossing the chasm by hope.

    The Hype Cycle and Educational Technology¶

    Technologies do not spread in a smooth line. They attract attention in bursts. The hype cycle, a model developed by the research firm Gartner in the mid-1990s, describes the typical pattern of expectations about a new technology over time, in five stages:

    1. Innovation trigger: a breakthrough or product launch sparks interest.
    2. Peak of inflated expectations: publicity builds, success stories are repeated, and expectations exceed what the technology can do. The peak of inflated expectations is the point of maximum hype.
    3. Trough of disillusionment: the technology fails to meet the inflated expectations, interest fades, and early projects are abandoned. The trough of disillusionment is the low point of confidence.
    4. Slope of enlightenment: a smaller group continues experimenting and learns what the technology is actually good for.
    5. Plateau of productivity: the technology is understood, its benefits are realized, and it is adopted for what it does well. The plateau of productivity is steady, useful adoption.

    The hype cycle is a useful description and not a scientific law. Academic reviews have found limited evidence that technologies follow it reliably, and Gartner's placements are judgments. Its value is as a reminder that expectations and reality diverge in a predictable direction early on, and that the disappointment of the trough is not necessarily the end of the technology.

    EdTech hype waves are the repeated cycles of hype and disappointment that educational technology has gone through, each with its own peak and trough. The history of Chapter 3 gave several: film in 1913, teaching machines in the 1950s, classroom computers in the 1980s, interactive whiteboards, and one-to-one tablets, such as the Los Angeles iPad rollout of 2013, which was cancelled after technical problems, weak curriculum, and a scandal over the contract. The most recent clear wave before AI was the massive open online course. The New York Times called 2012 "the year of the MOOC," and universities rushed to join. By 2013, the Udacity founder Sebastian Thrun was quoted saying that the product was "lousy" for many students, and completion rates for the free courses were often under ten percent. MOOCs did not vanish. They settled into a useful niche in professional and continuing education.

    Worked Example: Where Is Generative AI?¶

    The table places events in the story of generative AI in schools on the hype cycle. It is a reading of the evidence, and you should expect reasonable people to disagree about the exact stage.

    Event Stage Why
    ChatGPT released in November 2022 Innovation trigger A breakthrough product sparks wide interest
    2023 predictions that AI tutors would replace teachers; schools ban, then rush to adopt Peak of inflated expectations Expectations well beyond what studies show
    Reports of tutors giving wrong answers, students offloading thinking, a vendor collapsing after a large district purchase Toward the trough Real failures meet the inflated claims
    Randomized studies showing guided tutors help and open access harms Slope of enlightenment Experiments clarify what works and why
    Guided AI built into ordinary tools, with guardrails Plateau of productivity (not yet reached) The settled, useful use

    The important implication for a school: if the hype cycle is a fair picture, the schools that gain most are the ones that neither join the peak nor flee the trough. They learn during the trough, with small pilots, so that they are ready when the slope arrives.

    Disappointment is data, not a verdict

    Notice what the trough of disillusionment really is: the moment when claims meet evidence. Some technologies fall out of use there, and others, like MOOCs, find their real job. A school that watches the trough closely learns what is genuinely useful, and learns it cheaply.

    Adoption Readiness¶

    Adoption readiness is the degree to which a person, school, or district is prepared to adopt a particular innovation. It has two sides. One is the innovation's readiness, as the five attributes measure: how advantageous, compatible, simple, trialable, and observable it appears. The other is the organization's readiness: whether it has the devices, connectivity, time, training, leadership support, and trust that adoption needs, and whether its culture is open to change. A strong innovation in an unready school stalls, and a mediocre one in a ready school can succeed for the wrong reasons.

    A practical readiness check asks six questions of a school before a pilot:

    1. Does the innovation score well on the five attributes for our teachers?
    2. Do teachers have the time and training to try it?
    3. Do students have the devices and connectivity (Chapter 9)?
    4. Do we have the privacy and governance basics in place for its level (Chapter 10)?
    5. Is there an opinion leader willing to try it and be honest about it?
    6. Is the principal ready to protect the pilot from other demands?

    Worked Example: A Barrier Diagnosis¶

    The district's second school, Eastside, tries the same Level 2 textbook, and after a month only two of fifteen teachers are using it. The principal collects the reasons teachers give.

    What teachers say Which attribute is the barrier
    "It doesn't fit our pacing guide, which says what to teach each week." Compatibility
    "I couldn't figure out how to log the students in." Complexity
    "I'm not sure it's better than the book we have." Relative advantage
    "I'd try it, but the district says we must commit for a year." Trialability
    "I don't know anyone who has used it; I've never seen it in a classroom." Observability

    Five teachers, five different attributes. The fix is therefore not a single push, such as more training, but a targeted response to each: align the textbook with the pacing guide, simplify the sign-in, share Maya's results, offer a one-unit trial, and invite colleagues to visit Ms. Rivera's classroom. The attributes make the diagnosis specific.

    Estonia: A System Adopting on Purpose¶

    One real case shows a whole system working with these ideas. The book's AI-First Stories appendix describes Estonia's AI Leap (in Estonian, TI-Hüpe), a national program launched in 2025 that gives secondary students and teachers access to AI learning tools, along with teacher training. It builds on Estonia's earlier success bringing computers and programming into its schools. By the terms of this chapter, the strategy has several of the right features: it began with a defined group (a beachhead of older students and their teachers), it paired the tool with training (part of a whole product), it had the backing of a national government (a change agent), and it built on a record of earlier success (compatibility and credibility). The program is new and its results are not yet known, so it is a useful model of method and not yet proof of effect.

    Answering the Question from the Other Principal¶

    Ms. Okafor's colleague asked why her result for six teachers should work for his forty. The answer from this chapter is that it will not automatically, and that the reasons are knowable. The innovation has to look good on the five attributes to his teachers. The early majority at his school need proof from people like them, so an opinion leader, not an enthusiast, should carry the story. The pilot must be turned into a whole product with training, support, and integration, and the first expansion should be a beachhead, with a few schools and one grade, instead of everyone at once. And the district should expect a trough: some early enthusiasm will fade, and what remains will be what works.

    The story has both sides. The benefit: a clear method for getting a good idea past the first sixteen percent of adopters. The risks: mistaking early adopters' enthusiasm for general readiness, rolling out too widely and too fast, riding the hype and then abandoning a useful tool at the trough, and treating hesitant teachers as the problem. The response: diagnose the attributes, pick a beachhead, build the whole product, choose the right messengers, and treat the trough as a learning period.

    Don't mistake the first 16 percent for everyone

    A common trap is reading a pilot's success as proof of readiness for the whole system, when the pilot was run by people who wanted to try it. Instead, ask what the early majority will need that the pilot did not provide, such as training, integration, references, and a trial that doesn't lock them in, and plan for that before you scale.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • An innovation is anything perceived as new; diffusion of innovations is its spread through channels, over time, in a social system, and it moves through trust in peers more than through announcements.
    • The five innovation attributes, relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability, predict much of the speed of spread and diagnose a stall.
    • The technology adoption curve has innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), the early majority (34%), the late majority (34%), and laggards (16%); the S-curve of adoption is the cumulative form, with the steepest part in the early majority.
    • Opinion leaders persuade by respect, and change agents work through them; pick the respected skeptic as messenger.
    • The chasm lies between early adopters and the early majority; pragmatist buyers want proof and a whole product. Crossing the chasm works through a beachhead strategy and trusted references.
    • The hype cycle, with its peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, and plateau of productivity, describes expectations over time; EdTech hype waves have repeated, from MOOCs to tablets, and the trough is a time to learn.
    • Adoption readiness has an innovation side and an organization side; diagnose a stall by attribute, not by blaming people.
