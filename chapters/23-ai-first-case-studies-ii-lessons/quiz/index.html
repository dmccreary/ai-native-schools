    Quiz: AI-First Case Studies II: Lessons for Practice¶

    Test your understanding of what past failures and successes teach, how to evaluate result claims, the affordable Alpha model, and how to make an honest recommendation to a school board with these review questions.

    1. What is the main lesson the chapter draws from the 2013 Los Angeles iPad rollout?¶

    1. Tablets are too fragile for daily use by students in large districts
    2. A district should buy devices from several vendors rather than one
    3. Buying devices before having a plan for teaching wastes money and public trust
    4. Curriculum from a single publisher is always weaker than open content
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. LAUSD started a $1.3 billion plan to give every student an iPad loaded with Pearson curriculum, and it was cancelled amid technical problems, weak curriculum, and a scandal over the contract award. The lesson is that buying devices before a teaching plan wastes money and trust. The chapter also calls it an edtech hype wave in miniature, announced at the peak and cancelled in the trough. Its matching practices are a readiness assessment, a pilot, and procurement criteria.

    See: The Los Angeles iPad Rollout

    2. Looking across the failures in this chapter, what does the book conclude about why school reforms fail?¶

    1. The technology was not good enough, so schools should wait for better AI
    2. Mostly not technology: pedagogy came second, trust was not built, hype outran evidence
    3. Reforms fail mainly because teacher unions block them during contract negotiations
    4. Reforms fail because school boards approve far too few pilots each year
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter names four recurring causes: technology came before pedagogy (the iPad rollout, AltSchool), the grammar of schooling and culture absorbed the change, trust was not built (inBloom, Summit), and hype outran the evidence. It warns against reading the cases as "the technology wasn't good enough," which suggests waiting for better technology. Mostly the technology worked as designed; planning, trust, procurement, culture, and listening failed, and better AI will not fix those.

    See: Why School Reforms Fail

    3. A vendor's brochure says, "92 percent of teachers report that students love our AI tutor." Which of the chapter's six questions most directly exposes the weakness of this claim?¶

    1. Where did the students start?
    2. Compared with whom?
    3. How big, and for how long?
    4. What was actually measured?
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The claim reports teacher satisfaction, and the chapter's sixth question, "What was actually measured?", exposes that satisfaction or usage is not learning. The other questions also matter but do not target the core problem here: a baseline and a comparison group would be needed to judge any gain, yet this claim does not even measure a gain. Chapter 2's evidence levels place satisfaction surveys in the bin of claims that show no evidence of effect.

    Concept Tested: Evaluating Result Claims

    See: Evaluating Result Claims

    4. A district is about to make a large commitment to a young AI startup with an impressive demo. Which practice from the chapter's failure table would most directly have prevented the LAUSD Ed chatbot loss?¶

    1. Screen-time limits and a people-centered afternoon program for students
    2. Vendor evaluation with a viability knockout rule and staged payments
    3. Informed consent and data minimization for every participating family
    4. Redesigning assessments instead of using AI detectors
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The district committed about $6 million to AllHere and spent about $3 million before the company folded within months of launch, with no check on its financial health. The chapter matches this failure to vendor evaluation, a knockout rule on viability, and staged payments, the rushed procurement and vendor risks of Chapters 10 and 21. Screen limits fit Summit Learning, consent and minimization fit inBloom, and assessment redesign fits the detector study.

    5. Using the chapter's method for the affordable Alpha model, a 12,000-student district estimates $17.24 per student for a Level 4 textbook with selective chatbot use and needs 24 full-time afternoon specialists at $60,000 each. With a print-textbook baseline of $53.33 per student, what is the net added cost per student per year?¶

    1. About $84
    2. About $137
    3. About $120
    4. About $64
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The specialists cost 24 x $60,000 = $1,440,000, or $120 per student for 12,000 students. Adding $17.24 gives a new cost of about $137.24, and subtracting the $53.33 print baseline leaves about $84. The $137 answer forgets the print savings, $120 counts only the specialists, and $64 is the chapter's own figure for 20 specialists. As in the chapter, afternoon staffing is the largest piece of the cost.

    6. A district is choosing between an AI system that replaces its small-group human tutors and one that gives those human tutors live suggestions during sessions. Which case from the chapter is most relevant, and what does it suggest?¶

    1. Khanmigo, which suggests that AI tutors should replace human tutors as soon as possible
    2. AltSchool, which suggests that personalized software works best with no adults present
    3. Tutor CoPilot, which suggests AI works well when it makes human tutors better
    4. Your Brain on ChatGPT, which suggests that all AI assistance harms learning
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. In the 2024 Stanford Tutor CoPilot study, human tutors received live AI suggestions instead of being replaced. Students' mastery improved, the weakest tutors improved the most, and the cost per tutor was small. The chapter's lesson is that AI works well when it makes people better at helping students, which supports the AI-coaching-teachers idea of Chapter 21. Khanmigo is a both-sided case, AltSchool shows the failure of technology without culture, and the MIT study is a small preprint.

    See: Cases That Worked and Cases With Both Sides

    7. A parent cites the "Your Brain on ChatGPT" study as proof that AI permanently damages students' thinking. How should a careful reader assess this claim using the chapter?¶

    1. The study is small and still a preprint, so it flags a risk without proving its size
    2. The study is a large randomized trial, so it proves that the harm is permanent
    3. The study found no effect, so the parent's concern has no basis
    4. The study was independently replicated, so it settles the question
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The 2025 MIT Media Lab study found that students who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed lower brain engagement in EEG readings and remembered less of what they wrote. The chapter notes it was small and is still a preprint, so it supports the risk of handing thinking over without proving its size. Applying the six questions, "has anyone outside checked it?" gets "not yet." The parent's concern is legitimate, but "permanent damage" overstates what the evidence shows.

    Concept Tested: Your Brain on ChatGPT

    See: Evaluating Result Claims

    8. The chapter estimates that the affordable Alpha model adds about $64 per student per year, compared with Alpha's $40,000 to $65,000 tuition. What caution does the chapter attach to this comparison?¶

    1. The $64 figure includes devices, facilities, and transportation, so it is overstated
    2. Alpha's tuition is the same at every campus, so a single comparison is fair
    3. The $64 figure has been confirmed by an independent evaluation of the full package
    4. The tuition pays for a whole school, while $64 is an increment on top of public spending
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter cautions that tuition pays for an entire school, while the $64 is only the increment on top of existing public spending, so the fair comparison is a public school at about $15,664 per student against a private school at $40,000 to $65,000. The figures are illustrative and exclude devices, facilities, and transportation, which reverses the first option. Alpha's tuition varies by campus, and no independent evidence yet shows this package matches Alpha's reported outcomes.

    9. A teacher drafts a two-sided story: a student solves a hard problem with a hint-first tutor, and later the teacher finds in the log that the tutor gave another student the answer on the first request. The story ends there. Using the chapter's structure, what is missing?¶

    1. The benefit, since the story does not show the tutor helping anyone
    2. The response to the risk and the positive being strengthened
    3. The risk, since the log review shows nothing went wrong
    4. Nothing, since a two-sided story only needs one benefit and one risk
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A two-sided story shows a benefit growing stronger and a risk being reduced, with the school's response. The chapter's "Maya and the Hint" has four parts: the benefit (a patient tutor at her own pace), the risk (an answer-giving tutor found in the log), the response (the teacher flags it, the vendor fixes it, the school adds a weekly check), and the strengthened positive (Maya presents her solved problem). This draft has the first two and stops before the response and the strengthened positive.

    10. A principal must recommend a decision to the board. Draft 1 says: "AI is the future. Alpha School's results prove it works. We should adopt it district-wide." Draft 2 asks for a one-year, two-school pilot; separates randomized tutoring evidence from self-reported whole-school claims; attaches the seven-risk checklist; reports gaps by subgroup; gives a net cost; and states a scale, extend, or stop rule. Which draft better meets the chapter's standard, and why?¶

    1. Draft 1, because boards respond best to a confident vision without any details
    2. Draft 1, because Alpha's results are strong enough to skip a pilot
    3. Draft 2, because it states evidence levels, risks, equity, cost, and a set rule
    4. Neither, because a board recommendation should avoid naming any risks
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's school board recommendation has seven parts: the recommendation in one sentence, the case, the evidence with its level, the risks and responses, the equity analysis, the cost, and the decision rule and timeline. Draft 2 includes all of them. Draft 1 treats self-reported results as proof and skips the pilot, the pattern behind several of the chapter's failures. The chapter says the best recommendation asks the board not to believe but to test, and promises to report honestly.
