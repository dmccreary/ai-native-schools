    AI-First Case Studies I: Promise and Peril¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter presents a first set of real cases that show both the promise and the peril of AI-first schools: Alpha School, the Turkish high school chatbot study, the Harvard physics AI tutor, and the risk-and-response framework used to weigh them. Readers finish able to analyze a story about AI in schools to find which benefit and which risk it shows.

    Why Stories¶

    The board has read the evidence, the design, and the plan. At the last meeting, Mr. Alvarez, who asked "doubles what?" in Chapter 4, says what many people feel after a long presentation. "I follow the argument. But I keep thinking about the schools that already tried this. What happened to them?"

    It is the right instinct. Arguments convince the mind, and stories show what an argument looks like when it meets a real school. This chapter and the next turn from theory to cases. They tell the stories of real schools, studies, and products that have tried some version of the model, both where it seemed to work and where it did not, and they use a single framework to read each one.

    The cases come from the book's AI-First Stories appendix, which collects them with their sources. The appendix carries a caution that applies here too: many of these stories are still unfolding, and some figures come from news coverage, preprints, and the schools' own claims. Before any number in this chapter is quoted elsewhere, it should be checked against the original source and dated.

    What happened to the schools that tried it?

    This chapter reads real cases the way the book has taught you to read evidence: with a benefit, a risk, and a response for each. You'll finish able to take any story about AI in schools and say which benefit it shows, which risk, and what the school did about it. Let's find the balance between the promise in the headline and the peril in the footnote.

    How the Book Uses Stories¶

    Storytelling is the use of narrative to convey information, in which events are linked in time and cause around people, goals, and obstacles. People tend to understand and remember information better when it comes in story form, though the strength of the effect varies and popular claims about it are often exaggerated. This book uses stories in two ways, and it keeps them distinct so that readers never mistake one for the other.

    A real case study is an account of an actual school, program, or study, based on reported facts and sources, analyzed to draw lessons (Chapter 2's case study method). Every real case in this book names its source, and says where the figures are uncertain. A composite story is a fictional account assembled from patterns seen in many real situations, with invented characters, used to make an idea concrete. Maya, Jordan, Dev, Ms. Okafor, Ms. Rivera, and Lincoln Middle School are composites. They never claim to be real, and no quotation or outcome attached to them should be taken as a fact about a real person. An illustrated story, which the course description calls for in most chapters, is a story, real or composite, told with images, such as a short graphic-novel scene, to reach readers who learn well visually.

    The distinction matters ethically. A composite story is honest when it is labeled, and dishonest when it is passed off as a real case. A real case is honest when it reports what the source says and no more.

    A Framework for Reading Cases¶

    Every case in this chapter is read with the same five questions, which give the chapter its shape. The questions follow from the book's stated goal, which the course description puts as strengthening the positives and reducing the negatives.

    Benefits of AI-first schools are the gains that the model promises: learning at each student's own pace, and more time for curiosity, the arts, and teamwork. Risks of AI-first schools are the harms it can bring. The course description names seven: screen time, handing thinking over to AI, wrong AI answers, privacy risks, cost and equity gaps, fear of job loss, and unstructured afternoons. Every case is read for which of these it shows.

    The risk-and-response framework pairs each risk a story shows with what the school did, or could do, about it. It is a table with four columns: the story's benefit, the risk it also shows, the response, and the lesson. A lesson from a case is the transferable point that the case teaches about something beyond itself.

    Strengthen the positives is the half of the goal that builds on what works: protecting and expanding the benefits, such as mastery-based pacing and a structured afternoon. Reduce the negatives is the other half: lowering the likelihood or the harm of each risk. Risk mitigation is the set of actions that do the reducing, and it has four general forms: avoid the risk entirely, reduce its likelihood or harm, transfer it (for example, to an insurer or to a contract), or accept it knowingly. A school chooses among them for each risk.

    Risk A typical mitigation Where the book develops it
    Screen time Cap the screens in the morning; break every 25 to 30 minutes; keep the afternoon off-screen Chapter 12
    Handing thinking over to AI Hint-first tutors; assessments that show process Chapters 11, 12, 20
    Wrong AI answers Ground the tutor in vetted content; check with tools; teach verification Chapters 5, 12
    Privacy Minimize data; governance; contracts Chapters 10, 21
    Cost and equity gaps Open Level 2 and 3 content; devices; a free afternoon Chapters 7, 9, 20, 21
    Fear of job loss Honest roles, written commitments, coaching Chapter 19
    Unstructured afternoon The SAFE practices and trained adults Chapter 14

    Case 1: Alpha School¶

    Alpha School is a private school network that began in Austin, Texas, in 2014 and now has campuses in several cities, including Boston, Miami, and New York. Its model is the best-known working example of the morning-and-afternoon design. Students spend about two hours a day on adaptive academic software, a model the school calls 2 Hour Learning, and the rest of the day on life-skills workshops and projects led by adult guides. Two-to-three-hour learning is the broader claim, which this chapter and Chapter 11 examine, that with adaptive software a student can reach a traditional day's academic results in two to three hours.

    Alpha adult guides are the adults who lead the afternoon and support students in the morning. They are not classroom lecturers. They support motivation, effort, and progress, and run workshops and projects. This is the learning guide of Chapter 20, and the design raises the question Chapter 20 flagged: when a student needs a human explanation of a difficult idea, is there an adult who can give one?

    Alpha tuition range is the cost the schools charge: about $40,000 a year at the original Austin campus and about $65,000 a year at newer campuses in Boston, Miami, and New York. Each figure applies to its campuses, and the higher figure should not be given as the price for every campus.

    Framework column Alpha School
    Benefit A structure that frees most of the day for projects, workshops, and people; adaptive pacing in the morning
    Risk also shown Cost and equity: the price is about 2.6 to 4.2 times public per-pupil spending of about $15,600; and the results are reported by the school, with no independent evaluation in this book's sources
    Response The book asks how to offer the same structure to every student, using open Level 2 and Level 3 textbooks, learning graphs, and a structured afternoon in place of costly live AI
    Lesson The structure can work, at private-school prices

    Case 2: Unbound Academy and the Software Is Not the Model¶

    Unbound Academy is a free online charter school for grades 4 to 8 in Arizona that uses Alpha School's 2 Hour Learning software. The Arizona State Board for Charter Schools approved it by a 4–3 vote. According to the appendix, a board slideshow reportedly showed about 10 percent of students proficient in math and 28 percent in English language arts, below state averages, against the school's projection of 90 percent or more within two to three years. The school also claims that students learn 2.8 times faster on its own growth tests. The reports do not say where students started, so it is unclear how much they grew.

    The lesson of the two cases together is the principle this book calls software is not the model: the same software, used without the adults and the in-person afternoon, did not carry over its results. The software is one part of the model, and the adult guides, the projects, and the people are the others. A school that buys the software and omits the afternoon has bought a fraction of what it saw.

    Framework column Unbound Academy
    Benefit Free to families, which addresses the cost barrier; an adaptive morning
    Risk also shown The software without the afternoon: early reported results below state averages, with no baseline to judge growth
    Response Hold claims to the evidence standard of Chapter 2; ask for starting points and independent evaluation
    Lesson The morning software alone is not the whole model

    Case 3: The Turkish High School Study¶

    The Turkish high school study, by Hamsa Bastani and colleagues at the Wharton School in 2024, was a randomized field experiment in a Turkish high school. Students practicing mathematics were given either no AI, open access to a GPT-4 chatbot, or a version with guardrails that gave hints instead of answers. The students with open access did much better on the practice problems. On later exams taken without AI, however, they did about 17 percent worse than students who had never had access, and the guardrailed version removed most of that harm.

    The lesson is design over raw AI power: the same underlying AI helped or harmed depending on its design, so what matters about an AI tutor is whether it gives hints or answers, not how powerful it is. The study is a randomized comparison, which makes it strong evidence of the direction of the effect, though it covers one school and short-term math, and its size should not be applied to every setting.

    Framework column Turkish high school study
    Benefit Better practice performance with AI help
    Risk also shown Handing thinking over: worse later exams without AI
    Response The hint-first version, with guardrails, removed most of the harm
    Lesson Design matters more than power

    Case 4: The Harvard Physics AI Tutor¶

    The Harvard physics AI tutor study, by Gregory Kestin and colleagues at Harvard, published in 2025, was a randomized study in an introductory physics course. Students who learned from a carefully designed AI tutor, built on proven teaching practices instead of being an open chatbot, learned more, in less time, than students in an active-learning class led by an instructor. It is the strongest piece of evidence for the claim that a well-designed AI tutor can make a shorter academic morning work.

    It is also the study with the most limits to remember: one course, at one university, with college students who are unusually well prepared. Whether the result holds for a middle schooler who is behind in fractions is an open question that only independent studies in those settings can answer.

    Framework column Harvard physics AI tutor
    Benefit Learned more in less time
    Risk also shown Generalization: one course, one university; privacy and over-reliance remain to be managed
    Response A tutor designed on proven practices, with guidance and structure
    Lesson A well-designed tutor can help, and design is the condition

    The two studies are one lesson from both ends

    Notice how neatly the Turkish and Harvard studies fit together: one shows the harm of an open chatbot and the other the benefit of a designed tutor. Together they say what Chapter 12 argued, that the question to ask of any AI tutor is how it was built.

    Case 5: Summit Learning¶

    Summit Learning was a personalized-learning platform developed with support from Facebook engineers and rolled out to many schools. In 2018 and 2019 students in Brooklyn walked out, and families in Kansas protested, complaining about long hours on screens and too little contact with teachers. The appendix calls it the clearest real example of the screen-time risk.

    The case shows the risk of a model that is technology-led without a human afternoon: students felt isolated, and families lost trust. It is the reason the book keeps the morning short and the afternoon social. It also shows how quickly local trust can fail when students and families are not part of the design (Chapter 19's student voice and communication).

    Framework column Summit Learning
    Benefit Personalized pacing and a platform that many schools adopted
    Risk also shown Screen time and too little contact with teachers
    Response (In this case, protest rather than design) a short screen-based morning, a people-centered afternoon, and families and students heard early
    Lesson A personalized morning must stay short, and the rest of the day must be with other people

    A Pilot Risk Checklist¶

    A pilot risk checklist is a short list of the main risks that a pilot must be checked against, each with a named response, before the pilot starts. It turns the cases of this chapter into a tool. It has one line for each of the seven risks the course description names.

    Risk What the pilot must have Check
    Screen time A cap on morning screen time and breaks (Chapter 12) A schedule with the cap
    Handing thinking over to AI Hint-first tutoring and assessments that show process (Chapters 12, 20) A test of the tutor's first reply
    Wrong AI answers Grounding in vetted content and checks by tools (Chapter 12) A log review plan
    Privacy Data minimization, governance, and a contract with the checklist terms (Chapters 10, 21) A signed data agreement
    Cost and equity Devices, a free afternoon, and subgroup monitoring (Chapters 20, 21) A gap report by subgroup
    Fear of job loss A written statement of the teacher's role and a coaching plan (Chapter 19) A document shared with staff
    Unstructured afternoon SAFE practices and trained adults (Chapter 14) An afternoon quality rating

    A district shows Ms. Okafor a pilot plan. She reads it against the checklist.

    Risk The plan says Covered?

    1. The learner reads one plan and sees the seven risks.
    2. The learner selects the missing risk and submits it. The sim marks it correct or incorrect and shows the feedback text.
    3. If incorrect, the learner may choose again; the second attempt does not change the score. After the second attempt, the correct risk and reason are shown.
    4. After all 6 plans, the sim shows the score.
    5. The learner should notice that every plan sounds thorough until it is checked line by line.

    Feedback: 6 plans, shuffled once per session, two attempts each. Correct: "Correct: [risk]. [Why text]." Incorrect: "Not quite. Check the plan against each of the seven risks. [Why text for the correct answer, without naming the risk]." After the second attempt the correct risk is revealed and the plan counts as missed. A running score "First-try correct: n of 6" is shown. Final message for a score >= 5: "You can find the gap in a plan." For a score < 5: "Review the checklist and try again; the plans will be reshuffled."

    Starting State: Plan 1 of the shuffled order is shown with the seven risks, the score "First-try correct: 0 of 6," and the prompt "Which risk does this plan fail to address?"

    Chapter Anchors: The chapter's checklist lists seven risks and the worked example finds a plan with no response to wrong answers. The mastery bar is 5 of 6.

    What the Cases Say Together¶

    Read together, the five cases say five things. A structure with an adaptive morning and a people-centered afternoon can work, but at private-school prices (Alpha). The software does not carry the results by itself (Unbound). How the AI is designed decides help or harm (Turkey, Harvard). A technology-led personalized model that crowds out people loses trust fast (Summit). And the way to read any such story is to name its benefit, its risk, its response, and its lesson.

    The evidence behind them is uneven, which is itself a lesson: the strongest studies, the randomized ones, concern tutors, and the evidence for whole-school models is self-reported or early. The honest reading is that the components have support and the package is still being tested.

    The story of this chapter has both sides, like all the stories in this book. The benefit: real cases that make the argument concrete and keep it honest. The risks: reading a case as proof, quoting a figure that has not been checked, and treating a composite as a real person. The response is the framework and the habit: source every real case, label every composite, and pair every benefit with its risk.

    A case is a story, not a result

    A common trap is treating one vivid case, in either direction, as proof. One school's success doesn't show the model works elsewhere, and one failure doesn't show it can't. Ask what kind of evidence the case rests on, what the source is, and what would have to be true for it to apply to your school.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Storytelling helps understanding, and the book separates the real case study (sourced), the composite story (labeled, fictional), and the illustrated story.
    • The risk-and-response framework reads each case as benefit, risk, response, and lesson from a case, in service of strengthening the positives and reducing the negatives through risk mitigation (avoid, reduce, transfer, accept).
    • The benefits of AI-first schools are own-pace learning and time for people; the risks are screen time, handing thinking over, wrong answers, privacy, cost and equity gaps, fear of job loss, and unstructured afternoons.
    • Alpha School runs 2 Hour Learning with Alpha adult guides, at an Alpha tuition range of about $40,000 to $65,000; Unbound Academy uses the same software without the afternoon, which shows that software is not the model.
    • The Turkish high school study (about 17 percent worse on later exams with open access; hints removed most of the harm) and the Harvard physics AI tutor show design over raw AI power.
    • Summit Learning is the clearest screen-time cautionary case.
    • The pilot risk checklist has one line per risk, and two-to-three-hour learning is a plausible and still unproven claim for the whole package.
