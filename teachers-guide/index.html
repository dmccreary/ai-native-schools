    Teacher's Guide¶

    This guide is for anyone who leads a group through AI-Native Schools: Mornings with AI, Afternoons with Each Other. That might be a professor teaching an education course, a district leader running a professional development series, a principal leading a staff book study, or a school board preparing for a decision about AI. It explains how the book is built, how to use each feature with a group, and how to adapt the book for your own setting. No technical background is assumed, and technical terms are defined the first time they appear.

    Who the Book Is Written For¶

    Most intelligent textbooks are written for students. This one is written for the adults who make decisions about schools: K-12 teachers, principals and administrators, school board members, policymakers, curriculum designers, education students, and parents. Your learners are those adults. They do not need a programming or statistics background, and the few charts in the book, such as the doubling curves in Chapter 5, are explained from first principles.

    The book makes one prediction and works out what follows from it. Within three to four years, almost every student may have a free, high-quality, adaptive textbook. If that happens, schools will not need to spend most of the day on direct instruction, and they will have to decide what to do with the freed time. The book's answer is in its subtitle: a shorter academic morning with AI, and a pro-social afternoon of arts, sports, clubs, and projects with other people.

    The Book's Stance: Benefits and Risks Together¶

    Every story in the book about AI in schools shows a benefit, a risk, and how a school can respond to the risk. The goal is to strengthen the positives and reduce the negatives, not to sell AI or to warn people away from it. Keep that balance when you lead discussions. A useful habit is to ask the group three questions about every case:

    1. What went well, and for whom?
    2. What went wrong, or could go wrong?
    3. What would a careful school do about the risk?

    Groups often split into enthusiasts and skeptics early. The three questions give both sides a job.

    How the Book Is Organized¶

    The book has 23 chapters covering 532 concepts. Each chapter builds on earlier ones, so work through them in order unless you are deliberately skipping a part your group already knows.

    Chapters Part Main Question
    1–3 Foundations What are the basic structures of schools and learning, and how do we judge evidence?
    4–5 The evidence for the prediction How fast is AI capability growing, and how should we read the trend?
    6–10 Intelligent textbooks and their infrastructure What are the five levels of intelligent textbooks, what do they cost, and what student data do they need?
    11–12 The shorter academic morning Can students learn the same material in less time, and how do we keep AI from doing their thinking?
    13–14 The pro-social afternoon What fills the rest of the day, and why do students need other people?
    15–19 Changing the culture of a school How do schools adopt new practices, and what small moves make change stick?
    20–21 Putting the model into practice How do you redesign a schedule, buy products safely, and measure a pilot?
    22–23 Case studies What do real AI-first schools and products teach us, both good and bad?

    The AI-First Stories appendix collects the real cases used throughout the book, grouped into stories that worked, stories that went wrong, and stories with both sides. It is a good place to start if you want to pick cases for a single session.

    What Each Chapter Contains¶

    Every chapter follows the same pattern:

    1. Summary. A short overview of what the chapter covers.
    2. Concepts Covered. A numbered list of the concepts from the learning graph that the chapter teaches.
    3. Prerequisites. Links to the earlier chapters the reader should finish first.
    4. An opening story. Most chapters open with a composite story about a teacher, principal, student, or family. A recurring cast appears across chapters. For example, Mr. Dang, a skeptical veteran teacher, asks in Chapter 11 for proof that a shorter morning can work, and the chapter ends by answering him.
    5. Main content with worked examples. The text uses worked examples instead of long explanations: calculations, side-by-side comparisons, and step-by-step cases.
    6. Diagrams and MicroSims. Interactive simulations sit next to the concepts they illustrate (see "Using the MicroSims" below).
    7. Key Takeaways. A bulleted summary of the chapter's most important points, with key terms in bold.

    Each chapter also has two companion pages, listed under the chapter in the left navigation: a Quiz and Annotated References.

    Indigo, the Learning Mascot¶

    The chapters include short callout boxes from Indigo, a barn owl who acts as the book's guide. A character like this is called a pedagogical agent. Indigo welcomes readers at the start of each chapter, points out key insights, offers practical tips, warns about common mistakes, encourages readers through harder material, and celebrates at the end. Indigo's catchphrase, "Let's find the balance," reflects the book's stance on benefits and risks. Indigo appears only a handful of times per chapter, and never in back-to-back boxes.

    With adult groups, Indigo's "warning" boxes work well as discussion prompts, because each one names a mistake that real schools have made.

    Planning Your Course or Series¶

    The book adapts to several formats. These are starting points, not rules.

    Format Suggested Pacing
    Semester course (about 15 weeks) One or two chapters a week. Pair Chapters 1–3 in the first two weeks and give the case studies (22–23) a full week each.
    Professional development series (6–8 sessions) One session per part, using the table above. Assign the chapters as reading before each session and spend the session on MicroSims, cases, and discussion.
    Staff or board book study (4 sessions) Session 1: Chapters 4–6 (the prediction and the five levels). Session 2: Chapters 11–14 (morning and afternoon). Session 3: Chapters 15–19 (change). Session 4: Chapters 20–23 (practice and cases).
    Single workshop (2–3 hours) Chapter 6 (the five levels), two contrasting cases from the AI-First Stories appendix, and the Morning Time Budget MicroSim from Chapter 11.

    Before, During, and After a Session¶

    • Before. Assign the chapter as reading. Ask each participant to bring one claim from the chapter they found surprising and one they doubt.
    • During. Project one MicroSim and have the group predict what will happen before you change a control. Then run one case through the three questions above.
    • After. Use the chapter quiz as a quick self-check, and ask participants to apply one idea to their own school, such as placing a product they use on the five-level scale or sketching one microstrategy from Chapter 19.

    A Capstone Idea¶

    Chapter 23 closes with how to evaluate result claims and make a recommendation to a school board. A strong final assignment asks each participant, or each small group, to write a one-page recommendation for their own school or district. The recommendation should name one benefit to strengthen, one risk to reduce, the safeguard that addresses the risk, and how the school would measure whether a pilot worked.

    Using the MicroSims¶

    What Is a MicroSim?¶

    A MicroSim (short for micro-simulation) is a small interactive program that runs in a web browser. Readers do not install anything; MicroSims work on any device with a modern browser such as Chrome, Firefox, Safari, or Edge. Each one lets the reader change something, using sliders, buttons, drop-down menus, or sorting cards, and see the result right away.

    The book contains 66 MicroSims. They fall into two broad kinds:

    Kind What It Does Examples
    Explorers and calculators Readers change inputs and watch a model respond Doubling Time Projection Calculator, Morning Time Budget, AI Cost per Student Calculator, Coach Capacity Calculator
    Judgment practice Readers sort or classify short scenarios and get feedback on each choice Product Level Classifier, "Which Kotter Step Is This?", Contract Clause Review, Find the Missing Safeguard

    They are built with a few different tools. Most use p5.js, a library for interactive graphics. A few charts use Chart.js, and the concept maps, including the Learning Graph Viewer, use vis-network, a library for drawing networks of connected nodes. You do not need to know any of these to use the sims.

    How MicroSims Appear in the Chapters¶

    MicroSims are placed inside the chapter text using an iframe, a web feature that shows one web page inside another. They load automatically when the chapter page opens. Below each one is a collapsible specification section, labeled with the sim's name. Click it to see the learning objective, the Bloom's Taxonomy level the sim targets, and how its controls work. These specifications are useful for lesson planning because they state what the sim is meant to teach.

    The full list is on the MicroSims page.

    Tips for Using MicroSims with a Group¶

    1. Ask for predictions first. Before moving a slider, ask the group what they expect. The surprise when a prediction fails is where the learning happens, especially with the exponential-growth sims in Chapter 5.
    2. Use the judgment sims as discussion tools. Have small groups agree on an answer before submitting. The disagreements are often more useful than the score.
    3. Connect the sim to a real decision. After the AI Cost per Student Calculator, ask what the numbers would be for your own district.
    4. Reset and try again. Every sim can be reset. Encourage people to try an extreme setting to find where a model breaks.
    5. Plan for connectivity. MicroSims need an internet connection unless you build the site on your own computer (see "Customizing Your Own Version" below).

    Embedding a MicroSim in Your Own Page¶

    You can place any MicroSim on a page you control, such as a course site in Canvas or Schoology, a Google Site, or a blog. Paste one line of HTML:

    <iframe src="https://dmccreary.github.io/ai-native-schools/sims/morning-time-budget/main.html"
    width="100%" height="680px" scrolling="no"></iframe>

    Replace morning-time-budget with the folder name of any MicroSim. You can find the folder name in the sim's web address. If the bottom of the sim is cut off, increase the height value.

    Using the Quizzes¶

    Each chapter has a quiz page with about ten multiple-choice questions, 230 questions in all. Each question opens to reveal the correct answer, an explanation of why the other choices are wrong, the concept it tests, and a link back to the section of the chapter that covers it.

    The quizzes are self-checks, not graded tests. Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. Ways to use them:

    • Exit check. Have participants take the quiz at the end of a session to see which ideas need another pass.
    • Pre-reading check. Assign the quiz before the chapter to find out what the group already believes. Several questions target common misconceptions, such as how large the effect of tutoring really is.
    • Pair discussion. Have pairs argue for an answer before revealing it.
    • A question bank. Use the questions to build your own assessments. They are openly licensed (see "Understanding the License" below).

    Bloom's Taxonomy¶

    The quiz questions and MicroSims are written at different levels of Bloom's Taxonomy, a framework that sorts thinking tasks from simple to complex. Chapter 1 teaches it in detail.

    Level What the Reader Does Example Verbs
    Remember Recall facts and definitions Define, list, name
    Understand Explain ideas in their own words Explain, describe, summarize
    Apply Use an idea in a new situation Calculate, classify, use
    Analyze Break a situation into parts and relationships Compare, differentiate, diagnose
    Evaluate Make a judgment against criteria Assess, critique, justify
    Create Produce something new Design, plan, propose

    The quizzes lean toward Understand, Apply, and Analyze. The judgment MicroSims and the capstone recommendation reach Evaluate and Create.

    Using the Glossary and FAQ¶

    The Glossary defines 532 terms, one for each concept in the learning graph. The definitions are short and precise. Use it for vocabulary previews before a chapter, or ask participants to rewrite a definition for a specific audience, such as parents or a school board.

    The FAQ answers 105 questions that readers commonly ask, from what a learning graph is to how the five-level model handles privacy. It works well as a set of discussion openers: pick two or three questions, have the group answer them, then compare with the book's answer.

    Both pages can be searched with your browser's find command (Ctrl+F on Windows, Cmd+F on Mac), and the search bar at the top of every page searches the whole book.

    Using the References¶

    Each chapter's Annotated References page lists about ten sources for further reading. Each entry gives the title, the source, and a sentence or two on why it matters to the chapter. The lists mix Wikipedia articles, which are easy to reach and have stable web addresses, with books by authors such as Benjamin Bloom and Salman Khan and with research papers such as the Harvard physics AI tutor study.

    The references are a good source of assigned readings for graduate courses. Ask participants to read one primary study and compare it with how the chapter summarizes it.

    When a Link Breaks¶

    Link rot is when a web link stops working because the page moved or was deleted. If you find a broken link:

    1. Search for the title on the source's website.
    2. Look it up on the Wayback Machine, which keeps archived copies of web pages.
    3. Report it as a GitHub Issue (see "Sending Feedback" below).

    The Learning Graph¶

    A learning graph is a map of every concept in the book and which concepts each one depends on. It is a directed acyclic graph (DAG): each concept is a dot (called a node), and arrows show which concepts must come first. "Acyclic" means the arrows never loop back, so there is always a sensible order to learn things in. Chapter 9 explains learning graphs in depth, because they are also one of the building blocks of the adaptive textbooks the book describes.

    You can explore this book's graph in the Learning Graph Viewer. Ways teachers use it:

    • Checking prerequisites. Before a session, look up a concept and see what the group needs to know first.
    • Diagnosing confusion. If a participant struggles with an idea, trace its arrows back to find the missing piece.
    • Mapping your own curriculum. Compare the graph with an existing course outline to find gaps or overlaps.
    • Teaching the idea itself. Showing the graph is the fastest way to explain how an adaptive textbook could choose a student's next step.

    The Learning Graph section of the navigation also includes the full concept list, how the concepts are grouped into categories, and quality reports.

    This Book as an Example of Its Own Subject¶

    The book is itself a level 2 (interactive) intelligent textbook, using the five-level model from Chapter 6. It has MicroSims, a learning graph, quizzes, a glossary, and an FAQ, but it does not track individual readers or adapt to them, and it has no chatbot. That makes it a useful exhibit: participants can experience level 2 first-hand, then discuss what a level 3 version would need. The answer, covered in Chapters 8 and 10, is a record of each reader's progress, which brings privacy law and data governance into play.

    Sending Feedback¶

    The book is an open-source project hosted on GitHub, a website where people develop software and documents together. You do not need to know programming to report a problem.

    A GitHub Issue is like a support ticket: a way to report an error, suggest an improvement, or ask a question. To open one:

    1. Go to the book's repository.
    2. Click the Issues tab, then the green New issue button.
    3. Give the issue a clear title, such as "Broken link in Chapter 7 references."
    4. Describe which page has the problem and what you expected to see.
    5. Click Submit new issue.

    You need a free GitHub account to open an issue. If you would rather not create one, use the Contact page.

    Feedback that is especially welcome includes factual errors, broken links, MicroSims that do not load or behave strangely, accessibility problems, and new real-world cases, especially ones that show both a benefit and a risk.

    Customizing Your Own Version¶

    Because the book is open, you can make your own version, for example with cases from your own state or district. These terms come up along the way:

    • Repository (repo). A folder on GitHub that holds all of a project's files.
    • Fork. A copy of a repository in your own GitHub account.
    • Clone. A copy of a repository on your own computer.
    • Git. A tool that tracks every change to a set of files.
    • Markdown. A simple text format. **bold** makes bold, # Heading makes a heading, and a line starting with - makes a bullet point.
    • MkDocs. The program that turns the Markdown files into this website.
    • mkdocs.yml. The settings file that controls the site's title, colors, and navigation menu.

    Step 1: Copy the Book¶

    The easier way is to fork it: sign in to GitHub, go to the book's repository, and click Fork in the upper right.

    If you want to work on your own computer instead, install Git and clone it from a terminal:

    git clone https://github.com/dmccreary/ai-native-schools.git

    Step 2: Make Changes¶

    All of the content is in the docs/ folder as Markdown files, which you can edit with any text editor. To change the title and description, edit these lines in mkdocs.yml:

    site_name: 'Your Version of the Title'
site_description: 'Your description'
site_author: 'Your Name'

    To change the colors, edit the palette section. This book uses indigo and orange:

    theme:
  palette:
    primary: 'indigo'
    accent: 'orange'

    Other available colors include red, pink, purple, blue, teal, green, amber, brown, and grey. The logo in the upper-left corner is set by the logo: line under theme:.

    To add or remove a page, add or remove its line in the nav: section of mkdocs.yml. A page that is not listed there will not appear in the menu.

    Step 3: Preview Your Version¶

    1. Install Python 3.8 or newer from python.org.

    2. Install MkDocs and its Material theme:

      pip install mkdocs mkdocs-material

    3. In the project folder, start the preview server:

      cd ai-native-schools
mkdocs serve

    4. Open http://127.0.0.1:8000/ai-native-schools/ in your browser. The page refreshes each time you save a file.

    Step 4: Publish It¶

    To publish your version as a free website on GitHub Pages, run:

    mkdocs gh-deploy

    Your site will appear at https://YOUR-USERNAME.github.io/ai-native-schools/ within a few minutes.

    Analytics and Student Data¶

    Web analytics measures how visitors use a website, such as which pages they open and for how long. For a course, it can show which chapters participants read and which they skip.

    Google Analytics¶

    Google Analytics is a free service that counts visits to a website. It is not turned on for this book yet. If you publish your own version and want it:

    1. Sign in at analytics.google.com and create a property, Google's name for a tracked website.
    2. Copy the Measurement ID, which looks like G-XXXXXXXXXX.

    3. Add it to the extra: section of mkdocs.yml:

      extra:
  analytics:
    provider: google
    property: G-XXXXXXXXXX

    4. Publish the site again. Data starts to appear within a day or two.

    Google Analytics reports totals across all visitors, not the progress of individual people.

    xAPI and Learning Record Stores¶

    xAPI (Experience API) is a standard for recording specific learning actions, such as "Maria moved the doubling-time slider" or "Maria answered quiz question 3 correctly." The records are stored in a Learning Record Store (LRS), a database built for learning data. Chapter 8 explains both in detail. This book's MicroSims do not send xAPI records today.

    Recording what individual learners do moves a textbook toward level 3, and that is the book's privacy inflection point. Before you collect records about identifiable students, check these laws, all covered in Chapter 10:

    • FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act). U.S. law protecting student education records.
    • COPPA (Children's Online Privacy Protection Act). U.S. law with extra protections for children under 13.
    • GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation). European Union law that applies to learners in the EU.
    • State student-privacy laws, which many U.S. states have added on top of federal law.

    If you work with adult participants in a course, your institution's own data policies still apply. If you plan to use any of these tools with K-12 students, talk with your district's data privacy officer first. The inBloom and PowerSchool stories in Chapter 10 show what happens when schools skip that step.
