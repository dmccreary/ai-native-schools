    AI-Native Schools¶

    Title: AI-Native Schools: Mornings with AI, Afternoons with Each Other

    Target Audience: K-12 teachers, school principals and administrators, school board members, education policymakers, curriculum and instructional designers, education students, and parents who want to understand how AI will reshape the school day

    Prerequisites: None. Readers should be comfortable using a web browser. Experience with an AI chat assistant is helpful but not required. No programming or statistics background is assumed; the few charts and growth curves in the book are explained from first principles.

    Course Overview¶

    This book makes a prediction and then works out what follows from it. The prediction is that within three to four years almost every student will have access to a free, high-quality, interactive textbook that adapts to their needs. If that happens, schools will no longer need to spend most of the day on direct instruction, and they will have to decide what to do with the time that frees up. The book's answer is in its subtitle: mornings with AI, afternoons with each other.

    The book is honest that this change can go well or badly. Real AI-first schools and products have produced both strong results and expensive failures. The book's goal is to strengthen the positives and reduce the negatives, and it uses stories to do this. Each part draws on real cases, collected in the AI-First Stories appendix, and on composite stories of students, teachers, and families. Every story about a risk is paired with what a school can do about it.

    The book has five parts.

    Part 1: The Evidence for the Prediction¶

    METR's task-horizon studies measure the length of task, in human working time, that an AI system can complete correctly. That horizon has been doubling about every five months. Readers learn how the measurement works, how to read a doubling curve on a logarithmic scale, and what steady exponential growth means over a three-to-four-year planning window. They also learn what could slow the trend, and why schools should plan for it anyway.

    This growth is driving the intelligent textbook movement, which began in earnest in 2025. At the core of the book is the five-level model of intelligent textbooks, from McCreary's paper A Five-Level Classification Framework for Intelligent Textbooks: Lessons from Autonomous Vehicle Standards (EdArXiv preprint, December 2025). The model is based on the SAE J3016 levels for self-driving cars, which gave that industry a shared vocabulary, graduated regulation, and realistic expectations while the technology changed quickly. The five levels are:

    1. Static textbooks: fixed content with no interactivity, like traditional print and PDF textbooks
    2. Interactive textbooks: MicroSims, learning graphs, embedded simulations, and recommended learning paths
    3. Adaptive textbooks: content driven by each student's goals and progress, which requires storing student records
    4. Chatbot textbooks: conversational tutoring through chatbots and large language models
    5. Autonomous AI textbooks: real-time content generation and fully personalized instruction

    Readers learn to place any textbook or AI product at its level and to use the levels as a shared vocabulary for buying decisions and policy. The levels also explain the economics. As AI improves, level 2 interactive content becomes cheap to produce, heading toward pennies per student per day, and is often free. Level 2 textbooks are being published in growing numbers, and level 3, 4, and 5 textbooks are on the horizon. Readers learn how the OpenBook QR Score measures the quality and reusability of open textbooks, and why those scores are rising quickly.

    Level 3 marks a privacy inflection point. Below it, a textbook needs almost no student data. At level 3 and above, it needs detailed learning histories, which brings in FERPA, COPPA, and GDPR and calls for stronger governance and checks for algorithmic bias. The inBloom shutdown and the PowerSchool data breach show what happens when this is done badly. Readers learn how xAPI and Learning Record Stores (LRS) make personalization possible while students and families keep control of their own data.

    Part 2: The Shorter Academic Morning¶

    If students can reach today's learning results in two to three hours a day with interactive intelligent textbooks, the traditional eight hours of sitting through direct instruction is no longer needed. In the morning, students follow their own curiosity through adaptive textbooks that pace the material, check mastery, and fill gaps.

    The evidence for this is promising but mixed, and the book presents both sides. A carefully designed AI tutor helped Harvard physics students learn more in less time, and a low-cost AI tutoring pilot in Nigeria produced large gains. But a study in Turkish high schools found that students with open access to an AI chatbot did worse on later exams than students who never used it, while a version that gave hints instead of answers removed most of the harm. The lesson is that how the AI is designed matters more than how powerful it is.

    Alpha School is the best-known working example of the morning-and-afternoon model. Its students spend about two hours a day on adaptive academic software and the rest of the day on life-skills workshops and projects led by adult guides. Tuition ranges from about $40,000 a year at the original Austin campus to about $65,000 a year at newer campuses in Boston, Miami, and New York, far beyond what public schools spend per student. Unbound Academy, a free online charter school in Arizona that uses the same software without in-person afternoons, reportedly scored below state averages in its early results. Together, the two stories show that the software alone is not the model.

    The book's answer to the cost problem is a low-cost, energy-efficient, adaptive design built from:

    • Intelligent textbooks at level 2 and level 3, written once with AI and then served to every student as ordinary web pages at almost no cost
    • MicroSims, small interactive simulations that run in the browser with no server or AI model behind them
    • Learning graphs, which map each concept and its prerequisites so the textbook can choose a student's next step
    • xAPI and a Learning Record Store, which record what each student has done and mastered, so the textbook can adapt without a live AI model

    AI does its expensive work once, when the content is written, instead of every time a student asks a question. Most students never send a request to a large language model, which keeps cost and energy use low and avoids the risk of students handing their thinking over to a chatbot. Chatbot tutoring (level 4) is used only where it adds real value, with guardrails that give hints instead of answers.

    Part 3: The Pro-Social Afternoon¶

    The central question of the book is: what will fill the rest of the day? The answer is pro-social, hands-on activity: music, dance, theater, the visual arts, sports, coding and robotics clubs, maker spaces, and project-based learning. These activities draw on social constructionism, the idea that students learn the most when they build things and solve problems together. The afternoon focuses on creativity, collaboration, communication, and empathy.

    AI can teach critical thinking and computational thinking well. Students still need to practice using those skills with other people, on teams, toward shared goals, and that is what the afternoon is for. The afternoon does not have to be invented from scratch. High Tech High's project-based learning, Finland's phenomenon-based learning, and El Sistema's youth orchestras already show it working. The book also warns about the opposite failure: afternoons that drift into unstructured free time.

    Part 4: Changing the Culture of a School¶

    A new schedule on paper changes nothing if teachers, students, families, and school boards keep working the way they always have. Schools are known for absorbing reforms without changing much; Tyack and Cuban call the stable structure underneath this the "grammar of schooling." AltSchool, Summit Learning, and the Los Angeles iPad rollout all show technology-first reforms failing to change school culture. The book looks at change on two scales.

    • The whole organization. Readers learn technology adoption models: Rogers' diffusion of innovations and its adopter categories (innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards), Moore's chasm between early adopters and the early majority, and the hype cycle. They then study organizational change models: Lewin's unfreeze-change-refreeze, Kotter's eight steps for leading change, the ADKAR model, Bridges' transition model, Schein's levels of organizational culture, Senge's learning organization and systems thinking, and Fullan's research on educational change. The Concerns-Based Adoption Model (CBAM) is covered in depth because it was built to track how teachers adopt new practices. Estonia's national AI Leap program shows a whole system adopting AI on purpose.
    • Microstrategies. These are small, concrete moves that leaders and teachers can make next week: recruiting teacher champions, running a single pilot classroom, getting visible early results, finding and copying "bright spots," shrinking the first step, peer observation, and professional learning communities. Readers also learn how to answer specific concerns, respond to resistance with empathy, and talk with families before rumors spread.

    Readers learn to fit the strategy to where each group sits on the adoption curve and to each teacher's stage of concern, so that small moves add up to lasting change. The changing role of the teacher, from lecturer to coach and mentor, runs through this part. Tutor CoPilot, where AI coached human tutors instead of replacing them, shows how AI can make teachers better rather than make them unnecessary.

    Part 5: Putting the Model into Practice¶

    The final part is practical. Readers learn how to redesign a school schedule and staffing model, buy AI products safely, plan and run a pilot, protect student privacy, keep access equitable, assess learning, and measure whether the new model is working. The Los Angeles "Ed" chatbot, where a district spent millions on a startup that collapsed within months, shows why schools need procurement rules before they buy. AI cheating detectors, which wrongly flagged non-native English writers, show why assessment should be redesigned instead of policed.

    Main Topics Covered¶

    The Evidence for the Prediction¶

    • The METR task-horizon studies: how the 50% task-completion time horizon is measured
    • Exponential growth and doubling times: reading log-scale charts and projecting a five-month doubling over three to four years
    • Limits and uncertainty in AI forecasts
    • The intelligent textbook movement since 2025
    • The five-level model of intelligent textbooks (the core framework of the book)
    • The SAE J3016 autonomous-vehicle levels as a model for classifying textbooks
    • Level 1, static textbooks: fixed content, no interactivity
    • Level 2, interactive textbooks: MicroSims, learning graphs, simulations, and path recommendations
    • Level 3, adaptive textbooks: goals, progress tracking, and student records
    • Level 4, chatbot textbooks: conversational tutoring with large language models
    • Level 5, autonomous AI textbooks: real-time content generation and full personalization
    • Precision in level terminology: avoiding vague "AI-powered" marketing claims
    • The economics of content creation: falling production costs and the penny-per-day vision
    • The commoditization of level 1 and level 2 content and what it means for publishers and schools
    • Open educational resources (OER) and the falling cost of high-quality textbooks
    • The OpenBook QR Score: measuring textbook quality and reusability
    • The level 3 privacy inflection point: why student records change everything
    • FERPA, COPPA, and GDPR obligations at level 3 and above
    • Algorithmic bias auditing and governance for adaptive systems
    • The trust paradox: personalization requires data, and data requires trust
    • xAPI and Learning Record Stores for student-controlled, portable learning data

    The Shorter Academic Morning¶

    • The history of direct instruction and the industrial-era school day
    • Adaptive learning, mastery learning, and Bloom's two-sigma problem
    • Curiosity-driven learning and student agency
    • The compressed academic morning: reaching today's results in two to three hours
    • AI tutors: what they do well and where they fail
    • Cognitive offloading: when students hand their thinking over to AI
    • Guardrails: AI that gives hints instead of answers
    • Teaching critical thinking and computational thinking with AI
    • The Alpha School model: two hours of adaptive academics and afternoons of life-skills workshops
    • The cost problem: Alpha tuition of about $40,000 to $65,000 per student per year compared with public school spending
    • Unbound Academy: what happens when the software is used without in-person afternoons
    • Learning graphs, MicroSims, and other building blocks of interactive textbooks
    • Adapting with learning graphs instead of live AI: choosing the next concept from prerequisites and mastery data
    • Low-cost AI-first schools: write content once, serve it to every student at almost no cost
    • Energy use of AI in education: why most students do not need direct access to a large language model
    • Comparing the cost per student of level 2, level 3, and level 4 textbooks

    The Pro-Social Afternoon¶

    • Social constructionism, constructivism, and learning by building together
    • Pro-social learning: collaboration, communication, and teamwork
    • Applying critical and computational thinking on teams
    • Empathy and social-emotional learning (SEL)
    • Creativity and the arts: music, dance, theater, and visual art
    • Clubs and extracurriculars: coding, robotics, maker spaces, and sports
    • Project-based learning and team problem solving
    • Proven afternoon models: High Tech High, phenomenon-based learning, and El Sistema
    • Structure in the afternoon: avoiding drift into unstructured free time

    Changing the Culture of a School¶

    • School culture: shared beliefs, norms, rituals, and the "grammar of schooling"
    • Why school reforms fail: lessons from AltSchool, Summit Learning, and the Los Angeles iPad rollout
    • Rogers' diffusion of innovations and the technology adoption curve
    • Adopter categories: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards
    • The five innovation attributes that predict adoption: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability
    • Moore's chasm: moving from early adopters to the early majority
    • The hype cycle and managing expectations
    • Lewin's unfreeze-change-refreeze model
    • Kotter's eight steps for leading change
    • The ADKAR model: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, and reinforcement
    • Bridges' transition model: endings, the neutral zone, and new beginnings
    • Schein's three levels of organizational culture: artifacts, espoused values, and basic assumptions
    • Senge's learning organization and systems thinking in schools
    • Fullan's research on educational change and coherence
    • The Concerns-Based Adoption Model (CBAM): stages of concern and levels of use
    • National adoption: Estonia's AI Leap
    • Resistance to change: sources, legitimate concerns, and responding with empathy
    • Microstrategies for change: teacher champions, pilot classrooms, early results, and smaller first steps
    • Bright spots and positive deviance: finding what already works and spreading it
    • Professional learning communities, peer observation, and coaching
    • The changing role of the teacher: from lecturer to coach, mentor, and facilitator
    • AI that coaches teachers: lessons from Tutor CoPilot
    • Stakeholder engagement: teachers, unions, students, families, and school boards
    • Communicating change to families and the community

    Putting the Model into Practice¶

    • Redesigning the school schedule and staffing model
    • Safe procurement of AI products: lessons from the Los Angeles "Ed" chatbot
    • Using the five levels for procurement criteria and level-specific policy
    • Student data privacy and the responsible use of AI with minors
    • Data breaches and vendor risk: lessons from inBloom and PowerSchool
    • Equity and access: devices, connectivity, cost, and closing gaps rather than widening them
    • Assessment in an AI-native school: mastery data from the morning, portfolios and performances from the afternoon
    • AI cheating detectors and why assessment should be redesigned instead of policed
    • Judging evidence: separating independent test results from self-reported growth claims
    • Change management: piloting, measuring, and scaling
    • Making change last: embedding new practices in policy, schedules, and hiring

    Stories Across the Book¶

    • Storytelling as a method: using real cases and composite stories to explore change
    • AI-first case studies: stories that worked, stories that went wrong, and stories with both sides
    • The positives of AI-first schools: learning at each student's pace, more time for curiosity, the arts, and teamwork
    • The negatives of AI-first schools: screen time, handing thinking over to AI, wrong AI answers, privacy risks, cost and equity gaps, fear of job loss, and unstructured afternoons
    • Strengthening the positives and reducing the negatives: a risk-and-response framework

    Topics Not Covered¶

    • How to build an intelligent textbook step by step (covered in the companion book Designing and Building Intelligent Textbooks)
    • How to score an open textbook by hand (covered in OER Evaluator)
    • The technical internals of large language models and how they are trained
    • Programming or software development
    • Detailed lesson plans for individual subjects
    • School finance and budgeting beyond the staffing and scheduling changes the model requires
    • Higher education and corporate training

    Learning Outcomes¶

    These learning outcomes are organized using the 2001 revision of Bloom's Taxonomy: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create.

    After this course, readers will be able to:

    Remember¶

    • Define task horizon, doubling time, direct instruction, intelligent textbook, adaptive learning, cognitive offloading, and social constructionism
    • State the approximate doubling time reported by the METR task-horizon studies
    • List the five levels of intelligent textbooks and the defining feature of each
    • Recall the SAE J3016 standard that the five-level model is based on
    • Identify level 3 as the privacy inflection point
    • Recall the parts of the OpenBook QR Score and what Q and R stand for
    • Recall the Alpha School tuition range and how its school day is divided
    • Name the main categories of pro-social afternoon activities
    • Name the five adopter categories on the technology adoption curve
    • List the steps of Kotter's change model and the five elements of ADKAR
    • Recall the stages of concern in the Concerns-Based Adoption Model
    • Name the key case studies in the AI-First Stories appendix and the main lesson of each
    • List the main benefits and risks of moving to an AI-first school

    Understand¶

    • Explain how METR measures the length of task an AI system can complete
    • Explain why a five-month doubling time produces large changes over three to four years
    • Explain why the autonomous-vehicle levels are a useful model for classifying textbooks
    • Describe how a textbook adapts to a student's needs at each of the five levels
    • Explain why level 3 and higher textbooks need student records and stronger privacy protection
    • Explain how falling AI costs commoditize level 1 and level 2 content
    • Explain how intelligent textbooks, MicroSims, learning graphs, xAPI, and an LRS can adapt to students without live AI calls
    • Explain why writing content once costs far less, and uses far less energy, than calling a large language model for every student
    • Summarize the case that two to three hours of adaptive learning can match today's results, including the evidence against it
    • Explain why AI that gives hints helps learning while AI that gives answers can hurt it
    • Explain social constructionism and why students learn well when solving problems together
    • Describe how the teacher's role changes in an AI-native school
    • Explain why schools often absorb reforms without changing their underlying culture
    • Explain the chasm between early adopters and the early majority
    • Describe the difference between whole-organization change models and microstrategies, and why a school needs both

    Apply¶

    • Project an AI capability trend forward using a doubling time
    • Classify an existing textbook or AI learning product by its level in the five-level model
    • Use the OpenBook QR Score to compare two open textbooks
    • Write procurement criteria that state the required textbook level and its privacy requirements
    • Estimate the cost per student of an AI-first school built on open level 2 and level 3 textbooks
    • Draft a daily schedule that pairs a morning adaptive-learning block with afternoon pro-social activities
    • Match afternoon activities to the collaboration, creativity, and empathy skills they build
    • Place staff, student, and family groups on the technology adoption curve
    • Choose a microstrategy suited to a teacher's stage of concern
    • Apply Kotter's eight steps or ADKAR to a planned change at a specific school
    • Match each risk of the AI-first model to a specific way to reduce it
    • Use the lessons of a past failure, such as the Los Angeles "Ed" chatbot or Summit Learning, to write a risk checklist for a pilot

    Analyze¶

    • Distinguish the skills AI can teach well (critical and computational thinking) from the skills that need practice with other people (teamwork, communication, empathy)
    • Compare the traditional school day with the AI-native school day in terms of time, cost, staffing, and outcomes
    • Compare Alpha School with Unbound Academy to separate the effect of the software from the effect of in-person guides and afternoons
    • Compare the cost structure of Alpha School with a low-cost model built on open textbooks
    • Identify the assumptions behind the book's prediction and what would happen if each one failed
    • Examine how the AI-native model could widen or narrow equity gaps
    • Analyze a school's culture using Schein's three levels
    • Diagnose why a past technology rollout stalled, using a case such as AltSchool or the Los Angeles iPad program
    • Separate legitimate concerns from general resistance to change
    • Analyze a story about AI in schools to find which benefits and which risks it shows

    Evaluate¶

    • Judge the strength of the evidence behind AI capability forecasts
    • Evaluate claims of results from AI-first schools, separating independent test results from self-reported growth
    • Critique an AI tutor or intelligent textbook for quality, guardrails, privacy, and fit with student needs
    • Evaluate a vendor's claimed textbook level against the five-level definitions
    • Assess whether a school's data governance is ready for level 3 and higher textbooks
    • Assess whether a school is ready to pilot a shorter academic morning
    • Evaluate afternoon programs on whether they build real collaboration and empathy skills
    • Judge which change model fits a given school's size, culture, and history
    • Evaluate whether a pilot has crossed the chasm and is ready to scale
    • Judge whether a proposed AI-first program builds on its strengths and deals with its risks
    • Justify a recommendation to a school board for or against adopting the model

    Create¶

    • Design an AI-native school day for a specific grade level and community
    • Design a low-cost technology plan that uses learning graphs, MicroSims, and an LRS instead of live AI for most students
    • Create an afternoon program built around team projects that apply critical and computational thinking
    • Develop a pilot plan with goals, a timeline, staffing, family communication, and success measures
    • Construct an assessment plan that combines morning mastery data with afternoon portfolios and performances
    • Construct a culture-change plan that combines a whole-organization change model with specific microstrategies
    • Write a story about a student, teacher, or family in an AI-native school that shows a benefit, a risk, and how the school responds

    Sample Capstone Projects¶

    1. AI-Native School Day Design

      • Description: Design a full weekly schedule for a real or imagined school that uses a morning adaptive-learning block and afternoon pro-social activities
      • Skills Demonstrated: Scheduling, staffing, matching activities to skills
      • Deliverables: A weekly schedule, a staffing plan, and a one-page rationale

    2. Pilot Program Proposal

      • Description: Write a proposal to a school board for a one-semester pilot of the AI-native model
      • Skills Demonstrated: Using evidence, assessing risk, planning for privacy and equity
      • Deliverables: A written proposal with goals, timeline, budget impact, success measures, and a risk checklist drawn from the AI-First Stories

    3. Forecast Briefing

      • Description: Prepare a short briefing that explains the METR task-horizon trend and what it means for schools over the next four years
      • Skills Demonstrated: Reading exponential trends, communicating uncertainty
      • Deliverables: A slide deck or one-page briefing with a projection chart

    4. Afternoon Club Design

      • Description: Design a club or team project that requires students to apply critical and computational thinking together
      • Skills Demonstrated: Project-based learning design, collaboration, empathy goals
      • Deliverables: A club charter, a sample project, and a rubric for teamwork

    5. Five-Level Product Audit

      • Description: Audit three AI learning products or textbooks a school is considering, assign each a level in the five-level model, and check their privacy practices and guardrails against that level
      • Skills Demonstrated: Classification, vendor evaluation, privacy governance
      • Deliverables: A comparison table and a recommendation memo

    6. School Culture Change Plan

      • Description: Write a two-year plan for moving a real or imagined school to the AI-native model
      • Skills Demonstrated: Assessing culture, mapping the adoption curve, combining a change model with microstrategies, engaging stakeholders
      • Deliverables: A culture assessment, an adoption-curve map of stakeholders, a staged change plan, and a family communication plan

    7. Affordable Alpha

      • Description: Redesign the Alpha School model so a public school could afford it, using open intelligent textbooks, MicroSims, learning graphs, xAPI, and an LRS, while keeping the in-person afternoon that Unbound Academy lacked
      • Skills Demonstrated: Cost modeling, technology design, comparing energy use
      • Deliverables: A cost-per-student comparison, a technology diagram, and a list of trade-offs

    8. Two-Sided Story

      • Description: Write a short story about one day in an AI-native school that shows one benefit growing stronger and one risk being reduced
      • Skills Demonstrated: Storytelling, risk analysis, empathy
      • Deliverables: A 1,500-word story with a one-page commentary linking it to the book's risk-and-response framework
