    Poster Ideas¶

    Ideas for full-page overlay infographic posters.

    Each poster is an infographic overlay MicroSim: a text-free background image with an interactive layer of labels on top, built with the shared engines in docs/sims/shared-libs/. There are two engines:

    • Callout (diagram.js) draws numbered markers with leader lines. Use it for one complex scene where small parts need names.
    • Grid (grid-diagram.js) draws large rectangular hover zones. Use it for side-by-side comparisons and posters split into regions.

    The first poster, Crossing the Chasm (Chapter 15), is built and is the model for the twelve below. Its background is a hand-built SVG drawn by a Python script, so no text-to-image step is needed. Most posters below can be drawn the same way. A few (marked) would look better painted from an image-prompt.md.

    Rules that apply to every poster

    • No text in the image, not even the title. Every word lives in data.json.
    • Exception: grid posters may print column headers in the image, with showLabels: false.
    • Every poster shows a benefit, a risk, and the school's response to the risk (the book's story-balance rule).
    • Any number baked into a zone's facts goes through Phases 1–4 of the verified-infographic guide first, and carries a source_id. Numbers taken from the book's own worked examples cite the chapter instead.
    • Each poster starts at status: scaffold, is embedded in its chapter under a #### Diagram: heading, and is added to nav: and docs/sims/index.md.

    1. The Factory Day vs. the AI-Native Day¶

    Chapters: 3 (Industrial-Era School Model), 20 (Designing the AI-Native School Day) Engine: Grid, two columns, each split into a morning band and an afternoon band (four zones plus two header zones) Why a poster: the whole argument of the book is a comparison between two school days. Seeing them side by side, hour by hour, makes it concrete.

    Image: two vertical school-day timelines drawn as buildings in cross-section. - Left, the factory day: identical classrooms stacked like a production line, rows of desks facing the front, a bell on the wall, students grouped by age, and a clock that dominates each room. - Right, the AI-native day: - Morning: small clusters of students at laptops, each at a different point on a winding path, with a coach moving between them. - Afternoon: open spaces with project tables, a garden, a stage and a workshop.

    Zones

    Zone Summary Facts to show
    Factory morning Everyone hears the same lesson at the same pace Bell schedule; age-based grade levels; seat time; Carnegie unit
    Factory afternoon More of the same, by subject Teacher workload and burnout; little time for projects
    AI-native morning A compressed academic morning, about two to three hours, at each student's own pace Personalized pacing; mastery learning; gap filling; reduced direct instruction
    AI-native afternoon Freed-up time for people skills Projects, clubs, service, the pro-social afternoon (Ch 14)
    Teacher, left Lecturer and grader —
    Teacher, right Coach, mentor, facilitator Changing teacher role (Ch 20)

    Story balance: - Benefit: freed-up time. - Risk: too much screen time and isolated students. - Response: morning screen-time limits, and a structured (not empty) afternoon.

    To verify: the length of the compressed morning. Use the book's phrasing and cite Chapter 11.

    2. Five Levels: Textbooks and Self-Driving Cars¶

    Chapter: 6 (The Five-Level Model of Intelligent Textbooks) Engine: Grid, five columns in two rows (cars on top, textbooks below), so one zone per level per row Why a poster: the chapter's analogy to the SAE driving levels is the key to the model, and it lands best when the two ladders sit side by side.

    Image: five columns that rise like a staircase from left to right. - Top row: a car at each level, from a car with a driver's hands on the wheel to a car with no steering wheel at all. - Bottom row: a textbook at each level: - a closed print book - a laptop with a slider and a graph - a laptop with a branching path - a laptop with a chat bubble - a laptop generating pages on the fly - Small column headers "1"–"5" may be printed, with showLabels: false.

    Zones (textbook row)

    Level Summary Facts
    1 Static Fixed content, like print and PDF No interactivity; nothing known about the student
    2 Interactive Responds to what the learner does Embedded simulations, quizzes, learning graphs
    3 Adaptive Driven by each student's progress Needs stored student records (Ch 8, 10)
    4 Chatbot Conversational tutoring Large language models; inference cost (Ch 7)
    5 Autonomous Real-time content generation Full personalization; not yet realistic

    Story balance: - Benefit: each level adds power. - Risk: each level also adds cost, data and things that can go wrong. Vendors claim a higher level than they deliver. - Response: the chapter's rule that a product's level is the highest capability it delivers in use.

    To verify: the SAE level names (cite SAE J3016) and the date of the five-level paper (December 2025 preprint).

    3. Seat Time vs. Mastery¶

    Chapters: 3 (Seat Time, Carnegie Unit), 11 (Mastery Learning) Engine: Grid, two columns Why a poster: the difference is about time, and a picture of two students' paths through the same unit makes the hidden gaps visible.

    Image: two staircases of the same five concepts (blocks). - Left, seat time: a calendar marches forward and the student climbs on schedule, but some blocks are cracked or missing. The higher blocks wobble on the gaps. - Right, mastery: the student stays on a block until it is solid (a small green check shape, not text), then climbs. Every block is whole, and some students are higher than others.

    Zones

    Zone Summary Facts
    Seat time: the calendar Time is fixed; learning varies Carnegie unit; the class moves on together
    Seat time: the cracked blocks Gaps build up silently Later concepts depend on earlier ones (learning dependency, Ch 8)
    Mastery: the solid blocks Learning is fixed; time varies Mastery threshold; gap filling
    Mastery: the spread of students Students finish at different times Personalized pacing; what the class does together instead

    Story balance: - Benefit: no hidden gaps. - Risk: students spread far apart, and slow students fall further behind in time. - Response: tutoring and gap filling for those who are stuck, and shared afternoon work that does not depend on pace.

    To verify: none; this poster is conceptual.

    4. Anatomy of a Learning Record¶

    Chapter: 8 (Tracking Mastery: xAPI and the Learning Record Store) Engine: Callout, top-bottom layout, about 10 callouts Why a poster: the path from a click to a dashboard is invisible to most teachers and parents. A single picture of it answers the chapter's opening question, "How does it know?"

    Image: a left-to-right flow: 1. A student at a laptop moving a slider in a MicroSim. 2. A glowing envelope leaves the laptop. The envelope is drawn as three linked tiles, shown only as a person icon, an action icon and an object icon. 3. It travels to a filing-cabinet server (the Learning Record Store). 4. The cabinet feeds a teacher's dashboard with bars. 5. The cabinet also feeds a learning graph whose next node lights up. 6. A small key on a lanyard hangs from the student, standing for student-controlled data.

    Callouts: the MicroSim; the xAPI statement; actor; verb; object; result (score); the Learning Record Store; the dashboard (learning analytics); the learning graph choosing the next concept; the key (data portability and student control).

    Story balance: - Benefit: the textbook adapts without a live AI model, cheaply. - Risk: a detailed learning history is sensitive data. - Response: data minimization and student-controlled records (links to poster 5).

    To verify: the xAPI statement structure (cite the xAPI specification).

    5. The Student Record Vault¶

    Chapter: 10 (Student Privacy and Data Governance) Engine: Callout, side-panel layout, about 12 callouts Why a poster: "who may see what" is a set of nested rules that is hard to follow in prose and easy to follow as rings.

    Image: - Centre: a student's record as a glowing folder inside a round vault. - Rings: concentric walls, each with a gate. - Innermost ring: the student and the family. - Second ring: the teacher and the school. - Third ring: the district. - Outer ring: the vendors and researchers. - Around the rings: - a crack in the outer wall with papers blowing out (a data breach) - a shredder by one gate (the retention policy) - a small balance scale on a desk (bias auditing)

    Callouts: student data vs. personally identifiable data; behavioral data; FERPA (the school's gate); COPPA (the under-13 gate); GDPR (where it applies); data minimization (a small vault); informed consent; the retention-policy shredder; vendor risk; the breach crack; algorithmic bias auditing; the trust paradox.

    Story balance: - Benefit: detailed records let a textbook adapt. - Risk: the privacy inflection point, and the inBloom and PowerSchool cases. - Response: governance readiness before adoption.

    To verify: the scope of each law, the inBloom and PowerSchool facts, and any numbers about breaches. Run the verified-infographic Phases 1–4.

    6. Answer Machine vs. Thinking Tutor¶

    Chapter: 12 (AI Guardrails: Teaching Thinking Without Over-Reliance) Engine: Grid, two columns of three rows (the question, the AI's move, what the student is left with) Why a poster: the same homework question handled two ways shows exactly what a guardrail changes.

    Image: two chat conversations on two phones. Speech bubbles hold only shapes and icons, no words. - Left phone, open AI: one big bubble with a finished answer (a checkmark and a neat page). The student leans back, and a small, empty thought cloud floats above them. - Right phone, guided AI: a chain of short bubbles, with a question-mark icon, a lightbulb hint and a smaller question mark. The student is writing on paper, and the thought cloud above them holds four small icons: puzzle pieces (decomposition), a repeating pattern (pattern recognition), a simplified shape (abstraction) and numbered steps (algorithmic thinking).

    Zones

    Zone Summary Facts
    Open AI: the move Gives the answer Fast and convenient
    Open AI: the result Cognitive offloading Over-reliance; confidently wrong answers go unchecked
    Guided AI: the move Hints instead of answers Socratic questioning; the tutor holds back
    Guided AI: the result The student does the thinking The four computational-thinking skills
    The guardrails What makes the right phone different Mastery threshold; selective chatbot use; screen-time limits

    Story balance: - Benefit: an AI tutor is patient and always available. - Risk: over-reliance. - Response: guardrails, and the guided-AI design.

    To verify: any study figures quoted about tutoring outcomes (run the verified phases, or keep the poster qualitative).

    7. The EdTech Hype Cycle¶

    Chapter: 15 (Technology Adoption, the hype-cycle section) Engine: Callout, top-bottom layout, about 11 callouts Why a poster: a century of education technology on one curve is the fastest way to show that the pattern repeats.

    Image: a landscape whose skyline is the hype-cycle curve. - The five phases: - a rocket launch at the trigger - a crowded, fireworks-lit peak - a foggy valley at the trough - a gentle staircase up the slope - a calm, productive plateau with a working classroom - Objects placed on the curve, one per technology wave: - an old radio - a classroom TV on a cart - a computer-lab desktop - a laptop cart - a MOOC certificate - a glowing chat bubble for generative AI

    Callouts: the five phases (5), plus one per technology wave (6). The wave placements must match the chapter's own hype-cycle table.

    Story balance: - Benefit: some technologies do reach the plateau. - Risk: buying at the peak, and abandoning at the trough. - Response: realistic expectations, and staying on the slope of enlightenment, where a small group keeps learning what works.

    To verify: the dates and claims for each wave. Run Phases 1–4; Gartner is the source for the curve itself.

    8. The Rider, the Elephant and the Path¶

    Chapter: 19 (Microstrategies for Leading Change, the Switch framework) Engine: Callout, side-panel layout, 10 callouts Why a poster: the Heaths' framework is already an image. Drawing it, with each of the nine moves placed in the scene, turns a list into something memorable.

    Image: - The rider: a small figure holding a map and pointing ahead. A bright patch of the landscape glows in the distance (a bright spot), and a far-off hilltop shows a school building at the destination. - The elephant: a large elephant, partly tired. A heart shape floats near it, a small step-sized stone sits right in front of its foot (shrink the change), and a team badge hangs on its harness (grow your people). - The path: - a smooth, fenced path with a well-worn groove (build habits) - a signpost with no words (tweak the environment) - a herd of elephants walking ahead (rally the herd)

    Callouts: direct the rider (follow the bright spots, script the critical moves, point to the destination); motivate the elephant (find the feeling, shrink the change, grow your people); shape the path (tweak the environment, build habits, rally the herd); plus one overall callout for the three-part idea.

    Story balance: - Benefit: each move is small and cheap. - Risk: a clear plan (rider) with no motivation (elephant) stalls. - Response: address all three, and use the chapter's examples, such as "a login that works first time."

    To verify: none beyond attribution to Heath and Heath (2010), Switch.

    9. Kotter's Eight Steps as a Mountain Trail¶

    Chapter: 17 (Leading Change: Vision, Culture, and Early-Stage Concerns) Engine: Callout, dual-panel layout, 8 step callouts plus 2 risk callouts Why a poster: Kotter's steps are a sequence, and a trail up a mountain shows that skipping one leaves you stranded.

    Image: a mountain with a switchback trail and eight camps. 1. A camp with a fire alarm-style flare (urgency) 2. A rope team (the guiding coalition) 3. A telescope pointed at the summit (vision) 4. A signal flag station (communicating) 5. Fallen logs being cleared (removing obstacles) 6. A small flag planted on a ledge (a short-term win) 7. A wider camp with more tents (building on change) 8. A stone cairn at the summit (anchoring in culture)

    Two hazards complete the scene: a climber sliding back down a shortcut gully, and a summit cairn that is only half built.

    Callouts: the eight steps, using the book's names (sense of urgency, guiding coalition, change vision, communicating the vision, removing obstacles, short-term wins, building on change, anchoring in culture). The two risk callouts are: - Skipping steps: the shortcut gully. - Declaring victory too soon: the half-built cairn.

    Story balance: - Benefit: a clear order of work. - Risk: skipping steps, or declaring victory too soon. - Response: Lewin's refreeze and Kotter's anchoring, as the chapter pairs them.

    To verify: step names (cite Kotter, Leading Change).

    10. Schein's Culture Iceberg¶

    Chapters: 17 (Schein's culture model), 18 (culture assessment) Engine: Callout, side-panel layout, about 9 callouts Why a poster: Schein's three layers are about what you can and cannot see, and an iceberg shows that directly. It also explains why reforms that change only the surface fail.

    Image: an iceberg in cross-section, at sea. - Above the water (artifacts): a school building with a bell tower, a printed schedule shape, a trophy case and a crowd at a pep rally (a ritual). - At the waterline (espoused values): a framed mission-statement shape with blank lines, and a banner with no text. - Deep below (basic assumptions): dark, heavy ice with fossil-like shapes, such as a single desk alone in a room ("a good teacher works alone") and a clock fused into the ice ("learning takes a fixed time"). - The reform ship: a small boat labelled with nothing, steering at the visible tip while the hidden mass sits beneath its path.

    Callouts: artifacts; school rituals; espoused values; shared beliefs and norms; basic assumptions (two examples); the grammar of schooling; the reform ship ("reform without real change"); culture assessment (a diver with a lamp).

    Story balance: - Benefit: culture holds a school together through hard times. - Risk: when stated values and real assumptions disagree, the assumptions win. - Response: assess culture before announcing change, and work on assumptions through small wins.

    To verify: none beyond attribution to Schein.

    11. What a Textbook Costs per Student¶

    Chapter: 7 (Content Economics and Open Educational Resources) Engine: Grid, four columns (Print, Level 2, Level 3, Level 4) Why a poster: a finance director's question ("What will it cost per student, all in?") has a picture answer: stacked cost blocks, one stack per option. Image: best painted from an image prompt, or built as an SVG with exact block heights.

    Image: four stacks of coin-like blocks on a table, with block height proportional to cost. - Print: one tall stack. - Level 2: a tiny stack. - Level 3: a slightly taller stack, with blocks in different colors for records, governance and training. - Level 4: taller again, with an added block glowing like a chat bubble (inference).

    A laptop sits behind all four stacks, slightly faded, to show the device cost that is not included.

    Zones

    Option Per student per year (devices already owned) Facts
    Print baseline about $53.33 $80 book, six years, four core subjects
    Level 2 $5.80 Penny-per-day content and hosting ($1.80) plus $4.00 of training
    Level 3 $12.80 Adds a learning record store ($3.00), privacy governance ($2.00) and $2.00 more training
    Level 4 $23.00 Adds inference ($7.20 at $2 per million tokens), log review ($1.00) and $2.00 more training
    The device (faded) about $75 a year $300 laptop over four years

    Story balance: - Benefit: content costs collapse. - Risk: hidden costs (devices, training, governance), plus publisher disruption. - Response: total cost of ownership and explicit cost models.

    To verify: all figures come from the chapter's illustrative model. Cite Chapter 7 and say "illustrative" in every zone. No web verification is needed unless real prices are added.

    12. Promise and Peril: Five AI-First Cases¶

    Chapter: 22 (AI-First Case Studies I: Promise and Peril) Engine: Grid, five columns (one per case) by three rows (benefit, risk, response), 15 zones Why a poster: the chapter's risk-and-response framework is a table. A poster that fills it in for all five cases shows the pattern at a glance, and it is the book's story-balance rule in one picture. Image: best painted.

    Image: five vertical panels, each showing a school scene in a consistent flat style: 1. A small private school with guides beside students at laptops (Alpha School) 2. A home screen with a single student and a virtual classroom (Unbound Academy) 3. A Turkish high-school math classroom with two groups, one with open AI and one with a guided tutor (the Turkish study) 4. A university physics lecture hall with students working with an AI tutor (Harvard) 5. A large public-school classroom with students on a learning platform and a mentor checking in (Summit Learning)

    In each panel, a green band at the top holds a symbol for its benefit, a red band in the middle a symbol for its risk, and a blue band at the bottom a symbol for its response.

    Zones: one per case per row, so 15 in all. Each zone's facts include its source.

    Story balance: built into the design. Every case gets all three rows, and a missing response is shown as an empty blue band, not invented.

    To verify: everything. Run the verified-infographic Phases 1–4 for every claim: - Alpha School's tuition: about $40,000 at the original Austin campus to about $65,000 at newer campuses. Never give $65,000 for every campus. - The study results. - The Unbound charter decisions. - The Summit Learning history.

    Allow asymmetric content where a case has no published evidence for a row.

    Suggested build order¶

    1. Posters 4, 8, 9 and 10: they can be drawn as SVGs from simple shapes, need no outside facts, and each fills a gap in a chapter with no picture of its main model.
    2. Posters 1, 3 and 6: two-column grid comparisons, quick to build.
    3. Posters 2 and 11: need care with exact figures and level names, but no web research.
    4. Posters 5, 7 and 12: need the verified-infographic research phases first.
